Although the algorithm in Theorem 1.1 does not fully resolve Problem 2, we believe it provides significant progress on that question. Notice, that Theorem 1.1 also does not resolve Problem 1, however, more precise running time analysis of Theorem 1.1 shows that it improves the O_<sup>⋆</sup>(M_<sup>1/3</sup>) time algorithm [JWZ25] when \(\delta>19\) (see Remark 1 for an exact runtime). Later in Theorem 1.3 we show that techniques behind Theorem 1.1 can be used to improve state-of-the-art for \(\delta\ge 1\) in the average-case regime.

One could ask whether the \(\mathcal{O}(1/\mathrm{log}\delta)\) term in the exponent can be significantly improved; for example, can we get 2<sup>O(_n_<sup>0.99</sup>)</sup> time, or at least 2<sup>O(_n/_log<sup>100</sup>_n_)</sup> time? We show that this is highly unlikely.

**Theorem 1.2.** _Under Hypothesis 1, there is no_ 2<sup>_o(n/_log_n_)</sup> _time algorithm that solves Pigeonhole Equal Subset Sum. Moreover, there is no_ \(\mathrm{poly}(n)\cdot M^{o(1/\mathrm{log}n)}\) _time algorithm for PESS_ (_even for_ \(\delta=n^{1/2+o(1)}\)).

This lower bound implies that the running time in Theorem 1.1 cannot be improved to \(M^{o(1/\mathrm{log}\delta)}\), and that our algorithm is optimal for polynomial \(\delta\) up to constants in the exponent. We also show a lower bound that excludes a 2<sup>_o(n/_log_n_)</sup> time algorithm for PESS, which conditionally completes our answer to Problem 2 (up to a single log_n_ factor in the exponent). See Figure 2 for an overview of our results.

Theorem 1.2 is conditioned on the hardness of lattice problems. Shortest Vector Problem is a fundamental hardness assumption. For a tunable parameter \(\kappa\ge 1\) and dimension _n_, one can obtain an \(n^\kappa\)-approximation in \(2^{\mathcal{O}(n/\kappa)}\) time [Sch87] and after a substantial effort over the past 40 years (see, e.g., [Sch87, SE94, GN08, ALNSD20]), no 2<sup>_o(n_)</sup> algorithm is known that would yield polynomial approximation for SVP. Significant improvements over these trade-offs would break modern cryptosystems and have been already assumed by Vafa–Vaikuntanathan [VV25] to establish hardness of _symmetric binary perceptron, number partitioning problem_ and more. Here, we consider a stronger, fine-grained version of their assumption <sup>3</sup>:

**Hypothesis 1.** _For any constant_ \(c\ge 1\)_, there exists_ \(\varepsilon>0\) _such that there is no_ \(\mathcal{O}(2^{\varepsilon n})\) _time algorithm that given an n-dimensional lattice_ L _with high probability returns a non-zero vector_ \(\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{L}\) _such that_ \(\|\mathbf{v}\|_2\le\mathcal{O}(n^c)\cdot\lambda_n(\mathcal{L})\).

We show that lack of 2<sup>_o(n_)</sup>-time polynomial approximation for this problem is a major obstacle to improve our \(M^{\mathcal{O}(1/\mathrm{log}\delta)}\)-time algorithm for PESS. We stress that Hypothesis 1 is not a standard complexity assumption, and to the best of our knowledge, we are the first ones to use it in that form. Regardless of whether one believes in Hypothesis 1 or not, our message is as follows:

_To improve the running time of Theorem 1.1, one would need first to make a substantial breakthrough for lattice problems._

Finally, we focus on the case \(M\le 2^n\). <sup>4</sup> The average-case setting is an important relaxation in which the algorithm is required to work correctly on most inputs rather than on every input. This perspective is relevant for both practical and theoretical reasons. For example, average-case algorithms for Subset Sum have been widely studied and play an important role in cryptography, since such instances were long considered as hardness assumptions [MH78, Sha84]. Understanding the average-case complexity of Subset Sum, therefore, matters for both practice and theory [LO85b, HGJ10, BCJ11, LO85a, CJL<sup>+</sup>92, DDKS12]. Motivated by this, we present an O_<sup>⋆</sup>(M_<sup>1/4</sup>)-time algorithm for equal Subset Sum in the average-case setting when \(M\le 2^n\).

<sup>3</sup>Formally, Hypothesis 1 concerns an important variant of the problem called _n_-th Successive Minima Problem. <sup>4</sup>The algorithm in the average case works even for density less than 1, when \(M=\Theta(n^\kappa\cdot 2^n)\) for some \(\kappa>0\); the only caveat is that the success probability decreases with increasing \(\kappa\).
