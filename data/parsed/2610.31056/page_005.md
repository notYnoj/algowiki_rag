<tbc>Vasilakis \[Vas22\] refined this idea to obtain an \(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(nM)\)-time algorithm. If the subsets are inspected in a suitable random order, the birthday paradox implies that a collision typically appears after about \(\mathrm{poly}(n)\cdot M^{1/2}\) steps, recovering the approach of Allcock et al. \[AHJ<sup>+</sup>22\]. Jin and Wu \[JW24\], and later Jin, Williams, and Zhang \[JWZ25\], further improve this line of attack by combining careful preprocessing with structural observations about subset-sum collisions to reach \(\mathrm{poly}(n)\cdot M^{1/3}\) running time.

**Ingredient 2: Differencing Algorithm.** Next, we reformulate the classical differencing technique of Karmarkar-Karp \[KK82\]. Although originally designed for partitioning in the average-case setting, the differencing strategy can be adapted to the Equal Subset Sum setting even in the worst-case setting. It gives a polynomial-time algorithm when integers are bounded by _M_=2<sup>_c_log<sup>2</sup>_n_</sup> for a small constant _c\>_0. We reinterpret this procedure as follows:

Let 0_\<a_<sub>1</sub>_\<...\<a<sub>n</sub>_ be the sorted input numbers and, for simplicity, assume that _n_ is a power of 2. For every \(i\in[n/2]\), define \(\Delta_i:=a_{2i}-a_{2i-1}\), the difference between the two consecutive numbers in the _i_-th pair (note that \(\Delta_i\ge 0\)). We call \(i\in[n/2]\) _good_ if \(\Delta_i\le 4M/n\), and _bad_ otherwise.

Observe that there are at least _n/_4 good pairs. Indeed, if there were more than _n/_4 bad indices, then \(\sum_{i\in\mathrm{bad}}\Delta_i>|\mathrm{bad}|\cdot\frac{4M}{n}>M\), which contradicts the fact that \(\sum_{i\in[n/2]}\Delta_i<a_n\le M\).

Now observe the following: if we discard bad indices, we obtain at least _n/_4 numbers whose values decreased by a factor _n/_4. If we repeat this for \(\kappa=\Theta(\mathrm{log}n)\) rounds, we get a number whose value is bounded by \(\le M\cdot(\frac{4}{n})^\kappa<1\) for sufficiently small constant \(c\in(0,1/4]\). <sup>5</sup> Therefore, eventually, we must observe integer zero, because throughout the procedure, the numbers are nonnegative integers.

The key combinatorial observation is that the sign chosen in the last \(\Delta_i\) determines to which subset the corresponding element _a<sub>i</sub>_ belongs in the final solution. Hence, an integer zero naturally corresponds to two different subsets of the same sum.

**Combining the ingredients.** Karmarkar-Karp differencing approach already has several applications in cryptology. Importantly for us, Wagner \[Wag02\] extended it to solve _the k-list problem_ in the average-case setting (see also an independent work of \[BKW03\]). Similarly to these works, we also combine dynamic programming with the Karmarkar-Karp differencing approach. Somehow surprisingly, in comparison to these approaches, our algorithm is deterministic and works in the worst case.

Let _k_ be a largest integer such that \(M\le k^{n/(4k)}\). For simplicity, let us assume that _k_ is a power of 2 and _k_ divides _n_ and let _W_:=2<sup>_n/_(2_k_)</sup> (in Section 4 we give tighter bounds). We start by partitioning the input into _k_ equal blocks and precompute the list of all subset sums inside each block to obtain sets \(\mathcal{A}_i^{(0)}\) for each \(i\in[k]\). Rather than matching high-order bits (as in the differencing approach), we repeatedly merge blocks and keep large sumsets that share the same low-order bits, i.e., we work modulo _W<sup>j</sup>_ in level _j_.

More precisely, at each level _j_=1,2_,...,_log_k_ we maintain sets \(\mathcal{A}_i^{(j)}\) for every \(i\in[k/2^j]\). The sets \(\mathcal{A}_i^{(j)}\) arise by _merging_ two sets \(\mathcal{A}_{2i-1}^{(j-1)}\) and \(\mathcal{A}_{2i}^{(j-1)}\) from the previous levels. Merging is performed by taking the sumset \(\mathcal{A}_{2i-1}^{(j-1)}+\mathcal{A}_{2i}^{(j-1)}\).<sup>6</sup> Notice that the size of that set increases; therefore, we need to _prune_ it. We achieve it by partitioning the sumset into _W_ sets of integers based on their value modulo _W<sup>j</sup>_ and keeping a representative subset of size exactly _W_ from the largest set. This pruning keeps the lists to have size exactly _W_ while maintaining a large ensemble of sums whose lowest bits agree (see Algorithm 1).
