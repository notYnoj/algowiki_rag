# Computing All-Pairs Shortest Communication Time Paths for Periodically Varying Satellite Networks

Koushik Sinha _Senior Member, IEEE_, Dibakar Saha, Sai Puppala and Bhabani P. Sinha _Fellow, IEEE_

**Abstract--**We address the problem of designing an efficient algorithm for computing the all-pairs shortest-time paths in time-varying networks, specifically targeting the dynamic topological characteristics of low earth orbit (LEO) satellite constellations. We model such temporal graphs as _G_=(_V,E<sub>t</sub>_), where _V_={1,2_,...,n_} is the set of _n_ vertices in the graph, and _E<sub>t</sub>_ is the set of _M_ time-varying edges considered over a total time window of size \(T=M\tau,\tau\) being the duration of each interval in that time window. For such a network, our algorithm finds the all-pair shortest paths in _O(M_<sup>2</sup>_n_<sup>3</sup>)-time. A key advantage of our approach is its computational structure, which mirrors two matrix multiplication steps, first in the forward direction and then in the backward direction. This structure of the algorithm makes it exceptionally suitable for parallelization on SIMD architectures like GPUs, thereby offering a significant speedup over traditional shortest-path algorithms and enabling efficient, network-wide message routing in rapidly changing LEO satellite constellations.

**Index Terms--**Temporal graphs, All-pairs shortest paths, Shortest distance path, Shortest time path, Satellite networks, LEO satellite constellations.

### 1 INTRODUCTION

W e are witnessing an era of unprecedented expansion in satellite launches, with ambitious plans for multi- orbit, mega-constellations of satellites in the coming decade, involving thousands of interconnected satellites spanning low earth orbit (LEO), medium earth orbit (MEO), and geostationary orbit (GEO). The future of connectivity is quite literally ascending into orbit, with LEO satellite meg- constellations projected to play a pivotal role in that transformation as inter-satellite links (ISLs) become increasingly central to constellation-wide routing and capacity management [1], [2]. With a significantly lower cost to build and launch compared to MEO and GEO satellites, LEO satellites are an extremely attractive proposition for future networks, especially in the context of 6G non-terrestrial networks (NTNs), as their shorter distances from the surface of the Earth translate to lower latency and higher link bandwidth [1]. As such, the concept of mega-constellations of LEO satellites for networking has gained significant traction in recent times, with major operators and programs advancing large-scale deployments and regulatory authorizations [3], [4], [5], [6].

The potential of such space-based networks extends far beyond just providing networking services -- the ability to host in-orbit computing, thereby transitioning satellites into an _integrated communication and computational fabric_

for offloading computation from terrestrial data centers [7]. In May 2025, China reported the launch of an initial set of satellites toward a space computing constellation for collaborative AI-oriented workloads [8]. Related plans have also been discussed publicly for India and Europe in the context of space-based computing and satellite-network capability roadmaps [9], [10]. Similarly, commercial providers have announced space and satellite cloud/edge offerings and supporting ground-to-space service infrastructure [11], [12], [13]. Considering the vast computational resources circling in orbit across the earth, such an in-orbit distributed or edge computing platform can host a myriad of applications, including disaster response, situational awareness for space traffic management, and onboard AI-based filtering and prioritization for Earth-observation data [7].

A key challenge in harnessing LEO satellite constellations for networking, or in-orbit computing, arises from the inherent temporal dynamics of LEO satellite networks. High-speed orbital motion induces time-varying ISL characteristics that lead to dynamic topology changes as satellites traverse deterministic orbital configurations. This predictability enables forward-looking planning and routing mechanisms that explicitly incorporate time dependence [2], [14]. By leveraging the predictable movement of satellites, the network topology for future time slots can be accurately forecasted, and such satellite networks can be represented naturally as time-evolving (temporal) graphs where edges appear and disappear over time [15], [16].

Such a temporal graph can be derived as an ordered list or a stream of timed events, such as additions or deletions of edges as connectivity becomes feasible or infeasible.
