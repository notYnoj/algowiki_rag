Small vertex separators are known to exist for many classes of graphs with uniform weights \(w(\cdot)=1\). Planar graphs are known to admit \(\frac{1}{3}\)-balanced vertex separators of size \(O(\sqrt{n})\) \[43, 33\], genus-_g_ graphs are known to admit \(\frac{1}{3}\)-balanced vertex separators of size \(O(\sqrt{gn})\) \[21\], and similar results are known for minor-free graphs \[22\], geometric graphs \[37, 42\], induced-minor-free graphs \[25\], and other classes \[36, 12\]. These small separators also have numerous applications. To name a few, they naturally allow devising divide and conquer algorithms, dynamic programming algorithms, subexponential algorithms for NP-hard problems on special graphs, and maximum matching algorithms \[39, 34, 25\].

Unfortunately, while optimal planar separators can be found in linear time, none of these other results correspond to particularly fast algorithms often requiring exponential time in a parameter. For genus-_g_ graphs, the best-known separator theorem requires a surface-map/rotation system \[21\], which takes time exponential in _g_ to compute \[38, 6\], meaning it cannot be used to compute separators for graphs with even a small genus of polylog(_n_) in polynomial time. In the case of minor-free graphs, the time complexity can be even worse: One of the best-known separator theorems \[22\] corresponds to an algorithm which uses time proportional to multiple tower functions in the size of the minor. At best, some of the constructions correspond to polynomial-time algorithms (with a large polynomial overhead), such as those of induced-minor-free graphs \[25\], but such results are typically conjectured to be significantly sub-optimal existential bounds for the classes to begin with. Due to the significant overhead of these algorithms, many direct applications of recursively-separable graphs assume some routine is provided that produces a separator, and the time complexity of the assumed routine is incorporated into their final result \[15, 26, 2, 16, 23, 17, 10, 32, 44\].

Even for cases where the graph class might admit a fast algorithm (such as planar graphs), such algorithms only apply to graphs within their respective classes. In most practical settings for graph partitioning, we do not have any special guarantees that the graph falls into a certain class, but a graph might still have a small separator without properly falling into a special class: For instance, road networks, which are known to admit _empirically_ good separators \[14\], are _almost_ planar, in the sense that removing a relatively small number of vertices corresponding to structures like bridges will make them planar. Or, more theoretically, a graph could be nearly minor-free, in the sense that it contains a very large minor-free induced subgraph.

Thus, we naturally turn to the question of approximation algorithms: Given a graph _G_, can we approximate the minimum _c_-balanced vertex separator? It turns out that this is essentially impossible as-stated: It is NP-hard to approximate the minimum _c_-balanced vertex separator up to an _additive_ approximation of \(O(n^{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon})\) for any \(\epsilon>0\) \[7\]. Fortunately, we may instead use _pseudo_-approximations (also called bi-criteria approximations): For a graph _G_ whose minimum _c_-balanced vertex separator has size OPT_<sub>c</sub>_, we can produce a \(\Theta(c)\)-balanced vertex separator with size close to OPT_<sub>c</sub>_.
