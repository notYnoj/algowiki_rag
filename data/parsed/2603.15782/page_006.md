<tbc>\(v_i\in\mathbb{R}^n\), we use scalar variables \(x_i\ge 0\) that indicate whether node _i_ belongs to the separator.

One could now try to apply the primal-dual MMWU framework described above: maintain primal variables _v<sub>i</sub>,x<sub>i</sub>_, find an inequality violated by these variables, and update these variables. Unfortunately, we were unable to find such a scheme. Variables _v<sub>i</sub>_ and _x<sub>i</sub>_ play very different roles, and it is not clear how to jointly update them while having a small "width" of the oracle. We also tried to find a usable min-max formulation, but, as mentioned, the balance requirement prevents the same trick of [28] from being applied to our problem, since the MMWU algorithm does not automatically satisfy these constraints.

Overcoming this difficulty was the first technical challenge that we faced. To resolve it, we avoid the standard primal-dual MMWU framework, and instead use a hybrid of the two approaches (which we will call the "partial-variable" MMWU framework): we maintain and update variables _v<sub>i</sub>_, but not _x<sub>i</sub>_. The latter are maintained neither explicitly nor implicitly, even though they are part of the primal SDP formulation. We carefully design an oracle that does not need variables _x<sub>i</sub>_ as input, while still outputting dual variables, and we apply the MMWU regret-minimization bound directly to the output of this oracle. We also extend this framework to a more general class of SDPs.

**Technical contribution #2: new oracle for the padding bound** To prove Theorems 1 and 5, we provide two different implementations of the oracle. The oracle for the first theorem is very close to the one in [24]; the main difference is that we generalize the minimum _s-t_ cut subroutine for edge separator to _element_ separators. For Theorem 5 we use a very different approach. First, we use random sampling to construct an instance of a multicommodity flow problem, which we then approximately solve using the algorithm in [8]. If the flow is large then its demand graph gives a valid output of the oracle (a feedback matrix of small width). The case of small flow is the most technically challenging part of the analysis. First, we use dual prices of the multicommodity flow problem to construct a shortest path pseudometric on _V_ . We then apply the definition of the padding bound and show the existence of Lipschitz approximation to Gaussian projections. Using this approximation, we finally prove that an _s-t_ minimum cut computation for a random Gaussian direction yields a balanced element cut of small cost with constant probability.

## 1.3.1 Bit complexity

Like previous works [3, 28], we apply maximum flow as an important subroutine. Moreover, depending on the value of the maximum flow, we may need to produce a corresponding minimum cut. For both vertex capacitated and directed graphs, the fastest-known maximum flow algorithm (exact, approximate, or otherwise) is the recent almost-linear time exact maximum flow result of Chen et al. [9], which only applies to networks with bounded integral capacities. This presents an issue for prior work: As presented, both the work of Arora and Kale for balanced edge separator [3] and its follow-up works [40, 24], as well as the work of Lau, Tung, and Wang [28] present their algorithms for real-valued weights. Since almost-linear time maximum flow requires bounded integers, a bit more work would be required to complete the analysis in these works in this paradigm.

In the case of balanced edge separator, there are alternative strongly near-linear-time undirected maximum flow routines that may be used instead [20]. However, in the case of directed edge expansion, there is no such alternative.

For our problem, we use the element-capacitated network to devise our maximum flow instances in such a manner that their capacities do not depend on the maintained variables {_v<sub>i</sub>_} in any way. However, while this is the main step required to avoid such issues, this does not automatically handle our bit complexity bounds, since our capacities do still depend on a number of different quantities, including our finite element costs, a guess at the value of the optimum solution (obtained via binary search), and several constants.
