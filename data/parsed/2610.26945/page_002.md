<tbc>theory repeatedly transform and multiply dense Fock, overlap and density matrices, both in localized-basis and in plane-wave implementations (e.g. \[3\]). With Bloch _k_-points these matrices are complex Hermitian, and many-body perturbation methods such as GW \[4\] are dominated by large complex products (ZGEMM). Complex GEMM is equally central to frequency-domain electromagnetics, quantum transport and quantum-circuit simulation.

These applications need double precision. Total-energy differences of a few meV on totals of 10<sup>3</sup>-10<sup>5</sup>Hartree, orbital orthogonalization, ill-conditioned overlap matrices and long iterative processes all amplify rounding errors beyond what single precision can absorb \[5\]. Mixed- precision algorithms such as iterative refinement move part of the work to lower precision \[6, 7\], but they still require an FP64 residual and do not replace GEMM itself.

## The FP64 gap and its emulation.

GPU vendors now spend most of their transistor budget on low-precision matrix engines for machine learning, and FP64 is falling behind. On NVIDIA’s current Blackwell generation, both the GeForce RTX 50 series and the RTX PRO workstation boards built on the same GB20x chips execute FP64 at 1/64 of the FP32 rate \[8\]. Even in the data centre, the Blackwell Ultra platform HGX B300 delivers 10 TFFLOP/s of FP64, compared with 296 TFFLOP/s for its predecessor HGX B200 \[9\]. Emulating FP64 GEMM with exact low-precision products is therefore attractive, and NVIDIA itself now ships such an emulation in cuBLAS \[10, 11\]. Ozaki scheme I \[12\] splits the significands into _L_ slices and needs _O(L_<sup>2</sup>) slice products; it has been mapped to Tensor Cores \[13\] and to INT8 engines \[14, 15\], and a related splitting recovers FP32 accuracy on Tensor Cores \[16\]. Ozaki scheme II \[17\] takes a different route. Instead of splitting the significands, it represents the integrated matrices by their residues modulo _s_ small, pairwise coprime integers and multiplies the residue matrices exactly. Each additional modulus adds about 8 bits of precision at the cost of one more INT8 product, so the work grows linearly rather than quadratically with the target precision. The scheme has since been extended to FP8 \[18\] and to complex products \[19\], and shown to be competitive on CPUs with AMX and SVE2 \[20\].

## Why Intel Arc.

Intel’s Xe2-based Arc Pro B60 offers 24GB of GDDR6 \[21\] at a retail price of about US$600 \[22\], an unusually large memory for its price class. Unlike the preceding Arc Alchemist generation, Xe2 has native FP64 support \[23\], but only at 1/16 of the FP32 rate: we measure 0.76 TFFLOP/s for DGEMM and 11.9 TFFLOP/s for SGEMM, while the XMX matrix engines deliver ≈190TOP/s of INT8. This ratio of ≈250 between INT8 and FP64 throughput lets the emulation pay off by an order of magnitude, and the large memory accommodates the _O(sN_<sup>2</sup>) workspace of the scheme for large problems. Intel has also announced, but not yet shipped, its Xe3P-based data-centre GPU "Crescent Island" \[24\], now the Intel GPU D- Series. Its first model, the D270, carries up to 480GB of LPDDR5X and is advertised for workloads from low-precision inference to FP64 scientific computing \[25\]. Both products thus combine large memory with strong low-precision throughput, which makes them natural targets for Ozaki scheme II.
