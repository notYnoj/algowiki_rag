On the GPU, the same batch of colors is evaluated in parallel across the device, which brings the batched `diff-soup` Jacobian to within a factor of about 2.4 of the analytical Jacobian ( 3), so the factorization of the pencil matrix _W_ remains the bottleneck.

For the two-dimensional problems considered here, the fill-in of the sparse factorization grows superlinearly with the degrees of freedom [76, 73], so the factorization dominates the solver cost at scale. Both `diff-soup` and the alternative scientific computing libraries employ sparse direct LU solvers, however at the scale of large two- and three-dimensional PDEs the superlinear scaling of the factorization becomes prohibitively expensive for CPU based algorithms like `SuperLU\_MT`, KLU, and the sparse LU of MATLAB, which are limited to a single core or a small number of threads. `diff-soup` is able to overcome this on much larger problems because `cuDSS` distributes the factorization across the GPU and exploits its massive parallelism, achieving up to a 440\(\times\) speedup compared to the same JIDA integration algorithm using a CPU-based KLU solver.

However, the GPU based `diff-soup` code incurs a fixed overhead, since the device kernels sacrifice per-operation efficiency for parallelism and every step requires GPU kernel launches and host-device synchronization. Consequently, the GPU solver exhibits a nearly constant cost on the Brusselator and Cahn-Hilliard problems until the factorization grows large enough to dominate the wall time (Figure 8b and d).
