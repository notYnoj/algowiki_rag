We implement three native optimization algorithms in `diff-soup`: a Newton trust-region method using the exact Hessian or its Gauss-Newton approximation, a Levenberg-Marquardt method, and a limited-memory BFGS method. Additionally, we provide wrappers to the `optax` and `optimistix` libraries for first-order training of embedded deep learning models and for further solvers.

We provide a variety of tools and modules in `diff-soup` for typical inference workflows including data assimilation, batching, and function approximation. Specifically, we provide pre-built tools for projecting image data onto a mesh, refining the mesh, and estimating initial and boundary conditions from image data. For batching, multiple shooting is employed to partition large trajectories into shorter windows that are joined by continuity constraints. This both reduces the computational cost of long trajectory simulations and improves the robustness and conditioning of the optimization problem [93, 25]. In addition, we offer a variety of prebuilt modules for approximating functions and operators that commonly occur in PDEs. Orthogonal polynomials, neural networks, and neural operators are supplied for approximating constitutive relationships, functions, and operators within PDEs. We provide Karhunen-Loève expansions and coordinate networks for approximating spatial fields both classically and using deep learning. Additionally, a variety of differentiable, piecewise interpolants such as constant, linear, and cubic polynomials are offered for time-varying signals.

As discussed in Section 3.2, `diff-soup` employs both data and model parallelism for distributing computation across multiple GPUs. Under data parallelism, subsets of a larger dataset, either distinct experiments or windows for multiple shooting, are distributed across all GPUs, and the losses, gradients, and curvature are then summed across the devices. We specialize this for hierarchical inference problems where the dataset consists of many experiments on distinct domains with multiple episodes per domain. The problem can be distributed into three types of parameters: global parameters shared by all experiments, cluster parameters that are shared by a single domain, and local parameters that are specific to a distinct episode. This allows us to exploit the arrowhead structure of the resulting joint Hessian and employ a block Newton solver that uses a Schur complement over the shared block.
