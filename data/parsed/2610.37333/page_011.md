Beneath the controllers, the QSS NDF and FLC BDF families differ in the response of the multistep coefficients in Eq. (3) to a realized change in the step-size history. The QSS implementation retains the constant-step coefficients at every step, which makes the formula and its error estimates exact only on a uniform step grid, so it prefers long runs at a fixed step size, defers order changes until the history has run uniformly for several steps, and answers a realized change by rescaling the entire backward-difference history onto the new grid [35]. The fully variable-coefficient alternative of EPISODE and VODE recomputes every coefficient from the realized nonuniform history, which gives the strongest stability on rapidly varying steps but lets every change in the history perturb the iteration matrix, sacrificing the reuse of the factorization [57, 64]. The FLC implementation compromises by pinning the leading coefficient at its constant-step value while the trailing coefficients track the realized history, so that the iteration matrix depends on the step history only through \(\gamma_n\) and the stability loss relative to the fully variable- coefficient form is modest [59]. `JNDF` follows the QSS convention with the backward-difference table of Shampine and Reichelt [35], while `JBDF` follows the FLC convention with the Nordsieck history of Hindmarsh et al. [31] and `JIDA` with the divided-difference history of Brenan et al. [63]. We refer the reader to the standard references for complete descriptions of the formulas, controllers, and failure heuristics [56, 65, 63, 35, 31, 29].

## 2.2. Exploiting Sparsity

As established above, the two dominant computational bottlenecks of the VSVO integration are the formation of the Jacobian _J<sub>n</sub>_ and the factorization of the pencil matrix _W<sub>n</sub>_ within the modified Newton iteration [29, 61]. Conventional discretization methods for PDEs, such as the finite difference method (FDM), the finite volume method (FVM), and the finite element method (FEM), represent local differential operators in a sparse form such that each component of _y_ couples to a bounded set of neighbors, so the Jacobian \(J_n=\partial f/\partial y\) has _O(n_) nonzero entries in a pattern that is fixed by the discretization _a priori_. Exploiting this property, we store _J<sub>n</sub>_ and _W<sub>n</sub>_ in sparse data structures that drastically reduce the memory footprint. In this section, we describe how `diff-soup` uses this structure to speed up the Jacobian computation, pencil matrix factorization, and efficiently handle dense, global variables.

Traditionally, differential equation libraries offer three routes to the Jacobian: analytical Jacobians supplied by hand, as in SUNDIALS and PETSc<tbc>
