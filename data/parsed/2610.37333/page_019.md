For a singular mass matrix, the terminal condition must itself be consistently initialized, because the algebraic components of the costate are determined by the adjoint constraints, and we refer the reader to Cao et al. \[84\] for the derivation in the fully implicit and higher-index settings.

The backward solve of Eq. (13) requires the forward trajectory _y(t_), and the implementations trade memory against recomputation through three strategies: a dense interpolant of the forward solution, checkpointing with segment recomputation, and re-integration of the state backward in time alongside the adjoint \[78, 34\]. The Julia ecosystem pairs the interpolant with an adaptive Gauss quadrature of the gradient integral in Eq. (14), which typically converges in far fewer evaluations than the time integration itself \[34, 86\]. For stiff problems, however, two stability concerns constrain these choices: the backward re-integration of the state, popularized for neural ODEs \[87\], is unstable because the reversed dissipative dynamics amplify small forward errors, and the adjoint system inherits the stiffness of the forward problem, so it must itself be integrated implicitly with the same sparse linear algebra as the forward solve \[86\]. The number of costates is parameter independent and quadrature accumulates the \(n_\theta\) gradient components additively, so the total cost scales as \(O(T(n)+n_\theta)\).

In the JAX-native engines, we instead differentiate the adaptive control loop in reverse mode as a discrete adjoint using the checkpointed while loop in `equinox`, which implements the optimal online checkpointing algorithms of Stumm and Walther \[88\] and Wang et al. \[89\]. Rather than storing the trajectory, the loop retains \(O(\sqrt{n_\text{steps}})\) checkpoints of the accepted states by default, where _n_steps is the user-prescribed step ceiling, and recomputes the segments between them during the backward sweep, bounding the memory at \(O(\sqrt{n_\text{steps}}n)\).
