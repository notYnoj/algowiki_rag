The Julia ecosystem exposes both routes behind a common sensitivity interface [34, 15]. Both the continuous and discrete paradigms can be computed in forward mode, where one tangent per parameter is propagated forward, or reverse (adjoint) mode, where one cotangent per scalar cost traverses backwards. This gives four methods as described in detail in this section and compared in  2: forward sensitivity analysis (FSA) and adjoint sensitivity analysis (ASA) for continuous sensitivities [84, 31], and forward and reverse AD for discrete sensitivities, as implemented in `diff-soup` [49].

FSA differentiates the governing equations with respect to each parameter. For a state-independent mass matrix, differentiating Eq. (2) with respect to \(\theta_j\) yields the tangent system

\(Ms'_j=\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}s_j+\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta_j},\) (11)

where \(s_j=\partial y/\partial\theta_j\in\mathbb{R}^n\) is the _j_-th column of the sensitivity matrix, and the fully implicit form of Eq. (1) differentiates the same way into \(\partial F/\partial ys_j+\partial F/\partial y's'_j+\partial F/\partial\theta_j=0\). The tangent systems are linear and share the pencil matrix _W_ with the forward solve, so the sensitivities advance alongside the state through cheap solves with the cached factors [31]. The sensitivity matrix is also useful beyond the gradient, as for least-squares cost functions the product \((\partial y/\partial\theta)^T(\partial y/\partial\theta)\) is the canonical Gauss-Newton approximation to the Hessian. Because the integrator carries one tangent system per parameter, the cost of FSA scales as \(O(n_\theta T(n))\), where _T(n_) denotes the cost of one forward VSVO solve under the sparse methods above.
