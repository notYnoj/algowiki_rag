Figure 4 shows that the largest support is not uniformly preferable. For a correction-free family the reduction factor is _p(n/(n_+1))<sup>3_N_</sup>. At fixed _q_, increasing _N_ decreases _n_ and increases this embedding overhead; corrections add a further cost. The fourteen-product family is therefore competitive at smaller dimensions, while the corrected twenty-eight-product family becomes preferable as its lower-order terms decrease relative to the aggregate. The same tradeoff delays the 105-product family: it remains below the 28-product family at _q_=4096, but exceeds it at _q_=14641. Larger tensor powers become useful when the additional outputs offset both embedding and correction costs.

The accuracy-constrained comparison in Figure 5 shows how parameter scaling changes the tradeoff. The smaller _q=n_<sup>2</sup> constructions become useful before the larger families can amortize their embedding costs. At larger dimensions, the _p_=32,35,37 and then _p_=125,133,141 families give the largest reductions at their respective precision thresholds. As the polynomials in Section 8.2 show, the benefit comes mainly from additional outputs rather than a smaller aggregate: more mixed products can be suppressed with little change to the correction budget. The half-, third-, and quarter-precision choices therefore provide distinct product counts at comparable total cost.
