# Mining Correlated Motifs with Wildcards in PPI Networks: Exact and Optimal

Lucas P. Oliveira<sup>∗</sup> Ben Bals<sup>†</sup> Aalt D. J. van Dijk<sup>‡</sup> Solon P. Pissis§

**Abstract.** A _protein-protein interaction_ (PPI) network is a graph whose nodes are protein sequences and whose edges represent physical interactions between proteins. _Correlated Motif Mining_ (CMM) seeks pairs of sequence motifs whose co-occurrence in interacting proteins reveals binding sites and interaction mechanisms. Formally, in the \((\ell,d)\)-CMM problem, we are given a graph _G_=(_V,E_), with \(V\subseteq\Sigma^*\) for an alphabet \(\Sigma\), and integers \(0\le d<\ell\), and we seek a pair {_X,Y_} of motifs, each of length \(\ell\) and containing _d_ wildcards, that maximizes a support measure _f(G,{X,Y_}); e.g., the count _f<sub>E</sub>_ of edges \(\{u,v\}\in E\) such that _X_ matches a substring of _u_ and _Y_ matches a substring of _v_, or vice versa. Although existing approaches have produced biologically meaningful results, they are either inefficient or heuristic-based, as the underlying optimization problem is NP-hard. Here, we revisit \((\ell,d)\)-CMM. For _d_=0, we give an exact O(_N_<sup>2</sup>)-time and O(_N_)-space algorithm, where _N_ is the total length of the node strings, and prove that this time is conditionally optimal under the Strong Exponential-Time Hypothesis. For any _d\>_0, we give an exact \(\mathcal{O}\left(\genfrac(){pt}{2}{\ell}{d}^2N^2\right)\)-time and O(_N_)-space algorithm, and observe that the problem is W[1]-hard parameterized by _d_. The latter algorithm improves the state of the art by exponentially large factors in both time and space. We further demonstrate experimentally that our algorithms are practical on real-world PPI networks and find significantly higher-scoring motif pairs than the state of the art. Moreover, we show that our algorithms find many top-scoring motif pairs that the state of the art misses entirely. Taken together, our results suggest that exact CMM with wildcards is more tractable in practice than previously assumed.

**1 Introduction.** Large-scale biological networks capturing protein-protein interactions (PPIs) are now available for a wide range of organisms [20-22]. While such data reveal how proteins function within a PPI

network, they offer no direct insight into how those interactions are encoded at the sequence level--in particular, which regions of protein sequences correspond to physical interaction sites. Popular tools such as AlphaFold [11] have revolutionized protein structure prediction, but experimental validation of interaction interfaces at scale remains a major bottleneck: the human proteome alone contains tens of thousands of binary interactions [20], and determining their structural basis experimentally is infeasible at this scale. Moreover, even large-scale structural characterization of interacting proteins would not directly answer the question of how interaction specificity is encoded in protein sequence or structure. In other words: _which specific residues determine which partners a protein can bind?_

To answer this question, a variety of bioinformatics approaches have been developed [8,10,23]. In particular, several algorithmic approaches have been proposed for locating binding sites by mining overrepresented pairs of sequence patterns, called _motif pairs_, from sequences of interacting proteins [1,15-18,25]. One such approach, known as _Correlated Motif Mining_ (CMM), identifies binding sites by searching for a consensus pattern in one group of proteins that interacts with nearly all proteins containing a second consensus pattern. When such a pair is found, both patterns are likely to represent complementary surface regions that enable physical binding. Figure 1.1 illustrates this idea: \(\{\mathsf{G}\diamond\mathsf{P}\diamond\mathsf{NY},\mathsf{P}\diamond\mathsf{P}\}\) and \(\{\mathsf{R}\diamond\mathsf{R}\diamond\mathsf{pS},\mathsf{L}\diamond\mathsf{R}\diamond\mathsf{pS}\}\) are two correlated motif pairs, where an undirected edge connects two protein sequences if the first contains motif \(\mathsf{G}\diamond\mathsf{P}\diamond\mathsf{NY}\) and the second contains motif \(\mathsf{P}\diamond\mathsf{P}\), and analogously for \(\{\mathsf{R}\diamond\mathsf{R}\diamond\mathsf{pS},\mathsf{L}\diamond\mathsf{R}\diamond\mathsf{pS}\}\). Although it remains unclear what fraction of protein interfaces can be described by correlated motifs of this kind, existing approaches consistently show that such motifs do contain meaningful information about interaction interfaces.

Existing computational approaches fall into two broad categories: (i) interaction-driven approaches [16-18]; and (ii) motif-driven approaches [1, 15,25]. Interaction-driven approaches search for (quasi- )bicliques--that is, subsets of nodes in a PPI network, in which nearly every node on one side is connected to nearly all nodes on the other.
