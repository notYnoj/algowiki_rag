## Round Reduction (Section 6).

We now give an informal overview of the reduction from _T_-round coloring algorithms to one-round symmetry breaking. Suppose that there exists a quantum-PN algorithm A which produces a proper coloring in _T_ communication rounds with probability 1. We show how to transform A into a one-round quantum-PN algorithm B which breaks symmetry on every block of 2_T_+2 consecutive nodes, meaning that such a block can never produce identical outputs. The idea is for B to simulate A using only one communication round. In this single round, neighboring nodes distribute enough maximally entangled states to support all the communication that A would perform during its _T_ rounds. Afterwards, every message that A would send is simulated by teleportation, but without sending the classical teleportation outcome. We call this procedure _wishful teleportation_.

Recall that in standard teleportation \[BBCJPW93\], a node _p_ can transmit a quantum state \(|\psi\rangle\) to a node \(p'\) using a shared maximally entangled state \(|\Phi\rangle\) and classical communication. To do so, _p_ performs a joint measurement on \(|\psi\rangle\) and its share of \(|\Phi\rangle\), obtaining a classical outcome _k_, also known as the _teleportation key_. The system held by \(p'\) then becomes similar to \(|\psi\rangle\), up to some correction _P<sub>k</sub>_. Thus, once _p_ communicates the classical key _k_ to \(p'\), the exact state \(|\psi\rangle\) can be recovered by \(p'\). In wishful teleportation, we replace the communication of _k_ by a guess of translation-invariant keys. This is likely to fail, but our reduction algorithm B can exploit it by outputting the guessed keys together with the outcome of the attempted simulation of A : if every guess is correct, then the simulation algorithm B faithfully reproduces A , which by assumption breaks symmetry; otherwise, the incorrect guess itself breaks symmetry. This guarantees that symmetry is broken by a one-round algorithm B whether A is faithfully simulated or not.

More precisely, in each round of communication \(t\in\{1,2,\dots,T\}\) simulated by wishful teleportation, node _p_ obtains two teleportation keys: _k<sub>t,L</sub>(p_) and _k<sub>t,R</sub>(p_) for the states it respectively attempts to teleport to its neighbors on the left (node _p_-1) and right (node _p_+1). To recover the states sent by nodes _p_-1 and _p_+1, node _p_ would need the keys _k<sub>t,R</sub>(p_-1) and _k<sub>t,L</sub>(p_+1), which are not communicated. Instead, _p_ simply guesses that they satisfy

(3) \(k_{t,R}(p)=k_{t,R}(p-1)\text{and}k_{t,L}(p)=k_{t,L}(p+1),\)

and applies the corrections _P_<sub>_k<sub>t,R</sub>(p_)</sub> and _P_<sub>_k<sub>t,L</sub>(p_)</sub> to the appropriate registers. Whenever these equalities hold, the corresponding teleportation step succeeds. See Figure 2 for an illustration.
