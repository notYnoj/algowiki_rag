## 7. Discussion

At finite precision, products of unitaries and continuous evolutions are the same object, whichever we start from. Holding the entries at finite precision turns a continuous evolution into a product within \(\sqrt{2}\sigma 2^{-k}T\) of it (Lemmas 3.4 and 3.8). Holding the clock at finite precision turns a product of unit steps from a smooth path with separated samples back into that path’s own evolution, up to the half-step phases and _O_(1_/T_) (Theorem 6.4). Theorems therefore transfer between the two settings, and a theorist can derive a discrete result from its continuous counterpart instead of proving it again. The quantum linear systems problem shows what this buys, since there the continuous theorem and the comparison together recover the \(O(\kappa/\delta)\) count that was previously obtained through a discrete adiabatic theorem (Corollaries A.2 and 6.9).

Because the comparisons concern evolutions rather than adiabatic transport, I expect them to help wherever a time-dependent evolution is simulated. Existing methods choose their discretization from the Hamiltonian in different ways. Wiebe, Berry, Høyer, and Sanders choose step lengths from the local size of the derivatives of _H_ \[29\]. Berry, Childs, Su, Wang, and Wiebe rescale time by \(\|H(t)\|\), so that costs scale with \(\int\|H(t)\|dt\) rather than \(Tmax_t\|H(t)\|\) \[4\]. In Low and Wiebe’s interaction-picture algorithm, the average of \(\|\dot{H}\|\) controls the number of discretization points and enters the cost logarithmically \[22\]. Poulin, Qarry, Somma, and Verstraete avoid smoothness assumptions altogether by sampling at random times \[25\]. Holding entries at finite precision needs neither derivatives nor random times, so I expect its equal-variation checkpoints to complement these methods. Each factor it produces is time-independent, and qubitization \[21\] can implement it. On analog hardware, a device whose finite-resolution controls truncate the entries of _H_ in a fixed basis implements _H_<sup>(_k_)</sup>(_t_), so Lemma 3.4 applies to it, and so does Theorem 3.11 whenever _H_<sup>(_k_)</sup> is piecewise constant, as it is for piecewise-monotone _H_ (Proposition 4.2).

The correspondence is first order, and that leaves its main open questions, since some continuous theorems give much more than _O_(1_/T_). For gapped Gevrey families whose derivatives all vanish at the two ends, the continuous leakage has bounds of the form exp(-_cT<sup>a</sup>_), with _c\>_0 and 0_\<a\<_1 \[13, 14\]. Analytic Hamiltonians even have exponentially small estimates under suitable conditions at the two ends \[17, 24, 15\]. At fine steps, transferring such a bound means taking the evolution error \(\varepsilon\) comparable to it, and the precision _k_ of Lemma 3.8, which is \(O(log(\sigma T/\varepsilon))\), then grows at most linearly in _T_. The factor counts of Lemma 3.8(2) and Section 4, however, grow like 2_<sup>k</sup>_, which, for a fixed piecewise-monotone path rescaled to \[0_,T_\], is like \(T/\varepsilon\)<tbc>
