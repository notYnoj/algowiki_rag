Much less is known about hypergraphs in the query model. While cut queries yield _O(n_)- and \(\widetilde{O}(n^2)\)-query algorithms for connectivity \[11\] and Min Cut \[32\], respectively, there are also clear limitations to cut’s effectiveness on hypergraphs. Chen et al. \[14\] identify a pair of hypergraphs sharing the same cut profile, making them indistinguishable to an algorithm equipped only with cut queries. The authors then prove that polynomially-many cut queries are insufficient to construct a cut-sparsifier, which is a key building block for cut query algorithms on graphs. To circumvent this barrier, Chen et al. introduce an edge oracle that returns a random hyperedge crossing the queried cut; using both cut and edge queries, they are able to construct \((1\pm\varepsilon)\)-approximate cut-sparsifiers for hypergraphs in \(\mathrm{poly}(n,\varepsilon)\) queries. In addition to their connectivity algorithm, Chakrabarty and Liao \[11\] further explored the power and limitations of cut queries, identifying that hyperedge parity is a barrier to hypergraph reconstruction and giving an algorithm to learn ‘even’ rank-_r_ hypergraphs using _O_(log_n_) cut queries per hyperedge when _r=O_(1).

While connectivity and minimum cut problems provide a natural starting point for research on the cut query model, other graph problems remain underexplored. In this work, we begin a systematic study of the query complexity of densest subgraph problems. For the Densest Subgraph problem (DSG), the goal is find a subset of vertices _S_ maximizing the ratio of edges to vertices within the induced subgraph _G_\[_S_\]. DSG is a natural problem to consider as a next step in studying the cut query complexity of (hyper)graph problems. Although more complex than connectivity and minimum cut problems, DSG still has many well-known polynomial-time algorithms and approximations in classical settings (see \[28\] for a detailed survey of algorithmic results and applications).

In particular, we will consider a line of work on DSG in the streaming model, initiated by the iterative peeling algorithm of Bahmani et al. \[4\]. By removing all vertices of degree less than \(2(1+\varepsilon)\) times the current density, they achieve a \((2+\varepsilon)\)-approximation for DSG in \(O(\mathrm{log}_{1+\varepsilon} n)\) rounds over the graph. Since we can learn induced degrees in _O_(1) cut queries, this algorithm can be implemented in the query model, as shown in Section B.2.

**Proposition 1.** Let _G_=(_V,E_) be a weighted graph. There is a deterministic algorithm that makes \(O(n/\varepsilon)\) cut queries and returns a \((2+\varepsilon)\)-approximation to the densest subgraph of _G_.

Following this peeling result, two works independently discovered that _uniformly sparsifying_ an unweighted graph down to \(O(n\mathrm{log}n/\varepsilon^2)\) edges preserves densest subgraphs up to a \((1\pm\varepsilon)\) factor \[16, 30\], giving single-round streaming algorithms. As a random edge can be sampled with _O_(log_n_) cut queries, Cohen-Addad et al. \[15\] observe that this result also generalizes to the query model.<sup>2</sup>

**Proposition 2.** Let _G_=(_V,E_) be a simple graph. There is a randomized algorithm that makes \(O(n\mathrm{log}^2n/\varepsilon^2)\) cut queries and returns a \((1+\varepsilon)\)-approximation to the densest subgraph of _G_.

More generally, Esfandiari et al. \[16\] prove that uniform sparsification gives approximations for _heavy subgraph problems_, a family of problems also including Densest Bipartite Subgraph, _d_-Max Cut, and _d_-Sum-Max Clustering.

We also study the related Densest _k_-Subgraph problem (D_k_S), where one must find a subgraph of cardinality _k_ with the most edges. Unlike Densest Subgraph, D_k_S is NP-hard and quite hard to approximate; it is known that D_k_S does not admit a PTAS under various complexity assumptions \[17, 26, 34\], and it is conjectured that the best-known \(O(n^{1/4+\varepsilon})\)-approximation of \[7\] is optimal.
