**Claim 11.** _If_ \(h_1h_2\in E(H)\)_, then T contains a directed path between h_<sub>1</sub> _and h_<sub>2</sub>_. Moreover, if for a tree edge_ \(h_3\in E(T)\)_, h_<sub>1</sub> _and h_<sub>2</sub> _lie in different weakly connected components of T-h_<sub>3</sub>_, then_ {_h_<sub>1</sub>_,h_<sub>2</sub>_,h_<sub>3</sub>} _is a triangle in H._

_Proof of Claim 11._ If \(h_1h_2\in E(H)\), then \(\mathrm{Inv}(h_1)\cap\mathrm{Inv}(h_2)\neq\emptyset\). Consider an edge _e_ in this intersection, and let _u_ and _v_ be the starting and ending vertices of _e_, respectively. By Observation 1, there exists a directed path _L_ from _v_ to _u_ in _T_, and since \(e\in\mathrm{Inv}(h_1)\cap\mathrm{Inv}(h_2)\), both _h_<sub>1</sub> and _h_<sub>2</sub> lie on _L_. Hence the directed path _P_ between _h_<sub>1</sub> and _h_<sub>2</sub> can be obtained as the subpath of _L_ between them.

Now assume that _h_<sub>1</sub> and _h_<sub>2</sub> lie in different weakly connected components of _T-h_<sub>3</sub>. Since the removal of _h_<sub>3</sub> from _T_ separates _h_<sub>1</sub> and _h_<sub>2</sub>, _h_<sub>3</sub> must belong to _P_ (and hence to _L_). Thus by Observation 1, \(e\in\mathrm{Inv}(h_3)\), and therefore \(e\in\mathrm{Inv}(h_1)\cap\mathrm{Inv}(h_2)\cap\mathrm{Inv}(h_3)\). Consequently, {_h_<sub>1</sub>_,h_<sub>2</sub>_,h_<sub>3</sub>} is a triangle in _H_. \(\lrcorner\)

Let _X_ and _Y_ be the weakly connected components of _T-e_<sub>1</sub>, and let \(X':=X+e_1\). Without loss of generality, assume that _e_<sub>2</sub> belongs to _X_. We claim that every _e<sub>i</sub>_ must lie inside \(X'\). Assume the contrary, and let _i_ be the first index such that _e<sub>i</sub>_ does not belong to \(X'\). By construction, _i\>_2, and hence \(e_{i-1},e_i\neq e_1\). Then _e_<sub>_i_-1</sub> lies in _X_ and _e<sub>i</sub>_ lies in _Y_ . Applying Claim 11 with _h_<sub>1</sub>=_e_<sub>_i_-1</sub>, _h_<sub>2</sub>=_e<sub>i</sub>_ and _h_<sub>3</sub>=_e_<sub>1</sub>, we obtain that {_e_<sub>1</sub>_,e_<sub>_i_-1</sub>_,e<sub>i</sub>_} is a triangle in _H_, which is a contradiction. Therefore, every _e<sub>i</sub>_ belongs to \(X'\).

Denote the endpoint of _e_<sub>1</sub> in _Y_ by _v_, and let \(T'\) be the underlying graph of _T_, rooted at _v_. Consider a tree edge \(e\in E(T)\) and suppose that it leads from _a_ to _b_. We say that it goes up if _b_ is closer to _v_ than _a_ in \(T'\). Otherwise, _e_ goes down. We also say that _e_ is an ancestor of \(e'\) if _e_ lies on the path between \(e'\) and _v_ in \(T'\). Note that _e_<sub>1</sub> is an ancestor of every _e<sub>i</sub>_: since \(v\in Y\) and each \(e_i\neq e_1\) lies in _X_, the path between _e<sub>i</sub>_ and _v_ in \(T'\) must contain _e_<sub>1</sub>.

Suppose that _T_ contains a directed path _P_, and let _e_ and \(e'\) be its first and last edges, respectively. We claim that _e_ and \(e'\) go in the same direction if and only if one is an ancestor of the other in \(T'\). Let _u_ be the vertex of _P_ that is closest to _v_ in \(T'\). Observe that _u_ divides _P_ into two parts in the following sense: edges encountered on the path before reaching _u_ go in one direction, and the remaining edges of _P_ go in the opposite direction. Therefore, _e_ and \(e'\) go in the same direction if and only if _u_ is either the first or the last vertex of _P_. Assume first that _u_ is an inner vertex of _P_. In this case, _e_ and \(e'\) lie in subtrees rooted at different children of _u_ in \(T'\), and hence neither edge is an ancestor of the other. Conversely, assume that _u_ is an endpoint of _P_. Without loss of generality, let _u_ be the first vertex of the path. Then _e_ is the only edge on _P_ incident to _u_. Since _u_ is closer to _v_ than any vertex of _P-e_, _e_ separates _P-e_ and _v_ in \(T'\). Therefore, _e_ is an ancestor of \(e'\).

Applying the statement above to the pair _e_<sub>1</sub> and _e_<sub>2</sub>, we obtain that these edges go in the same direction. Indeed, _e_<sub>1</sub> is an ancestor of _e_<sub>2</sub> in \(T'\), and since \(e_1e_2\in E(H)\), by Claim 11 there is a directed path between them in _T_. Similarly, _e_<sub>1</sub> and _e_<sub>2_t_+1</sub> go in the same direction, and therefore so do _e_<sub>2</sub> and _e_<sub>2_t_+1</sub>. Since indices of _e_<sub>2</sub> and _e_<sub>2_t_+1</sub> have different parities, the directions of _e_<sub>2</sub>_,e_<sub>3</sub>_,...,e_<sub>2_t_+1</sub> cannot strictly alternate. In other words, there must be some \(i\in[2,2t]\) such that _e<sub>i</sub>_ and _e_<sub>_i_+1</sub> go in the same direction. We know that \(e_ie_{i+1}\in E(H)\), and hence there is a directed path between them in _T_. As discussed above, combined with the same direction of these edges, this implies that one of them is an ancestor of the other. We consider two cases.

**Case** _e<sub>i</sub>_ **is an ancestor of** _e_<sub>_i_+1</sub>**.** By definition, _e<sub>i</sub>_ lies on the path from _e_<sub>_i_+1</sub> to _v_ in \(T'\). Since \(e_{i+1}\in X'\), the last edge of this path is _e_<sub>1</sub>. Notice that \(e_1\neq e_i,e_{i+1}\) because \(i\in[2,2t]\). Consequently, _e<sub>i</sub>_ separates _e_<sub>_i_+1</sub> and _e_<sub>1</sub> in \(T'\), and hence they lie in different weakly connected components of _T-e<sub>i</sub>_.<tbc>
