It remains to show that if neither reduction rule is applicable, then the size of \(S'\) is bounded by O(_k_+|_A_|). Intuitively, the argument is as follows: since every vertex in _T_ whose degree differs from two belongs to the set _S_, the tree can be decomposed into undirected paths whose endpoints lie in _S_ and whose internal vertices are all outside _S_. The number of such paths is |_S_|-1, which is bounded by Claim 4. After that, for any four consecutive vertices from \(S'\) on a single path, we can show that if Reduction Rule 2 is not applicable, then there is a profitable edge on this subpath. Moreover, Claim 5 guarantees that profitable edges from different subpaths (and from different paths) cannot share inverse edges. Hence, by Observation 4, we can sum up their profits. Therefore, if the size of \(S'\) is too large, it implies that I is a yes-instance.

**Claim 7.** _If Reduction Rule 1 and Reduction Rule 2 are not applicable and_ \(|S'|\ge 12|A|+16k\)_, then_ I _is a yes-instance._

_Proof of Claim 7._ First, observe that the underlying graph of _T_ can be represented as a set of paths P={_P_<sub>1</sub>_,...,P<sub>t</sub>_} such that:

• _t_=|_S_|-1;<tbc>

• for every path _P<sub>i</sub>_, its endpoints belong to _S_, while all its internal vertices lie outside _S_;<tbc>

• for every pair _u,v_ of consecutive vertices on a path, there is an edge in _T_ between _u_ and _v_ in one of the directions;<tbc>

• every edge of _T_ is covered by exactly one path from P.

To achieve this, we root the underlying graph of _T_ at an arbitrary vertex \(r\in S\). Then, for each \(s\in S\) with \(s\neq r\), we add to P the path from _s_ to its closest ancestor from _S_. Since \(r\in S\), such an ancestor always exists. Note that this construction guarantees that the first three required properties are satisfied. For the last property, recall that every leaf of _T_ is in _S_, and hence every edge of the tree is covered at least once. Conversely, suppose that two paths _P<sub>i</sub>_ and _P<sub>j</sub>_, starting at vertices _s_<sub>1</sub> and _s_<sub>2</sub> respectively, have a common edge. If _s_<sub>1</sub> is an ancestor of _s_<sub>2</sub> or vice versa, this is impossible by our construction. Otherwise, let _s_ be the least common ancestor of _s_<sub>1</sub> and _s_<sub>2</sub>. Since _P<sub>i</sub>_ and _P<sub>j</sub>_ share an edge, _s_ must be an internal vertex of both paths. But in this case, _s_ has degree at least three in _T_, and hence it must lie in _S_, which contradicts the structure of _P<sub>i</sub>_ and _P<sub>j</sub>_, because they must have ended at _s_. Notice that if \(t\ge 4(|A|+k)\), then by Claim 4, I is a yes-instance. Thus, we may assume that _t\<_4(|_A_|+_k_).

Consider a path \(P_i\in\mathcal{P}\), and let \(v_1,\dots,v_{p_i}\in P_i\) be the vertices from \(S'\) listed in the order they appear on the path. Note that for each pair _v<sub>j</sub>_ and _v_<sub>_j_+1</sub>, the subsegment of _P<sub>i</sub>_ between them must form a directed path in _T_. Indeed, if it did not, there would be a vertex _v_ between them that has either two incident incoming edges or two incident outgoing edges on the path. But we know that \(v\notin(S\cup S')\), and hence it must have in-degree one and out-degree one in _T_. We denote the directed path between _v<sub>j</sub>_ and _v_<sub>_j_+1</sub> by _P<sub>i,j</sub>_.
