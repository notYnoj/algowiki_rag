## 1 Introduction

The theory of potential maximal cliques (PMC) was pioneered more than twenty years ago by Bouchitté and Todinca \[3, 4, 5, 6\]. This combinatorial structure in graphs was introduced in the first place to compute efficiently the treewidth. Through the years, other applications of PMCs arose, a famous example being the design of polynomial-time algorithms for maximum weight independent set \[19, 24\]. Also, many efficient algorithms for NP-hard problems on graphs use the collection of PMCs as an input to achieve their goal, making thus the enumeration of PMCs be the bottleneck for numerous algorithmic challenges.

### 1.1 Motivations

Considering an undirected connected graph _G_=(_V,E_), a vertex set \(X\subseteq V\) is a _minimal separator_ of _G_ if there exists a pair \(a,b\in V\backslash X\) such that not only _a_ and _b_ are disconnected in _G_\\_X_ but also no proper subset of _X_ disconnects _a_ from _b_. We denote by \(\Delta_G\) the set of minimal separators of _G_. An apparently unrelated notion - but in fact with strong connections - is the concept of _potential maximal clique_ (PMC). A vertex set \(X\subseteq V\) is a PMC of _G_ if it is a maximal clique of some _minimal triangulation_ of _G_, _i.e._ an inclusion-wise minimal add-on of edges to make _G_ become chordal. Let \(\Pi_G\) denote the set of all PMCs of _G_. Concretely, due to the strong relationship between triangulations and tree decompositions, PMCs can be seen as "candidates for bags of useful tree decompositions", echoing the words of Grzesik, Klimosov, Pilipczuk and Pilipczuk in \[19\]. This reason explains why PMCs play a crucial role in the design of exact exponential-time algorithms, as detailed below. Note that PMCs can be defined for disconnected graphs, but since the set of PMCs of a disconnected graph is simply the disjoint union of PMCs of its connected components, all graphs considered in this paper are connected.

We call Treewidth the problem of determining the treewidth of a graph. Coming back to the analogy between PMCs and bags of tree decompositions, computing the set \(\Pi_G\) seems to be a natural approach to obtain the treewidth of _G_ and/or an optimal tree decomposition. This is what Bouchitté and Todinca stated formally in \[5\]. They proposed a dynamic programming procedure which solves Treewidth in time \(O(n^2\cdot|\Delta_G|\cdot|\Pi_G|)\), given that the sets \(\Delta_G\) and \(\Pi_G\) are provided, with _n_=|_V_|. This was later improved \[12\] with a running time \(O(n^3|\Pi_G|)\). Such execution times are exponential since a graph may admit a number of minimal separators and/or PMCs exponential in _n_.

In these aforementioned algorithms for Treewidth, PMCs are essential to achieve the divide-and-conquer scheme which determines the treewidth of successive graphs until going up to the input one. However, if we aim at using such algorithms in practice, we must be able to enumerate PMCs before the execution, as efficiently as possible. Minimal separators of a graph _G_ can be enumerated in polynomial delay \[1\], which implies a global enumeration time of \(O(n^3|\Delta_G|)\), which is output-linear. Bouchitté and Todinca \[6\] proposed an algorithm (that we call BT-enum) enumerating all PMCs of a graph with a running time in \(O(n^2m|\Delta_G|^2)\), where _m_=|_E_|. Additionally, they established the following inequality which strengthens even more the links between minimal separators and PMCs: \(\frac{|\Delta_G|}{n}\le|\Pi_G|\le n|\Delta_G|^2+n|\Delta_G|+1\). Due to the left-hand side of this inequality, their algorithm BT-enum guarantees an output-quadratic time: there can be instances on which the time taken by the enumeration is quadratic in the size of the output \(\Pi_G\).
