It is interesting that our catalytic algorithms require two passes over the output, as opposed to just a single one. We show that this is indeed necessary, by noting the following generalization of the Borodin-Cook method \[BC80; Bea91\] to catalytic space algorithms:

**Theorem 1.8.** For every _catalytic_ 1-output-pass algorithm that takes in a length-_n_ list of log(_n_)-bit integers and outputs the sorted list, we must have \(ST\unknown\Omega(n^2)\), where _S_ and _T_ are the clean space and runtime used by the algorithm, respectively.

It follows (from Theorem 1.8 and our algorithmic results) that catalytic algorithms which make one pass over the output tape are strictly weaker than those which make two passes. However, we note that our results nonetheless show some separation between catalytic and non-catalytic algorithms even with only a single pass over the output (see Proposition 5.3).

## 1.2 Related Work

As mentioned, few prior results in catalytic computing focus on time complexity, but two recent works have begun to explore this direction.

Cook and Pyne \[CP26\] give elegant algorithms for catalytically solving directed connectivity (with _O_(log_n_) clean space) in randomized \(\widetilde{O}(n\cdot m)\) time and deterministic \(\widetilde{O}(n^3\cdot m)\) time, and show how to estimate the probability that a _T_-step random walk ends at a given vertex in \(\widetilde{O}(mT^2/\varepsilon)\) time. These running times are close to the best-known running times achievable with large clean space, but are still at least one polynomial factor away.

Chmel, Dudeja, Koucký, Mertz, and Rajgopal \[CDKMR26\] studied how to transfer classical sublinear-space polynomial-time algorithms into the catalytic model. They give a catalytic algorithm for STCONN running in polynomial time, _O_(log_n_) clean space and \(n/2^{\Theta(\sqrt{\mathrm{log}n})}\) catalytic space, matching the best-known space bound; with randomness, their algorithm runs in \(O(m\cdot n^{2+\varepsilon})\) time on _n_-node _m_-edge graphs. They also give polynomial time algorithms for edit distance, LCS, and discrete Fréchet distance using sublinear catalytic space, largely matching known non-catalytic space bounds. However, their focus is primarily on achieving the correct space frontier using only catalytic space, while retaining polynomial time bounds. In contrast, we focus on preserving running times to the greatest extent possible, replacing the usual space of clever classical algorithms with almost entirely catalytic space.

A related space-restricted model is the "in-place" model, where a read-write tape with input _x_ must be overwritten with the output _f(x_), which has received recent attention in the computer algebra literature (e.g. \[Gre25\]) and in complexity theory (\[CGMPS25\]). The algorithms are usually allowed _O_(log_n_) bits of additional clean space, or sometimes _O_(log_n_) algebraic registers. In this model, among many other results, sorting can be computed in _O(n_log_n_) time \[Pas99\] and the Discrete Fourier Transform can be computed in _O(n_log_n_) time \[BE81; Tem91\]. Another variant is the restore model, wherein both the input and output tapes are read-write but the input tape must be restored to its initial contents at the end \[CMR18\]. In this augmented variant, there exist additive accumulation algorithms over fields for the truncated Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform in _O(n_log_n_) time, Strassen’s matrix multiplication algorithm in _O(n_log<sub>2</sub>(7)) time \[DG24a\], efficient polynomial remainder \[DG24b\], and (more generally) linear accumulation of any in-place computable function \[DG26\].

We make reference to known time-space tradeoff lower bounds, and prove an extension to a catalytic model in Section 5. These are among the small handful of unconditional lower bounds in complexity theory: a landmark result of Borodin and Cook shows that (in any "reasonable" model of computation, e.g. RAM Turing machines, with the transducer output model), the product of space overhead and runtime for any sorting algorithm must be at least \(\Omega(n^2/\mathrm{log}(n))\) \[BC80\], which Beame later improved to \(\Omega(n^2)\) \[Bea91\]. Unconditional time-space-product lower bounds have since been shown for other problems, such as discrete Fourier transform (DFT), integer multiplication, and matrix multiplication \[Yes84; Abr91\].
