\(\langle q,\tilde{x}_d\rangle\le\langle q,x_d\rangle\), and the error is confined to the documents’ smallest components. Thus, the sketch only perturbs the route the search takes through the graph, while the returned results are always ranked by their exact scores. Moreover, when _R_ is at least the number of non-zero components of every document, sketched search reduces to search over the full (quantized) vectors. On SPLADE- CoCondenser, whose documents have 127 non-zero components on average, we set _R_=80, which keeps only 62% of the non-zero entries while speeding up search by 1.22-1.38\(\times\) at recall@10 targets between 95% and 99%. We use _R_=128 for SPARSEUP, whose documents have 190 non-zero components on average. For both experiments, we set the value of _R_ via holdout training queries.

## 3.3 Sparse PiPNN

We now discuss the third core optimization behind GRIP, namely sparse partitioned graph construction. To motivate the need for a new graph construction approach that differs from the traditional paradigm of building navigable graphs by iteratively inserting documents via repeated calls to the beam search subroutine, we demonstrate in Figure 2 that on learned sparse embeddings the quality of<tbc>

<tbc>graph search improves substantially when the construction beam width (efc) is increased from 200 to 2000, while dense retrieval typically sees diminishing returns on search quality with efc values in the range of 100 to 300 [14, 26]. On MS MARCO v1, an HNSW graph built with an efc of 2000 is 1.7-3.5\(\times\) faster to search than one built with an efc of 200 at recall@10 targets between 95% and 98%, and the latter cannot exceed roughly 99% recall@10 at all. This finding further underscores the widely held observation that graph-based search is not particularly well-suited to sparse retrieval without further innovation. While we can overcome this shortcoming simply by increasing the efc parameter, this solution is not scalable as it requires 5.5-7.4 hours to construct an HNSW graph on MS MARCO v1 and extrapolating from those numbers, several days to build such a graph on MS MARCO v2’s 138M passages. This is impractical for collections that must be reindexed frequently.

To circumvent this prohibitively long index construction time, we turn to PiPNN [31], a recent algorithmic breakthrough in efficiently constructing graph-based ANN indexes that completely avoids the iterative search-based insertion procedure. Unfortunately, we cannot use PiPNN directly out-of-the-box for LSR because several steps of the algorithm are specifically designed for dense vectors. As described in Algorithm 2, we modify the original PiPNN construction procedure to be amenable to sparse vectors.

The key insight behind our _Sparse PiPNN_ approach is to replace PiPNN’s dense vector multiplication subroutines with sparse computations by utilizing inverted indexes to efficiently compute pairwise inner products. In particular, we apply this idea at the two stages of PiPNN that are dominated by dense computation. First, PiPNN partitions the collection by randomized ball carving: it samples a set of leaders, assigns every point to its nearest few leaders, and recurses until every partition holds at most _C_ points. Rather than comparing every point against every leader, we build an inverted index over the leaders’ non-zero components, so each point accumulates scores only for the leaders with which it shares at least one term; a point that shares no term with any leader is assigned to a leader chosen by hashing, so that such points do not all collapse into the same partition.

Second, within each leaf partition _P_, PiPNN computes all pairwise similarities with a dense matrix multiplication, at a cost of \(O(|P|^2\cdot|V|)\), which is wasteful when vectors span tens of thousands of dimensions but have only around a hundred non-zero components each. We instead build a small inverted index over the documents in _P_ and accumulate every pairwise inner product by iterating over its posting lists, at a cost of \(\sum_t|P_t|^2/2\), where _P<sub>t</sub>_ denotes the documents in _P_ with a non-zero weight on term _t_. The remaining steps of PiPNN, HashPrune and RobustPrune, carry over directly, with similarities measured by inner product rather than Euclidean distance throughout. Finally, deviating from the original PiPNN algorithm, we add a final refinement pass in which each document searches the finished graph starting from its own term-based seeds and re-prunes its neighbor list with the results. Although this pass performs one search per document, it searches a completed graph rather than inserting documents one at a time, and we find that it is important for search quality at very high recall targets.
