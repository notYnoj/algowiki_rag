## 7 Discussion

In this method the quantum processor only proposes coordinates. The classical computer then determines their coefficients by a Galerkin solve. The bounds of Sec. 3 relate the outcome law to the exposure of solution coordinates and turn the exposed coordinates into an energy-norm error. For positive-definite systems the Galerkin solve needs only a principal submatrix of \(\hat{A}\), and its error decreases monotonically as the sampled set grows.

The geometric formulation of Sec. 4 shows how these principles apply to linear systems from partial differential equations. The measurement basis matters more than the circuit. For the ducts, a Fourier basis matched to the boundary conditions reduces the subspace by one to two orders of magnitude, and the required number of modes grows slowly with the grid. The time schedule must be chosen for the linear system. The short step that is natural for eigenvalue SKQD fails on fine grids, because it does not dephase the low eigenvalues, whereas the multiscale heuristic improves on the fixed steps in several of our tests. Operator approximations are controlled by Proposition 15, while sampler approximations change coverage, achieved error, and postprocessing cost but not the target.

The examples also make the limitation concrete. In all problems studied, including curved vessels and chaotic cross sections, a classical residual-greedy selection finds the essential modes more efficiently than the quantum sampler. The measured efficiency \(\eta_\mathrm{s}\) lies between \(6\times 10^{-5}\) and \(6\times 10^{-2}\), so rare modes are covered poorly. The elliptic duct and the curved vessels nevertheless exhibit the mechanism by which sampling can help: when the source and the solution have different mode structures, the dephased law captures the solution while a source-based selection does not. A benefit requires that this mismatch occur together with an essential set that no classical method identifies cheaply. -space recovery [7] could supply a recovery signal where native filters are absent, such as the ellipse and the curved vessels. Initial states that flatten the outcome law on the essential set are a direct target for improving \(\eta_\mathrm{s}\).

The hardware experiments add two practical observations. First, the noise on both platforms behaved approximately as the replacement noise of Corollary 6, with a strength set mainly by the number of two-qubit gates. Second, a symmetry selection rule is an inexpensive noise diagnostic: the fraction of rejected lens shots measured the noise strength directly. Such rules exist whenever the cross section has an isometry that preserves the coefficients, and they should be built into the measurement basis where possible. On the other hand, the present experiments do not test the reduction of the subspace, because the number of shots exceeds the admissible dimension. Such a test requires larger registers and shallower circuits.

Several questions remain open. The high-probability bound of Corollary 4 requires complete discovery of an energy-norm essential set, which can be pessimistic; a bound in terms of retained energy would be sharper. The heuristic multiscale schedule performs well in our tests but has no guarantee. Finally, a useful-scale comparison must include classical input access, state preparation, and the full reconstruction cost, with matched accuracy.
