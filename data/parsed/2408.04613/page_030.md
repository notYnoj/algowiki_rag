It remains to analyze the time complexity of the reporting procedure. Note that if we have \(dp(\nu,i,\tau)>j\) in a recursive call, then we first find \(\ell\) in \(\mathcal{O}(\ell)\) time and then reconstruct the underlying subsequence in \(\mathcal{O}(\ell)\) time. Thus, all leaf recursive calls take \(\mathcal{O}(\ell_0)\) time in total.

We now prove that there are \(\mathcal{O}(\ell_0logn/\tau)\) internal recursive calls. Call a leaf recursive call _heavy_ if in it we have \(\ell\ge\tau/2\). We claim that each internal recursive call \(\nu\) has at least one heavy leaf recursive call in its backtracking subtree. Consider the deepest internal recursive call \(\mu\) in the subtree of \(\nu\). As \(\mu\) is the deepest internal recursive call, both its children are leaf recursive calls. As \(\mu\) is not a leaf recursive call, we have that \(\ell_\mu\ge\tau\) for it. As \(\ell_\mu=\ell_{\mu^L}+\ell_{\mu^R}\), we have that \(max\{\ell_{\mu^L},\ell_{\mu^R}\}\ge\tau/2\), and thus either \(\mu^L\) or \(\mu^R\) is a heavy leaf recursive call. Furthermore, note that, as the sum of \(\ell\) over all leaf recursive calls is equal to \(\ell_0\), there are at most \(2\ell_0/\tau\) heavy leaf recursive calls. Finally, as the depth of the recursion is O(log_n_), each heavy leaf recursive call has O(log_n_) ancestors, and thus there are \(\mathcal{O}(\ell_0logn/\tau)\) internal recursive calls in total as required.

Each internal recursive call takes O(log_n_) time dominated by the witness reconstruction. Therefore, the total time complexity is \(\mathcal{O}(\ell_0+\ell_0logn/\tau\cdot logn)=\mathcal{O}(\ell_0log^\alpha n)\), thus proving the claim. \(\blacktriangleleft\)

## B.1 Open Problem: Weighted Range LIS

Since Theorem 1.2 works for arbitrary Monge matrices and not only for unit-Monge matrices, the algorithm of Lemma B.14 can be easily extended to _weighted_ range LIS queries, where each element of _s_ is equipped with a positive weight and the queries ask to find maximum- weight increasing subsequences of _s[i..j_). For this, in Definition B.4, we make the weights of the diagonal edges equal to the weights of the corresponding elements of _s_, and the weights of backward edges larger than all weights in _s_. The only difference compared to the unweighted version of the problem is its time complexity, which depends on the core size of _M<sup>s</sup>_. In the unweighted case we have \(\delta(M^s)=\mathcal{O}(n)\) due to Observations 3.1 and B.10 because all elements of _M<sup>s</sup>_ are bounded by O(_n_). In the weighted case, we are only aware of the trivial bound \(\delta(M^s)=\mathcal{O}(n^2)\) if the individual weights are \(\Omega(n)\).

However, if \(\delta(M^s)\le\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^e)\) holds some constant \(e\ge 1\) and every length-_n_ sequence _s_, the whole algorithm takes time \(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^e)\) for preprocessing and O(log_n_) time for queries. To the best of our knowledge, no algorithm for weighted range LIS with truly sub-quadratic preprocessing time and poly-logarithmic query time is known. Therefore, a bound of the form \(\delta(M^s)\le\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^e)\) for _e\<_2 valid for every length-_n_ sequence _s_ would be of interest.

One way of proving such a bound could be a strengthened version of Theorem 1.1. Suppose that every two \(n\times n\) Monge matrices _A_ and _B_ satisfy \(\delta(A\otimes B)\le c\cdot(\delta(A)+\delta(B))+\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n)\) for some constant _c_; in particular, Theorem 1.1 proves this fact for _c_=2. Note that in a single recursive call, we recurse into two subproblems of size _n/_2, "desparsify" the resulting matrices, and then (max,+)-multiply them.
