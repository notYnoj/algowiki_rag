# A Simple and General Approach to top-_k_ Selection

Motti Goldberger<br> Yale School of Management, motti.goldberger@yale.edu<br> Nils Rudi<br> Yale School of Management, nils.rudi@yale.edu

**Abstract.** This paper considers the Bayesian top-_k_ identification problem, wherein a decision maker is tasked with selecting _k_ items from a pool of items of unknown quality. To assess the items, the decision maker adaptively allocates a fixed sampling budget, where each sample of an item yields a noisy observation of its quality, with the objective of minimizing the expected opportunity cost of the selected set. We propose an approach that allocates each batch of samples by minimizing a surrogate objective derived from an upper bound on the expected opportunity cost. The approach is general: samples can be allocated in batches, the selection size _k_ is arbitrary, and it applies to many sampling distributions. It is also simple and intuitive. We prove that the method’s posterior expected opportunity cost decays at an exponential rate of at least half the best rate achievable by any sampling rule. In simulations across a wide range of settings, the method is competitive with, and often improves upon, the best existing algorithm in each setting.

**Key words:** Ranking-and-Selection, Best-Arm Identification, Top-_k_ Identification

### 1. Introduction

This paper studies the problem of a decision maker who faces a large pool of items with unknown quality, can assess them through noisy and costly samples, and wants to identify and select the best item(s). This is commonly referred to as a _ranking and selection_ task in the operations literature (Chen et al. 2000, Chen and Lee 2010, Hong et al. 2021), and as a _best-arm(s) identification_ task in the multi-armed bandit literature (Kalyanakrishnan et al. 2012, Kaufmann et al. 2016, Russo 2020). This problem arises in many domains, including clinical trials (Villar et al. 2015, Chick et al. 2022), A/B/n testing (Scott 2015, Ju et al. 2019), machine-learning model selection and hyperparameter optimization (Snoek et al. 2012, Jamieson and Talwalkar 2016, Li et al. 2018), and simulation optimization (Fu et al. 2005). In each setting, the key question is how to adaptively allocate a fixed sampling budget to maximize the quality of the final selection.

A central goal of this paper is to treat the problem with generality so that the method is useful across a range of settings. Two aspects of this generality are worth discussing up front. The first concerns how samples are collected. The most adaptive option is to allocate one sample at a time and wait to observe the outcome before choosing the next. However, this can be impractical to implement. For example, in clinical trials, one may not be able to wait to observe an outcome before assigning the next treatment (Robertson et al. 2023), and in simulation optimization, many simulations may be run in parallel (Hong et al. 2021). We therefore consider the partially adaptive case, in which _batches_ of samples are chosen in rounds, and outcomes are observed between rounds.
