An alternative approach targets learning the allocation that maximizes the exponential rate at which the probability of incorrect selection decays as the budget grows, the large-deviation rate (Glynn and Juneja 2004). Algorithms of this kind run sequentially, again treating the current mean estimates as the truth, and choose allocations using the optimality conditions for this rate. Chen and Ryzhov (2023) establish such an algorithm that applies to general distributions in the _k_=1 setting and prove that the sampling proportions converge to the optimal proportions as the budget approaches infinity.

Next, we review methods that construct sampling rules directly from the current state. Methods similar in spirit to our approach are confidence and gap-based frequentist approaches, which allocate samples based on how close items are to the current estimated top-_k_ cutoff and how uncertain their estimated values are, such as gap-e (Gabillon et al. 2011, Bubeck et al. 2013) and KL-LUCB (Kaufmann and Kalyanakrishnan 2013). Our allocation algorithm is based on the same two quantities, but differs in how the top-_k_ cutoff is defined, how the distance from it is measured, and how uncertainty is used.

Elimination algorithms (Audibert et al. 2010, Karnin et al. 2013) divide sampling into rounds, allocate samples uniformly among the remaining items within a round, and eliminate the empirically weakest items according to an elimination schedule. The successive accepts and rejects (SAR) approach (Bubeck et al. 2013) also "accepts" empirically strong items, avoiding spending samples on items already determined to likely be in the top-_k_.

Algorithms based on the expected improvement (EI) rule, which originates from the Bayesian optimization literature (Jones et al. 1998), have been designed for top-1 selection problems (Ryzhov 2016, Qin et al. 2017, Chen and Ryzhov 2019). For each item, the algorithm computes the expected amount by which the item’s value exceeds that of the current best item, and samples the item with the largest improvement score. In the Gaussian case, Chen and Ryzhov (2019) show that a variant of EI achieves the optimal sampling proportions asymptotically.

A family of Bayesian algorithms referred to as _top two_ algorithms allocates a sample in each round by identifying a "leader" item and a "challenger" item, then flipping a weighted coin to decide which of the two to sample (Russo 2020). Various methods have been proposed for choosing the leader and challenger (Jourdan et al. 2022), for example, based on Thompson sampling (Russo 2020) or expected improvement scores (Qin et al. 2017). For _k_=1 and a broad class of sampling distributions, Russo (2020) proves that with an unbiased coin, the posterior probability of an incorrect selection decays at an exponential rate of at least half the best achievable rate. Russo (2020) further shows that with suitable tuning of the coin flip probability, it attains the optimal decay rate. Qin et al.
