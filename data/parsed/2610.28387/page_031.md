## 7 Discussion

The results of this study are potentially relevant to multivariate regional frequency analysis (Chebana and Ouarda, 2007; Akinsoji et al., 2026). In the discordancy and homogeneity tests of Chebana and Ouarda (2007), the dispersion of at-site L-comoment coefficients is weighted by record length, and its null distribution is obtained by simulating homogeneous regions from a fitted bivariate model, whose choice the authors note is subjective. The present estimators give the sampling covariance of each site’s L-comoments from the site’s own data, without a parametric model. They could potentially provide precision weights in place of weights based on record length, and they may serve as a basis for a simulation-free approximation to the null distribution of such statistics. These tests also use fourth-order L-comoment coefficients; the projection estimator covers them (Corollary 4.6), whereas the exactly unbiased estimator of order four requires the algorithmic extension noted in Remark 4.7. These order-four results follow directly from fixed-order U-statistic theory and the leave-one-out identity, and we include them as a supplementary extension because of this use in practice. Spatial dependence between sites, studied by Šimková (2017), is outside our assumption of independent pairs.

When _X_ and _Y_ are measured on the same scale, the symmetric component \(S_m=(\lambda_m^{[XY]}+\lambda_m^{[YX]})/2\) and the difference between the two directions \(D_m=(\lambda_m^{[XY]}-\lambda_m^{[YX]})/2\) may also be of interest; symmetric Gini constructions are studied by Sang et al. (2016). Following Serfling and Xiao (2007), we treated the ordered pair \((\lambda_m^{[XY]},\lambda_m^{[YX]})\) as the basic object. The vector \((\widehat{S}_2,\widehat{D}_2,\widehat{S}_3,\widehat{D}_3)^\mathsf{T}\) equals \(\mathsf{A}\widehat{\unknown}\), where A is block diagonal with two blocks \(\frac{1}{2}\big(\frac{1}{1}\frac{1}{1}\big)\). Its exact covariance is therefore \(\mathsf{A}\mathsf{Cov}(\widehat{\unknown})\mathsf{A}^\mathsf{T}\) by Theorem 3.1; as noted in Section 3.2, ignoring the correlation between the two directions overstates the variance of \(\widehat{D}_m\). Under exchangeability, (_X,Y_)=<sup>_d_</sup>(_Y,X_), we have \(\mathbb{E}(\widehat{D}_m)=0\) and \(\mathsf{Cov}(\widehat{S}_m,\widehat{D}_s)=0\) for \(m,s\in\{2,3\}\), which could serve as the basis of a test of exchangeability. Related tests based on the difference between the two directional Gini coefficients, such as the test for time reversibility of Shelef and Schechtman (2019), have relied on bootstrap approximations; for independent pairs, the exact covariance offers an alternative.

For routine asymptotic standard errors, the projection-based PSD estimator is a natural default. When finite-sample bias should be avoided, the unbiased estimator or its PSD projection is preferable, whereas the projection-based estimator can be preferable under squared-error loss for the whole covariance matrix (Section 6.3). For the ratio \(\widehat{\tau}_3\), the first-order approximation can overstate the variance under heavy-tailed margins, by up to 29% at _n_=20 in our GEV design (Appendix E.1). The exact covariance results extend those of Elamir and Seheult (2004) for univariate L-moments to L-comoments, and the rank-prefix algorithm of Section 5 makes the corresponding unbiased estimator practical for large samples. PSD and PD regularizations based on eigenvalue correction (Higham, 1988; Liu et al., 2014) are available when a covariance- matrix form or an inverse is required, although they generally sacrifice exact unbiasedness.<tbc>
