## 2.2 Representation and interpretation of L-comoments

L-comoments can also be written in terms of concomitants of order statistics, in the same way as univariate L-moments are written in terms of order statistics (Hosking, 1990). Consider a conceptual sample (_X_<sub>1</sub>_,Y_<sub>1</sub>)_,...,(X<sub>k</sub>,Y<sub>k</sub>_) of size _k_ from the distribution of (_X,Y_). If we order the sample by the _Y_ values and obtain the order statistics \(Y_{1:k}<\cdots<Y_{k:k}\), then the _X_ value paired with _Y<sub>r:k</sub>_ is called the _concomitant_ of _Y<sub>r:k</sub>_ and is denoted by _X_<sub>\[_r:k_\]</sub> (Yang, 1977; David and Nagaraja, 2003; Jamalizadeh and Balakrishnan, 2012). Note that _X_<sub>\[_r:k_\]</sub> is not the _r_th order statistic _X<sub>r:k</sub>_ of the _X_ values. It is the _X_ value of the pair whose _Y_ value has rank _r_, so _X_<sub>\[1:_k_\]</sub>_,...,X_<sub>\[_k:k_\]</sub> are in general not in increasing order. By Proposition 6 of Serfling and Xiao (2007),

\(\lambda_2^{[XY]}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\big(X_{[2:2]}-X_{[1:2]}\big),\lambda_3^{[XY]}=\frac{1}{3}\mathbb{E}\big(X_{[3:3]}-2X_{[2:3]}+X_{[1:3]}\big),\lambda_4^{[XY]}=\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}\big(X_{[4:4]}-3X_{[3:4]}+3X_{[2:4]}-X_{[1:4]}\big).\text{(2.4)}\)

In Appendix B.1, (2.4) is derived from Definition 2.1. Thus, if two pairs are drawn and the pair with the larger _Y_ value is identified, \(\lambda_2^{[XY]}\) is half the expected difference between the _X_ value of that pair and the _X_ value of the other pair. Similarly, if three pairs are drawn and sorted by their _Y_ values, \(\lambda_3^{[XY]}\) compares the _X_ value of the middle pair with the _X_ values of the two outer pairs.

Serfling and Xiao (2007) called \(\lambda_2^{[XY]}\), \(\lambda_3^{[XY]}\), and \(\lambda_4^{[XY]}\) the L-covariance, the L-coskewness, and the L-cokurtosis. They called the standardized versions \(\tau_m^{[XY]}=\lambda_m^{[XY]}/\lambda_2(X)\) and \(\tau_m^{[YX]}=\lambda_m^{[YX]}/\lambda_2(Y)\) the L-comoment coefficients, and \(\tau_2^{[XY]}\) in particular the L-correlation. The L- correlation lies in \[-1,1\], and it equals \(\pm 1\) if and only if _X_ is a strictly monotone function of _Y_ (Serfling and Xiao, 2007, Proposition 4). Under linear regression of _X_ on _Y_ with affinely equivalent margins (that is, _Y_ has the same distribution as \(\alpha+\beta X\) for some \(\alpha\) and some \(\beta>0\)), as in the bivariate normal distribution, it equals the Pearson correlation (Serfling and Xiao, 2007, Proposition 3), but it requires only finite first moments. Replacing _X_ by _F<sub>X</sub>(X_) in \(\tau_2^{[XY]}\) gives 12Cov{_F<sub>X</sub>(X),F<sub>Y</sub>(Y_)}, which is Spearman’s rank correlation. Each L-comoment carries the unit of its first variable, so it is divided by the L-scale of that variable. At order two, these coefficients are related to directional Gini correlations, and several symmetric Gini constructions have also been studied (Sang et al., 2016; Vanderford et al., 2020). In the rest of this paper we restrict attention to _m_=2,3, which are the orders most frequently used in applications. The fourth L-comoment is included above for completeness; Remark 4.7 and Section 7 discuss the extension of our results to order four and beyond.
