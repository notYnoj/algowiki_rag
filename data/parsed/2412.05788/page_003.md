## Diffusion Posterior Samplers.

Suppose we wish to solve the noisy inverse problem **y**=A(**x**<sub>0</sub>)+**n** with a diffusion (model) prior on **x**<sub>0</sub>. To frame the posterior as our new target, we can modify the reverse process, so it is instead steered by the conditional score \(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t}\mathrm{log}q(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{y})\). This quantity can be decomposed into a sum of two terms using the Bayes’ rule as (Chung et al., 2023; Song et al., 2023; Boys et al., 2024)

\(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t}\mathrm{log}q(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{y})=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t}\mathrm{log}q(\mathbf{x}_t)+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t}\mathrm{log}g(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_t),\) (2)

where \(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t}\mathrm{log}q(\mathbf{x}_t)\) is score of the prior (unconditional model) and \(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t}\mathrm{log}g(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_t)\) is called the _guidance term_. However, the guidance term is typically intractable, as it involves a direct comparison between a clean measurement and a noisy latent variable. Existing works bridge this gap through approximations that essentially noise the measurement or denoise the latent variable.

Song et al. (2020) define a measurement noising process \(\{\mathbf{y}_t\}_{t=0}^T\) that parallels the latent’s forward diffusion process, where **y**<sub>0</sub>=**y** represents the clean measurement and \(\nu(\mathbf{y}_t|\mathbf{y}_0)\) gives the noisy measurement distribution at time _t_. They subsequently project the latent variables towards or orthogonally onto the constraint manifold {**x**_<sub>t</sub>_:A(**x**_<sub>t</sub>_)=**y**_<sub>t</sub>_} to maintain sample consistency with the measurement throughout the entire denoising process. They make the approximation

\(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t}\mathrm{log}q(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{y})=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t}\mathrm{log}\int q(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{y}_t,\mathbf{y})\nu(\mathbf{y}_t|\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}_t\approx\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t}\mathrm{log}\int q(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{y}_t)\nu(\mathbf{y}_t|\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}_t\)<br>
\(\approx\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t}\mathrm{log}q(\mathbf{x}_t|\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t)=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t}\mathrm{log}q(\mathbf{x}_t)+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t}\mathrm{log}g(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t|\mathbf{x}_t),\) (3)

where it is assumed _q_(**x**_<sub>t</sub>_|**y**_<sub>t</sub>,_**y**)≈_q_(**x**_<sub>t</sub>_|**y**_<sub>t</sub>_) and \(\nu(\mathbf{y}_t|\mathbf{y})\approx\delta_{\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t}\) for \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t\sim\nu(\mathbf{y}_t|\mathbf{y})\). This measurement process is available under a linear structure to the inverse problem (see App. A.2). When \(\mathcal{A}(\cdot)\) is a masking transformation, e.g. in image in-painting problems, the sequence \(\{\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t\}_{t=1}^T\) is effectively constructed by forward diffusing the measurement **y**. Here, the guidance term’s contribution is equivalent to replacing or interpolating the masked segment of **x**_<sub>t</sub>_ with \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t\) (Song et al., 2021). This is known as the _replacement method_ (Trippe et al., 2022; Ho et al., 2022). More generally, we will refer to approaches that rely on the score estimate in eq. (3) as replacement methods in our experiments.

The replacement method becomes unsuitable, however, when the measurement process is intractable, as with non-linear inverse problems. Replacement in the linear masking case has also been empirically shown to lose coherence between the masked and unmasked regions (Ho et al., 2022). Alternatively, Chung et al. (2023) propose the diffusion posterior sampling (DPS) method, using the approximation

\(g(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_t)=\int g(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_0)q(\mathbf{x}_0|\mathbf{x}_t)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_0\approx g(\mathbf{y}|\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0(\mathbf{x}_t,t)),\) (4)

where it is assumed \(q(\mathbf{x}_0|\mathbf{x}_t)\approx\delta_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0(\mathbf{x}_t,t)}\), and \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0(\mathbf{x}_t,t)=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_0|\mathbf{x}_t]\) is the reconstructed (clean) sample. This leads to the score estimate

\(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t}\mathrm{log}q(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{y})\approx\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t}\mathrm{log}q(\mathbf{x}_t)+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t}\mathrm{log}g(\mathbf{y}|\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0(\mathbf{x}_t,t)),\)

where \(g(\mathbf{y}|\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0(\mathbf{x}_t,t))=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y};\mathcal{A}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0(\mathbf{x}_t,t)),\tilde{\sigma}_t^2\mathbf{I})\), whose logarithm’s gradient is computed via backpropagation. We refer to this method as _reconstruction guidance_.

The above methods rely on single-sample approximations. One can instead devise Gaussian approximations for _q_(**x**<sub>0</sub>|**x**_<sub>t</sub>_) (Song et al., 2023; Boys et al., 2024), but this too can be a strong assumption. Taking a different approach, one can adopt sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) algorithms (Del Moral et al., 2006) to correct such errors, given that it permits asymptotically exact posterior sampling with added liberties in the algorithm’s construction, namely the proposal and intermediate target choices. SMC methods are often used in the context of state space models (SSMs), with their Markovian structure and sequential measurements. A diffusion model’s reverse process naturally fits this framework, with the exception of measurements taken at intermediate steps. After all, the inverse problem is only defined on samples in the final target. A workaround is to adopt probabilistic models with a single terminal measurement **y**<sub>0</sub> or artificially construct a sequence of measurements \(\{\mathbf{y}_t\}_{t=1}^T\). However, the former produces low likelihood samples if filtering is only done at the last step, and the latter imposes a realisation of measurements that may not be easily satisfied. We discuss two ways adopted by SMC methods to mitigate these issues.
