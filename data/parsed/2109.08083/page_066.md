For a random variable _X(i_) we say that _X(i_) _becomes bad at step i_ if it deviates outside of the error bounds we defined above. We define the stopping time _T_ to be the earliest time _i_ such that either any of the primary and secondary variables we are tracking become bad, or \(i=(1-n^{-\alpha_\mathrm{gr}})n\), whichever occurs first. In order to show that with high probability \(T=(1-n^{-\alpha_\mathrm{gr}})n\), which would prove Theorem 3.28, it is convenient to consider shifted variables. In particular we let

We illustrate the idea of the critical interval method by discussing it with regards to the vertex degrees. The idea is that we only need to worry about a variable when it is close to either end of the interval listed above. For \(d_v^-(i)\) we refer to \[_np_<sup>3</sup>-_e<sub>d</sub>,np_<sup>3</sup>-_e<sub>d</sub>+f<sub>d</sub>_\] as its (lower) critical interval, where _f<sub>d</sub>=b_<sup>2/3</sup>_n_ (and for \(d_v^+(i)\) we refer to \[_np_<sup>3</sup>+_e<sub>d</sub>-f<sub>d</sub>,np_<sup>3</sup>+_e<sub>d</sub>_\] as its (upper) critical interval). Now suppose that \(d_v^-(i)\) first enters this critical interval at step _s_<sub>1_,v_</sub>_\<T_. That is, \(d_v^-(i)>np^3-e_d+f_d\) for all \(i\le s_{1,v}-1\) and \(d_v^-(s_{1,v})\le np^3-e_d+f_d\). Then we define \(T_{s_{1,v}}^-\) to be the first time _t\>s_<sub>1_,v_</sub> such that \(d_v^-(t)\) leaves the critical interval again. There are two possibilities for how it leaves - we could have \(d_v^-(T_{s_{1,v}}^-)<np^3-e_d\) or we could have \(d_v^-(T_{s_{1,v}}^-)>np^3-e_d+f_d\). In the first case we get that \(T_{s_{1,v}}^-=T\) is the final stopping time since _d<sub>v</sub>_ has become bad. We will, however, show that with high probability we are always in the second case whenever \(s_{1,v}<t\le(1-n^{-\alpha_\mathrm{gr}})n\), exploiting the self-correcting nature of the process. In fact, for \(j\ge 2\) and each _v_ we also define _s<sub>j,v</sub>_ be the first time \(t>T_{s_{j-1,v}}^-\) such that \(d_v^-(t)\) is in the lower critical interval and \(T_{s_{j,v}}^-\) to be the first time _t\>s<sub>j,v</sub>_ such that \(d_v^-(t)\) leaves the critical interval again. This is defined for all _j_ such that _s<sub>j,v</sub>\<T_. Then we show that for all _j_ such that _s<sub>j,v</sub>_ is defined, with high probability we are always in the second case. Since the one step change is always sufficiently small, we have that the second case always takes us into \[_np_<sup>3</sup>-_e<sub>d</sub>+f<sub>d</sub>,np_<sup>3</sup>+_e<sub>d</sub>-f<sub>d</sub>_\], i.e. we can not jump from one critical interval to the other (nor beyond it) in a single step of the algorithm.

In order to show that this deviation back towards the trajectory occurs when we hit a critical interval we use martingale concentration inequalities. To do this, we first show that the random variables _X_<sup>+</sup> are supermartingales and _X_<sup>-</sup> are submartingales. In fact, the nature of the critical interval method means that we actually show that subintervals of the process, starting from each time we enter the upper or lower critical interval to the first time they leave again, are super- or submartingales respectively. Formally, for a random variable _X_, writing \(\mathbb{E}'\) to denote conditional expectation with respect to the natural filtration, _X_ is a _supermartingale_ if \(\mathbb{E}'(X(i+1))\le X(i)\) and a _submartingale_ if \(\mathbb{E}'(X(i+1))\ge X(i)\) for all _i_ over which we are tracking the variable.
