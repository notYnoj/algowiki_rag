Broadly speaking, we refer to these as ‘degree-type conditions’.

Throughout the process, for a given graph \(G\subseteq\mathcal{T}\) , we will be interested in pairs (_v,S_) such that \(v\in V(G)\) and \(S\subseteq V(\mathcal{T})\), and the number of edges which contain _v_ and relate to _S_ in _G_ in some way. More specifically, we shall often have pairs (_v,S_) with _S_ of the form _I<sub>t<sub>i</sub></sub>_ or _I<sub>t<sub>i</sub></sub>_\\_I<sub>t<sub>j</sub></sub>_, and it may be that we are interested both in the number of edges that contain _v_ and _only_ other vertices in _S_, and also the number of edges that contain _v_ and _at least one_ vertex from _S_. To ensure that we have a notation that encompasses both of these scenarios (without having to change _S_), we shall first describe a more general notation, though most of the cases given by this setting will then also have a more concise shorthand notation.

Let \(G\subseteq\mathcal{T}\) and _w_ be a weight function defined on _E(G_). For sets \(S_1,S_2,S_3\subseteq V(\mathcal{T})\) not necessarily distinct, we define

\(E_G(v,S_1,S_2,S_3):=\{e\unknown v:e=(v,e_1,e_2,e_3)\in G,e_i\in S_i\},\)

When _S_<sub>1</sub>=_S_<sub>2</sub>=_S_<sub>3</sub>=_S_, we also write _E<sub>G</sub>(v,S_) as a shorthand notation.
