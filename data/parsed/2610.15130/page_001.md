# Recall-Bounded Staleness for Filtered Multi-Tenant ANN Graph Indexes under Concurrent Updates

**_Abstract_--Production vector search must continuously ingest, delete, and re-tenant vectors while serving low-latency, high- recall, metadata-filtered approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) queries for many tenants with per-tenant service-level objectives. Existing graph-based ANN systems address these requirements largely in isolation, leaving recall during concurrent re- optimization without an analytical bound. We develop a graph- based index that combines streaming updates, filtered search, and multi-tenancy, with one theoretical result and two system mechanisms. Our theory decomposes per-tenant recall loss into staleness from updates not yet integrated into the graph and filtered navigability determined by the local density of points satisfying a tenant’s predicate. The staleness component exhibits anti-amortization on a merge-based graph, integrating a larger batch of inserts increases recall degradation super-linearly, so larger merges do not reduce total staleness loss. Holding the insert horizon fixed while increasing merge size \(20\times\), we observe a monotonic decrease in terminal recall@10 from 99.44% to 99.01%. For filtered search, the theory gives a parameter-free frontier-mass relationship in local predicate density and a query- averaged per-tenant recall bound. We instantiate the bound with a filter and tenant-aware staging cache and a select-not-resize scheduler with per-tenant staleness ceilings. On a FreshDiskANN substrate, the ceiling eliminates tight-tier SLA violations under the evaluated churn workload, while merge reordering changes terminal recall by only 0.02 percentage points. The results show how recall bounds can guide background maintenance without relying on merge resizing as a recall control mechanism.**

**_Index Terms_--Approximate nearest neighbor search, vector databases, graph indexes, streaming indexes, filtered search, multi-tenancy, recall, service-level objectives, index maintenance, concurrent updates.**

### I. INTRODUCTION

A WIDELY-HELD intuition about maintaining a graph- based approximate-nearest-neighbor (ANN) index under a stream of inserts is that batching amortizes cost, since folding more pending points into each merge lowers per-point maintenance overhead. This holds for _throughput_; for _recall_ it does not. Integrating a batch of _c_ inserts into a merge-based graph index degrades disk-graph recall@_k super-linearly_ in _c_, so the total recall loss accumulated over an insertion horizon does not fall with the merge size. Larger, less frequent merges are no better for recall than smaller, more frequent ones, and on our point estimates they are worse. We call this _anti- amortization_. In practice only the weak form matters, since re-sizing merges cannot engineer degradation away and can only _re-prioritize_ it, and this motivates the system we build. The design rests on the _direction_ of the effect (_L_<sup>∗</sup> non- decreasing in _c_), which we measure on two independent axes; the magnitude of the super-linearity is less tightly pinned, and

its per-merge-to-horizon assembly is a conservative step we find loose by about \(2\times\) (§V-B).

Anti-amortization matters because the workloads that most need graph ANN are exactly the ones that mutate continuously. Production vector search must ingest, delete, and re-tenant vectors while serving low-latency, high-recall queries that carry metadata predicates, for many tenants each with its own service-level objective (SLO). Recent work names the pain directly. CANDOR-Bench [1] observes that under concurrency "throughput in concurrent settings is often constrained by insertion overhead rather than query latency, which highlights a mismatch between streaming workloads and designs originally tuned for offline construction." The VDBMS Survey [2] places incremental _k_-NN among its open problems: e-commerce and recommender platforms retrieve an effectively very large _k_ in small increments so that results appear seamlessly, and while techniques exist for that access pattern, "it is unclear how to support this search inside vector indexes." A VLDB tutorial on filtered vector search [3] makes the corresponding point on the predicate axis, giving as a key objective "the importance of achieving stable recall, ideally in a declarative manner, ensuring consistent recall across queries." And, from industry, Pinecone [4] reports that "data is continuously inserted and deleted, and metadata may be updated independently of the vector index, yet filters must be applied accurately and efficiently at query time."

The literature is heavily worked along each axis _separately_ but not at their intersection. Filtered-static graph ANN (Filtered-DiskANN, ACORN, RNSG, iRangeGraph, STABLE) builds one index over the joint feature-and- attribute space and does not stream. Streaming graph ANN (FreshDiskANN, SPFresh, Mycelium-Index, and the recent single-tenant SOTA PipeANN/OdinANN) operates on unfiltered, single-tenant data and provides at best _empirical_ recall stability under churn. Multi-tenant vector search (Curator, Pinecone, GaussDB-Vector) is sparse; Curator is static and in- memory, Pinecone sits on an IVF substrate with no analytical bound, and none schedules background work by per-tenant SLA. No prior system occupies their intersection, and none bounds the recall a tenant loses during live re-optimization of a filtered graph index.

Our contribution occupies the axes those systems leave open, combining streaming, filtered, and multi-tenant operation with per-tenant SLA scheduling, an analytical bound, and disk-resident scale.
