## Why does the WS layer only match the tuned watershed at full data?

Because, given the same markers and the same foreground, the two compute the same partition ( 5), and the remaining errors are in marker detection, which both share: with ground-truth markers both reach _F_<sub>1</sub> about 0.87 ( 3). Every advantage the WS layer showed during the study was matched once the baseline received the same supervision: the edge target (Sect. 4.3, last rows of  2) and the distance head (Sect. 4.4). Changing the marker head does not change this: with a CenterNet-style centre-and-radius detector the WS layer stays at _F_<sub>1</sub> 0.795 and the baseline drops to 0.774 (record section "A detection head for the markers").

## Why did the oracle with true markers fall from 0.96 to 0.87?

Because of the hedging. Trained from ground-truth markers, the costs could trust every marker and the marker attachment overrode the foreground (oracle 0.959). Trained from the model’s own markers, the solve learns to veto a marker where the foreground is weak and to share contested pixels; with ground-truth markers it then drops 11% of them, and its oracle (0.872) falls to the watershed’s (0.867). The 0.17 _F_<sub>1</sub> between the earlier layer and its oracle was therefore a property of training on perfect markers, not headroom (record section "Training the solve from the model’s own markers"). The same hedging explains the layer’s PQ deficit (softer boundaries at equal detection, SQ 0.816 against 0.824) and its loss at the exact limit, which removes the hedging.

## Why can the marker selection not be learned through the layer?

Because the partition loss reaches a marker only through the pixels it claims. A spurious marker where the background is clearly stronger claims no pixels at any strength, so its strength receives no gradient (measured at 10<sup>-11</sup>) and is never pushed down; a true marker whose region spills over the nucleus rim loses rim pixels, so its strength is pushed down.
