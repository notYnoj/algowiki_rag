## Q Check of our Power Watershed limit against the authors’ reference code

Our exact limit (`power\_watershed` in `pw\_conditioning.py`, `power\_watershed\_ multi` in `pw\_layer.py`: a NumPy/SciPy re-implementation of Algorithm 1 of Couprie, Grady, Najman and Talbot, PAMI 2011) had only been validated against finite-p Random Walker solutions and against itself. It was checked against the authors' C/C++ implementation, `PW\_1.0.1` from `https://sourceforge.net/ projects/powerwatershed` (Camille Couprie, Sept 2010; PINK + CSparse), on the same problems. Harness and build notes: `experiments/pwref/` (README), records: `results/pwref/`.

**What the reference program does. powerwatsegm -a 2 -i image.pgm -s seeds.pgm** (or `-m` for a multi-label seed image, values 1..K) builds the 4- connected grid, weights w = 255-|I\_u-I\_v| (8-bit grey images; colour images use the maximum over channels), then _reconstructs_ the weights geodesically from the seeds (`grey\_weights\_PW`: the dilation of the seed-adjacent weights under the weights in the line graph, i.e. every edge's weight is lowered to the bottleneck of the best path that connects it to a seed), and runs `PowerWatershed\_q2` with the reconstructed weights for the plateau ordering and the original weights inside a plateau. A plateau is the grid-connected set of edges of equal reconstructed weight around the current maximum; inside it the edges whose original weight is higher are merged first (Kruskal, never two known nodes), and the unit-weight Random Walker is solved by a CSparse LU on the edges at the level, between merged nodes, with the seeds and the previously solved nodes as boundary. Potentials are stored in single precision. It writes `mask.pgm` (label of the largest potential; at exactly 0.5 the first label, the foreground), and `proba.pgm` = 255-255\(\cdot\)P(label 1) truncated to a byte. With `-g 1` the reconstructed weights replace the weights altogether (a different problem).

**Why the reconstruction is the algorithm, not a heuristic.** The plateau of Algorithm 1 lives in the merged graph: two equal-weight edges that touch the same merged node belong to one plateau even when they share no pixel. A breadth-first search over grid-adjacent edges of equal _raw_ weight splits such a plateau into pieces that are then solved one after the other, and the result depends on the order in which equal weights are sorted. The reconstruction lowers every interior edge of a region that is cut off from the seeds above level t to exactly t, so the reconstructed plateau at level t is grid-connected through those interior edges and is the merged-graph plateau (split at most at known nodes, which does not couple anything); the interior edges are then merged first, and the solve runs on the level-t edges between merged nodes. That is what our code does directly with a union-find over merged nodes. Both readings were verified (`check\_recon.py` reproduces the reconstructed weights of the dump exactly on 30 cases; `order\_dependence.py` runs the reference under three seeds of its unseeded `rand()`).
