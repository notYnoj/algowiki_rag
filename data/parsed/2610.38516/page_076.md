jacobian); ours takes 0.40-0.84 s for the scalar code and 0.43-1.0 s for the K- vector code, on at most 256 cost levels. Our general path per level (union-find compression, a sparse graph and its connected components over the whole image) is what costs; a C implementation of the same plateau-by-plateau scheme is an order of magnitude faster, as expected.

**What it changes.** Nothing in the code or the results: no bug was found, and every "limit" row of this report (sections 1-3, the PW-limit evaluations of sections 6-11, the K-label limits of section 11) stands as the exact p \(\rightarrow\infty\) limit as the authors compute it. The one convention to keep in mind when reading those rows is the tie at exactly 0.5, which our label maps resolve to the background and the reference to the first label; on the 8-bit cases above it touches at most 0.3 % of the pixels, and in the learned-cost experiments, where the costs are continuous, it cannot occur outside symmetric plateaus.

## Q.1 A Numba back end for the limit, bit-for-bit

The profile of the NumPy limit code explains the \(10\times\) gap to the C reference: the plateau solves are negligible (a few milliseconds of the 0.6 s on camera at 512<sup>2</sup>); on 8-bit costs the time is the whole-image passes repeated at each of the ~180 cost levels (union-find compression, a sparse graph and its connected components, `np.unique`), and on continuous learned costs quantised to 2<sup>-20</sup> (246 000 plateaus on a 256<sup>2</sup> graph) it is the Python edge loop of the scalar union-find. `pw\_fast.py` compiles exactly that bookkeeping with Numba (one sorted-edge pass, path-compressed union-find over merged nodes, the components of each level in the merged graph, the merges of the unambiguous components) and hands the ambiguous plateaus back to the same NumPy/SciPy solve code as before: the same dense solve per component on levels of at most 64 edges, the same single block LU on larger levels, the same unknown ordering, the same accumulation order of the right-hand sides and the same representatives of merged components. The results are therefore identical to the bit, ties at exactly 0.5 included, which `experiments/pwref/validate\_fast.py` checks with `np.array\_equal`: 400 random coarse 24<sup>2</sup> cases (two labels and 3-5 labels, with the small-plateau path on and off), the ten 512<sup>2</sup> cases of section 1 (scalar and as a two-label K-vector solve), and the learned-cost regime on the label-augmented 256<sup>2</sup> graph at three quantisations (scalar, and 40 markers): 0 mismatches on the Mac (Python 3.14, Numba 0.68) and on jacobian (3.12), the reference-harness records of section 12 are unchanged under the new back end, and the self- tests of `pw\_layer.py` print the same limit lines under both. `power\_watershed` and `power\_watershed\_multi` pick the back end automatically (`backend=None`: Numba when importable, else NumPy; `PW\_BACKEND=numpy|numba` overrides), so `pw\_limit\_numpy` and `pw\_limit\_multi\_numpy` and every evaluation script get it without change; the first call per process compiles for about 1.5 s and the compiled code is cached on disk.
