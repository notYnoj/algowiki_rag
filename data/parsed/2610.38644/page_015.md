<tbc>and 50,000 Adam iterations, whereas the second-order problem requires a specialized sequential "learning-outwards" schedule extending to approximately \(5\times 10^5\) iterations \[23\].

CFKS does not employ gradient-based training or an explicit finite feature realization. The three problems are solved through deterministic population-kernel assembly followed by the null-space and reduced SVD solves. The measured total assembly-and-solve times are approximately 2.15, 3.30, and 2.55 s for the first-order, second-order, and multiscale problems, respectively. The corresponding solution RMSEs are \(1.70\times 10^{-6}\), \(9.96\times 10^{-5}\), and \(2.24\times 10^{-5}\).  3 summarizes the comparison.

The comparison highlights a different computational route to high-frequency resolution. FBPINNs address spectral bias by decomposing the domain and training multiple local neural networks, whereas CFKS works directly with the deterministic population kernel associated with the Gaussian feature family. Consequently, the three ODEs require no backpropagation, neural architecture selection, or finite-center sampling. The present timings should not be interpreted as a direct hardware-normalized speed comparison with the results of \[23\]; rather, they show that the population-kernel formulation attains comparable high-frequency resolution through a small number of dense linear-algebra operations.

## 4.3 Poisson and biharmonic problems on the five-petal domain

Figure 2 summarizes the CFKS results for the Poisson and biharmonic problems on the irregular five-petal domain. The top row corresponds to the second-order Poisson problem and the bottom row to the fourth-order biharmonic problem.

For the Poisson case, CFKS reproduces the manufactured solution over the entire nonconvex domain with an independent solution RMSE of \(4.32\times 10^{-4}\) and a maximum absolute error of \(2.37\times 10^{-3}\). The maximum boundary error is \(2.23\times 10^{-5}\), confirming that the Dirichlet constraint is maintained accurately by the null-space formulation. The independent PDE residual has RMS \(7.32\times 10^{-1}\) and maximum magnitude 8.79. As seen in the top row of Fig. 2, the largest solution errors remain localized within the interior rather than being distributed uniformly over the domain.

The biharmonic problem provides a more demanding test because it requires fourth-order population-kernel derivatives together with simultaneous Dirichlet and normal-derivative boundary conditions. CFKS achieves an independent solution RMSE of \(1.71\times 10^{-3}\) and a maximum absolute error of \(6.51\times 10^{-3}\). The maximum training boundary error is \(1.21\times 10^{-5}\), while the dense Dirichlet-boundary error is \(1.05\times 10^{-6}\). The biharmonic residual has RMS \(2.83\times 10^3\) and maximum magnitude \(3.09\times 10^4\). Although the absolute residual is larger than in the Poisson case, this reflects the substantially larger scale introduced by repeated differentiation in the fourth-order operator; the solution itself remains accurate throughout the domain. The total CFKS solve time is 0.736 s, comprising approximately 0.663 s for kernel/operator assembly, 0.046 s for the null-space construction, and 0.020 s for the reduced SVD solve. The dominant computational cost is therefore the assembly of the high-order operator-kernel matrices rather than the constrained linear solve.

The two cases therefore show that the same CFKS construction extends from second- to fourth-order operators on irregular geometries without altering the underlying population-kernel representation. They also demonstrate that the center-support geometry need not coincide with the physical domain: the nonconvex boundary enters through the physical constraint functionals, while the population kernel is defined on the surrounding embedding region.
