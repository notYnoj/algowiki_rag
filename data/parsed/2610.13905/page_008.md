\(\mathcal{M}_{\langle m_0,k_0,n_0\rangle}=\sum_{j=1}^tU_j\otimes V_j\otimes W_j,\) (1.8)

where \(U_j\in\mathbb{C}^{m_0\times k_0}\), \(V_j\in\mathbb{C}^{k_0\times n_0}\), and \(W_j^T\in\mathbb{C}^{m_0\times n_0}\) are matricized copies of the columns of _U_, _V_ , and _W_ , respectively. Importantly, this identity is invariant under _De-Groote transformations_ \[18\], in which _U<sub>j</sub>_, _V<sub>j</sub>_, and _W<sub>j</sub>_ are replaced by _R_<sup>-1</sup>_U<sub>j</sub>S_, _S_<sup>-1</sup>_V<sub>j</sub>T_ and _T_<sup>-1</sup>_W<sub>j</sub>R_ for any set of basis transformations _R_, _S_, and _T_.

Note that the potential practical efficiency of an \(\langle m_0,k_0,n_0;t\rangle\) matrix multiplication algorithm can be gleaned from properties of its encoding matrices. For example, a _real_ algorithm, whose encoding matrices are all real valued, in practice, is likely faster than a _complex_ one (of comparable sparsity), which requires complex arithmetic even to multiply real matrices. Similarly, an algorithm with sparse encoding matrices will likely be faster in practice - hence the reference above to "sparsifying" an APA algorithm, which really refers to sparsifying its encoding matrices (see \[7\]).

Throughout, we use \(\mathbb{C}(\varepsilon)\), \(\mathbb{C}[\varepsilon]\) and \(\mathbb{C}[\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{-1}]\), to denote, respectively, the field of rational functions, the ring of polynomials, and the ring of Laurent polynomials in the variable \(\varepsilon\) with complex coefficients. We also use Matlab notation when convenient, e.g., _A_(:_,i_) and _A(i,_:) refer to a column/row of the matrix _A_. This extends to arbitrary index sets _I_. That is, _A_(:_,I_) collects the columns of _A_ with indices \(i\in I\).

**Remark 1.1.** In this paper, we assume that the remainder of an APA algorithm, meaning the difference between its output and the true product, takes the form \(\varepsilon R_1+\cdots+\varepsilon^dR_d\), where _R<sub>i</sub>_ is assumed to be not identically zero for all \(1\le i\le d\). This does not necessarily hold for all APA algorithms, which may have remainders that skip terms (see  1). This is relevant for implementation purposes since, in practice, the number of evaluations necessary to recover the exact product _AB_ from an APA algorithm is not determined by the largest exponent in its remainder but rather by the number of nonzero terms it contains. The catch here is that the number of terms in the remainder for a recursive implementation of a \(\langle m_0,k_0,n_0\rangle\) APA algorithm (that performs _L_ recursive steps) is _not_ necessarily _L_ times the number of terms in the base- case algorithm, and in fact in most cases it grows to _dL_ as _L_ increases. For this reason we stick with \(\varepsilon R_1+\cdots+\varepsilon^dR_d\) in our analysis.

## 2 A New Search Algorithm for APA Schemes

We start by presenting a new algorithm for deriving APA schemes. As mentioned above, existing methods for generating APA algorithms, e.g., those of Smirnov and Bini, take (approximate) floating-point decompositions of the matrix multiplication tensor and convert them into symbolic ones. Our approach flips this idea; we start from an exact algorithm and produce an APA one by applying a symbolic De-Groote transformation and subsequently deleting terms that are \(O(\varepsilon)\). That is, if \(\sum_{j=1}^rU_j\otimes V_j\otimes W_j\) is a decomposition of the matrix multiplication tensor \(\mathcal{M}_{\langle m,k,n\rangle}\) for \(U_j\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times k}\), \(V_j\in\mathbb{C}^{k\times n}\) and \(W_j\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}\) (recall Figure 2), then

\(\mathcal{M}_{\langle m,k,n\rangle}=\sum_{j=1}^rR^{-1}U_jS\otimes S^{-1}V_jT\otimes T^{-1}W_jR\) (2.1)

for any invertible _R_, _S_, and _T_. If additionally _R_, _S_, and _T_ have entries in \(\mathbb{C}(\varepsilon)\), we obtain a symbolic decomposition that is exact for any value of \(\varepsilon\). Deleting the terms for which every nonzero entry of \(R^{-1}U_jS\otimes S^{-1}V_jT\otimes T^{-1}W_jR\) is divisible by a positive power of \(\varepsilon\) yields an APA algorithm (possibly of lower rank) that can be made arbitrarily accurate as \(\varepsilon\rightarrow 0\).

The difficulty of this approach lies in finding an appropriate exact algorithm to use as a starting point (as we will see, not just any one will do). One way of doing this is by using recent work on generating exact decompositions \[39, 50\], which allows us to find exact algorithms satisfying the necessary conditions to produce lower rank APA algorithms. In this section, we provide an overview of this approach, including pseudocode and a motivating example, and discuss the results of our search.
