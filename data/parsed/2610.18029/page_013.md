Commonly, this process is just used to find the ground state energy of the Hamiltonian, but for us, we are interested in the states that give this lowest energy. Therefore, we hope to find all of the MWCs by running shots and keeping track of the states that produce these lowest energies. An important question of this approach is how many shots are required to find all of these cliques? In the case that any MWC is equally likely, this is exactly the well-known _Coupon Collection Problem_, where a coupon collector is wanting to collect all _N_ coupons by drawing with replacement. The expected number of trials for this problem is \(\Theta(NlogN)\).

## 4.1 Postprocessing

In this approach, we no longer strictly enforce the edge constraints. Therefore, in sampling low energy solutions, while we hope to mostly obtain ground states that correspond to the MWCs, we may still get results that are not optimal. They may contain edges of the complement graph, or simply just not be of maximum weight.

For the former, this is easy to check: simply construct the subgraph represented by the measured bit-string, and check for any edges. Therefore, we have an efficient path towards retrieving at least a feasible solution. In the latter scenario, given a feasible solution that is not optimal, a natural question to ask is whether we can efficiently reach an optimal solution from it. The answer is no, as a sub-optimal solution need not be contained with in an optimal one, and hence does not necessarily give a natural path to one.

One can consider an outputted result as a graph in its own right, albeit a graph that one would hope is closer to an optimal solution than the original input graph. Another (classical) method that can take a graph and output a more optimal solution is the conventional greedy algorithm for the clique problem. Recall that the algorithm works by removing vertices of the lowest degree (i.e. missing edges to the most vertices) until the remaining set of vertices has full connectivity. This algorithm is guaranteed to run in polynomial time returns a feasible solution (which is unfortunately is not guaranteed to be optimal, but is at least maximal). We can combine these two methods and consider the classical greedy algorithm as a postprocessing method, in order to ensure that the subgraphs that we consider are at least cliques, satisfying all edge constraints. Furthermore, given a clique, we can check for any additional vertices connected to all the vertices in the clique, and hence the union of the two is still a clique. Repeating this until we no longer add any more vertices gives us a maximal clique, that is, a clique that is not contained within a larger one.

By doing these two steps to all results from our quantum sampling-based algorithms, we can at least insist the subgraph we consider is a maximal clique, a much smaller set of graphs (there are at most 3<sup>_n/_3</sup> of them) than arbitrary subgraphs of the input graph.

A crucial step within the QAOA algorithm is the classical optimisation loop to find optimal parameter values based on the energy of each set of samples. However, as we have established in the section above, changing the postprocessing step, which enforces the edge constraints has a dramatic effect on our solutions, and their energies. Therefore, it would be remiss of us not to include this efficient classical postprocessing within the classical optimisation loop, in order for it to optimise over the true figure of merit, rather than some intermediate value.
