Therefore, we must infer a "best guess" from the information that is available to us [22]. Since genomes of organisms are directly impacted by evolution, genome sampling provides the most informative data about evolutionary history. Advances in high-throughput sequencing [16] have led to a remarkable growth in the availability of biological data, emphasising the necessity for highly performant algorithms across the wider field, including for phylogeny. When generating phylogenetic trees one needs to pick a model which guides the choice of a metric that represents the way evolution occurs. Then, the desired output is simply the trees that are optimal in that metric.

Quantum optimisation methods provide alternative distributions over candidate solutions to hard combinatorial problems. In the present work, we use these methods as components of a modular solver rather than as a replacement for the complete classical pipeline. Our aim is to determine whether graph reduction, exact reconstruction and sampling can be combined usefully for the maximum weight clique subproblem arising in maximum compatibility phylogeny.

We focus on the exact maximum compatibility algorithm of Cherry [5]. A central subproblem in this algorithm is the enumeration of maximum weight cliques in a compatibility graph. This differs from the conventional optimisation task in an important way: finding one maximum weight clique is not sufficient when the downstream procedure uses a collection of equally optimal solutions to construct a strict consensus tree.

Maximum compatibility is less widely used than criteria such as maximum parsimony or maximum likelihood, but it remains relevant when some characters are not consistent with a single tree. This can occur, for example, in the presence of homoplasy or convergent mutations. Cherry’s implementation is also used in pathogen phylogeny applications [5, 19]. We therefore use maximum compatibility as a concrete biological setting in which the diversity of optimal combinatorial solutions has a direct downstream role.

In particular, one scenario where maximum compatibility can be useful is in the presence of mutations that have been acquired through convergent evolution, such as the acquisition of antimicrobial resistance mutations following the release of a new drug. These mutations do not naturally fit within a phylogenetic tree, but Cherry has shown a small percentage of such mutations is harmful to Maximum Parsimony methods and we also validated this is true for the Maximum Likelihood implementation in _iqtree3_ [23]

Even the simpler problem of finding a single maximum weight clique is NP-hard and has strong worst-case inapproximability results [12, 11]. However, this worst-case complexity does not by itself describe the behaviour of the graphs produced by the phylogenetic pipeline. We therefore evaluate exact and heuristic solvers on graphs derived from SARS- CoV-2 sequence data, and separately examine their ability to find one optimum and to recover distinct optima.

Our main contributions are as follows: In section 3, we compare exact and heuristic clique solvers on graphs produced by the maximum compatibility pipeline. The results show a clear distinction between finding one optimum and obtaining broad coverage of the optimum set.
