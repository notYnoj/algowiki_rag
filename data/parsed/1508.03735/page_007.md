## 3.1 A Variant of the Index Function Problem

Before we present our lower bound, we introduce a variant of the _random index function_ problem \[KNR99\], which will be useful for our proof.

### MULTIPLE-INDEX

There are two players Alice and Bob. Alice receives as input a sequence of _t_ pairs, \(I=\langle(S_i,u_i):i=1,2,\dots,t\rangle\), where the _S<sub>i</sub>_ are disjoint sets each with _k_ elements, and _u<sub>i</sub>_ is uniformly distributed in _S<sub>i</sub>_. Based on her input Alice sends Bob a message _M(I_). Bob then receives (_S<sub>j</sub>,j_), where _j_ is chosen from \[_t_\]. Bob must determine _u<sub>j</sub>_; let his output be \(B(S_j,j,M(I))\in S_j\). We say that the protocol succeeds if _B(S<sub>j</sub>,j,M(I_))=_u<sub>j</sub>_. Let \(\ell(t,k,p)\) be the minimum number of bits (for the worst input) that Alice must send in order for Bob to succeed with probability at least _p_.

Note that if Bob guesses randomly, then the protocol already succeeds with probability _p_=1_/k_. The following result shows that any significant improvement over this trivial probability of success will require Alice to send Bob a long message. See appendix for a full proof.

**Lemma 1.** _For_ \(p\ge 1/k\)_, we have_ \(\ell(t,k,p)\ge(8loge)t(p-1/k)^2\).

## 3.2 Lower Bound for One-to-One Matchings

We will first focus on the lower bound on one-to-one matching and show the following.

**Theorem 1.** _Suppose the coordination protocol_ \(\Pi\) _for one-to-one matching guarantees an approximation ratio of_ \(\rho\) _in expectation. Then, the coordinator of_ \(\Pi\) _must broadcast_ \(\Omega(n/\rho^4)\) _bits on problem instances of size n (in the worst case)._

Fix the protocol \(\Pi\). We will extract a two-party communication protocol for the **MULTIPLE-INDEX** problem from \(\Pi\), and use the above lemma. As a first step for our lower bound proof, we will consider the following random graph construction process RanG.

### Random Graph Construction \(\mathsf{RanG}(\rho,n)\):

Let \(\kappa=\frac{n}{8\rho}\) and \(A=\frac{n}{16\rho^2}\). Consider the following random bipartite graph _G_ with vertex set (_V,W_) such that |_V_|=|_W_|=_n_.

• Randomly generate an ordering _w_<sub>1</sub>_,w_<sub>2</sub>_,...,w<sub>n</sub>_ of _W_ (all _n_! orderings being equally likely), and partition _W_ as \(W_1\cup W_2\) such that \(W_1=\{w_1,w_2,\dots,w_\kappa\}\), and \(W_2=\{w_{\kappa+1},w_{\kappa+2},\dots,w_n\}\).<tbc>

• Similarly, randomly generate an ordering _v_<sub>1</sub>_,v_<sub>2</sub>_,...,v<sub>n</sub>_ of _V_ , and partition _V_ into _n/A_ bocks, _B_<sub>1</sub>_,B_<sub>2</sub>_,...,B<sub>n/A</sub>_ (each with _A_ vertices), where<tbc>

<tbc>\(B_j:=\{v_i:(j-1)A+1\le i\le jA\}.\)<tbc>

• Connect _B<sub>j</sub>_ and _W_ as follows. First, we describe the connections between _V_ and _W_<sub>1</sub>. The neighbourhoods of the vertices in each _B<sub>j</sub>_ will be disjoint: we partition _W_<sub>1</sub> into equal-sized disjoint sets \((T_v:v\in B_j)\), and let the neighbours of \(v\in B_j\) be exactly the \(2\rho\) vertices in _T<sub>v</sub>_.<tbc>

• In addition, assign each vertex in _v_ one neighbor in _W_<sub>2</sub>, by connecting _V_ with _W_<sub>2</sub> in round-robin fashion -- connect vertex _v<sub>i</sub>_ to vertex _w<sub>j</sub>_, where \(j=(\kappa+imod(n-\kappa))\).

Before we prove Theorem 1, let us first observe that a graph generated by RanG always has a matching with high welfare.
