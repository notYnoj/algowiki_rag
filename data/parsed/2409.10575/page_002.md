In an SM instance of size _n_, _n_ men with _n_ women each rank all members of the opposite gender in a strict order of preference, with the goal to pair them in such a way that no man and woman would prefer each other over their current partner. Similarly, in an HR instance of size _n_, _n_ residents and _m_ hospitals with quotas strictly rank each other, aiming to make assignments that ensure no resident and hospital would prefer each other over their current assignments. In 2003, the concept was generalized further by the introduction of the stable _b_-matching problem, which extends the basic framework to accommodate multiple capacities (Fleiner 2003).

A natural variant of SM and HR incorporates ties and incomplete preference lists, allowing the agents to express indifference and exclude unacceptable members. These variants are known as the stable marriage problem with incomplete lists and ties (SMTI) (Iwama et al 1999) and the hospitals/residents problem with ties (HRT) (Irving et al 2000). Recently, the SMTI and HRT problems have received significant attention from researchers due to their relevance in diverse applications such as the child-adoption matching problem (Delorme et al 2019; Pettersson et al 2021) and the student-project allocation problem (Abraham et al 2003, 2007; Manlove and O’Malley 2008). When ties are present in the preference lists, three criteria are used to define the stability of a matching: _weak stability_, _strong stability_, and _super-stability_ (Irving 1994; Olaosebikan and Manlove 2022). Among these definitions, weak stability has been the focus of extensive research (Cao et al 2024; Delorme et al 2019; Gent and Prosser 2002; Gelain et al 2010).

Beyond achieving stability, objectives also include maximizing matching size and ensuring fairness. For a given SM or HR problem, all stable matchings have the same size (Gale and Sotomayor 1985). A stable matching can be easily found using the Gale- Shapley (GS) algorithm proposed by Gale and Shapley (1962). For the SMTI or HRT problem, a weakly stable matching can be obtained by applying the GS algorithm after breaking all ties. However, different methods of breaking ties generally result in stable matchings of varying sizes (Irving et al 2000, 2009). Consequently, a natural objective is to find a maximum cardinality weakly stable matching for the SMTI and HRT problems, referred to as MAX-SMTI and MAX-HRT, respectively. These problems have been proven to be NP-hard (Irving et al 2000; Manlove et al 2002). Equity is another criterion for matchings, especially in societal applications requiring a compromise stable matching (Romero-Medina 2005; Roth and Sotomayor 1990). A popular way to assess fairness between the two parties is the sex equality cost (Gusfield and Irving 1989). The problem of minimizing the sex equality cost in the SM problem is referred to as the sex-equal stable marriage (SESM) problem, which is NP-hard (Kato 1993). Numerous studies have investigated this problem and proposed corresponding algorithms to address it (Irving 2014; Iwama et al 2007; Viet et al 2016). However, efficiently solving large instances of MAX-SMTI and MAX-HRT remains an ongoing research topic, with limited studies striving for equity while solving the MAX-SMTI problem.
