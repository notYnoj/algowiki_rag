This is beneficial for algorithms that require fewer than _n_<sup>2</sup> queries to Max-Flow on the input graph. Second, our approach can work with any data structure for All-Pairs Max- Flow, rather than just the trivial one that outputs all _n_<sup>2</sup> values. This expands the set of potential algorithms to include those outputting data structures whose size is smaller than the output size of All-Pairs Max-Flow, which is _n_<sup>2</sup>. Moreover, this approach is robust enough to work even on directed graphs with general edge-capacities. Therefore, for subquartic algorithms it essentially settles the nondeterministic time complexity of verifying the cuts in all variants of All-Pairs Max-Flow via folklore reductions with little overhead.

Regarding flows, the _O(n_) bound naturally applies only for unit node-capacities. Are there nontrivial upper bounds in other settings? For general node-capacities, we answer this question affirmatively! Specifically, we show that for graphs with general node-capacities, even directed, _O(n_) edges are always sufficient to ship the maximum _st_-flow. This is done by identifying a certain pattern that can be eliminated from any flow while still preserving its value and without violating the flow constraints or increasing the volume <sup>17</sup>. For directed (multi-) graphs with unit _edge_-capacities, we use the upper bound of \(n\sqrt{\mathbf{value}(f_{st})}\) proved in \[KL98\] for the volume of _st_-flows _f<sub>st</sub>_, where **value**(_f<sub>st</sub>_) represents the amount of flow shipped from _s_ to _t_ in _f<sub>st</sub>_. Using these flow bounds, a nondeterministic algorithm for each variant can guess succinct maximum flows for the queried pairs whose support is bounded by the corresponding upper bounds, and then verify their feasibility and values. Finally, we believe that our results may also be relevant in other related fields.

## 1.4 Preliminaries

### General notations.

• (_Miscellaneous._) Throughout, we only consider connected graphs with \(m\ge n-1\), and we use the terms "node" and "vertex" interchangeably. Let _G_=(_V,E,c_) be a graph with capacity function _c_ defined on either its edges or nodes. For positive integers _a,b_, and _c_, we denote by \(\omega(a,b,c)\) the exponent such that \(n^{\omega(a,b,c)}\) is the time it takes to multiply an \(n^a\times n^b\) matrix by an \(n^b\times n^c\) matrix.<tbc>

• (_Flows._) For an _st_-flow _f<sub>st</sub>_ in _G_, we use **volume**(_f<sub>st</sub>_) to denote the size of the support of _f<sub>st</sub>_ in _G_, which is simply the number of edges in _G_ with nonzero flow shipped by _f<sub>st</sub>_. We use **value**(_f<sub>st</sub>_) to denote the total flow value shipped from _s_ to _t_ in _f<sub>st</sub>_, that is the (capacity of) the out-degree minus the in-degree of _s_ (equivalently, the in-degree minus the out-degree of _t_) in _f<sub>st</sub>_. We may assume without loss of generality that _f<sub>st</sub>_ is acyclic (in particular, the in-degree of _s_ and the out-degree of _t_ is 0), as removing cycles in _f<sub>st</sub>_ does not change **value**(_f<sub>st</sub>_), which we typically attempt to maximize, and can only decrease **volume**(_f<sub>st</sub>_), which we typically attempt to minimize. We denote by _V(f<sub>st</sub>_) the set of nodes in the support of _f<sub>st</sub>_, i.e., nodes with adjacent edges carrying non-zero flow. Depending on the context, we may use _f<sub>st</sub>_ to denote the support of the corresponding flow.<tbc>

• (_Cuts._) Given two node sets \(A,B\subseteq V\) (which could also be a singletons), we use _E(A,B_) to denote the set of edges between _A_ and _B_, and **value**(_A,B_) to denote the capacity of edges between _A_ and _B_, i.e., \(\mathbf{value}(A,B)=\mathbf{value}(E(A,B))=\sum_{a\in A,b\in B}c(a,b)\). An edge _uv_-cut is denoted by (_S<sub>u,v</sub>,S<sub>v,u</sub>_), and a vertex _uv_-cut by (_S<sub>u,v</sub>,C<sub>u,v</sub>,S<sub>v,u</sub>_), where _C<sub>u,v</sub>_ is called a (vertex) _uv_-separator. In these contexts, **value**(_C<sub>u,v</sub>_) is defined as the capacity of the separator, given by \(\sum_{w\in C_{u,v}}c(w)\).<tbc>

<sup>17</sup>An alternative proof uses linear programming arguments \[Che24\], as discussed later in this paper.
