AlphaTensor used deep reinforcement learning to discover matrix-multiplication algorithms that improved on Strassen’s construction for the first time in over fifty years [15]. FunSearch pairs a large language model with an automatic evaluator to search over programs, and improved the best known bounds for the cap-set problem and for online bin packing [30]. AlphaEvolve extends learning-guided discovery to broader scientific and algorithmic tasks [27]. PatternBoost alternates classical local search with a transformer trained on high-scoring candidates [11]. We build directly on PatternBoost, through its Explorer implementation [3]. In contrast to existing work, our candidates are geometric objects whose exact score is itself an optimization problem, which calls for carefully designed surrogates to guide the search. A complementary line of work settles extremal questions by complete search, most prominently SAT solving, and emits machine- checkable certificates, as in the resolution of the Boolean Pythagorean triples problem [20]. We share the insistence on independently verifiable certificates, but replace exhaustive search with learning-guided generation of geometric objects.

### 2.2 Machine learning for combinatorial optimization

This research area has received significant attention in the last decade with a large body of work. We refer the interested reader to the surveys by Bengio et al. [5], Cappart et al. [7], and Wang et al. [36]. This literature mostly learns to _solve_ instances, through learned branching, cutting, or heuristic acceleration, whereas we use learning to _generate_ extremal instances. The generation of extremal instances cannot be easily formulated as a solvable optimization problem: the objective we maximize is itself the output of an exact optimization, an LP and an ILP, so it cannot be predicted directly by a learned model. While some instance representations can be borrowed from this literature, the model here only proposes candidate instances, each of which is then scored by an exact solver, so every gap we report is an exact, independently computed value rather than a model estimate.

### 2.3 Instance hardness and adversarial instance generation

Searching for configurations that are hard for an exact objective places our task in the instance-hardness and instance-space literature. In this context, there has been work on instance-space analysis and the evolution<tbc>

<tbc>of discriminating test instances [32], principled measures of instance difficulty [31], methodologies and open challenges for understanding instance hardness [33], and the evolution of combinatorial instances that are difficult to solve [34]. Our work can be read as automated hard-instance discovery, with the distinction that every reported instance carries an exact, independently re-verifiable certificate.

### 2.4 Human-guided discovery and diversity- driven search

Two lines of work inform our human-in-the-loop strategy. The first is human-guided machine discovery: Davies et al. show that machine learning can guide expert mathematical intuition toward new results [13], and machine discovery and expert refinement can compound, as when human mathematicians rapidly improved on AlphaTensor’s machine-found matrix-multiplication schemes [21]. Our staircase construction is in the same spirit: the expert does not hand- build the answer but recasts the problem into a structured formulation in which the extremal obstruction is native, and the learned search then tunes it to exceed a known lower bound. The second is quality-diversity search, which deliberately maintains a diverse population rather than optimizing a single objective: novelty search abandons the objective in favor of behavioral novelty [24], and MAP-Elites illuminates a search space by retaining the best solution in each region of a feature space [26]. These ideas motivate the diversity controls that let the automated search reach its strongest fully automated construction, and the quality-diversity archive we propose as future work.

## 3 Problems and exact objectives

For this study, we consider three combinatorial geometry problems - maximum independent set of rectangles (MISR), unit-square stabbing, and guillotine cutting. Next, we describe the three problems and the best known upper and lower bounds for them.
