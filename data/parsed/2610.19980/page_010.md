Because good solutions occupy isolated points of the design space, they are reached only when the representation, the local-search operation, and the surrogate are chosen together for the problem at hand; a mismatch in any one of them caps the achievable score, however well the other two are tuned. This careful, joint exploration of the components, rather than the learned model on its own, is what turns the generic loop into a working discovery method. The records nonetheless stop improving well before the budget is exhausted, for reasons we examine next.

**The plateau of automated search.** The best scores reported above are reached quickly and then stop improving. We ask whether this plateau reflects a shortage of computation or a structural limit, and three measurements point to the latter. First, the ranking of configurations stabilizes early: by one quarter of the training budget, the Spearman rank correlation between the current ordering of the 27 configurations and their final ordering is already 0.908 for MISR, 0.899 for unit-square, and 0.944 for guillotine, where a value of 1 would mean the ordering never changes again; the remaining runtime thus mostly extends plateaus rather than rearranging which components win. Second, the learned model rarely produces a record: counting only sampled candidates that pass exact verification and strictly improve the best score, the transformer accounts for 0.0%, 0.5%, and 1.5% of all improvements (0, 1, and 1 records), against 78, 220, and 63 from the combined initialization and local-search path. <tbc>the plateaus are long: in the median configuration, 97.5%, 64.1%, and 87.2% of a run’s generations elapse after its best construction has appeared, and the best configurations reach their records after just 3.12, 0.37, and 5.81 of the 24 available hours. Validity is not the bottleneck either: guillotine proposals are usually repairable (median valid yield 86.7%, versus 11.1% and 26.0% for the two integrality-gap problems) yet still plateau, so the model is producing legal but not useful novelty. Taken together, these signatures make the plateau a reachability problem rather than a shortage of computation: the search cannot leave the region it first enters, and the intervention of Section 6 is precisely an attempt to place it in a different region from the outset.

The barriers behind these plateaus, ranked by their estimated leverage in Appendix E, recur across all three problems: closed representation families whose configurations collapse to a single score, exact witnesses such as LP duals and cut trees that are never fed back as move-level guidance, archive collapse under repeated fresh-model restarts, and the absence of a composition operator that could scale a certified witness while preserving its ratio.

If the plateau were caused by limited model capacity rather than by the search landscape, a larger transformer of the kind used in the original PatternBoost [11] should help. We tested this directly on unit-square stabbing by rerunning representative configurations with a scaled transformer of about 1.82M parameters in place of the compact 244K-parameter model, keeping everything else fixed. We found no clear advantage from the larger model: as  3 shows, it matched the compact model on the coordinate-mutation configuration and did slightly worse on the other two. The scaled model also trains far more slowly and completes only about one third as many search generations under the same wall-clock budget. Its one potential advantage is per-generation efficiency, since it occasionally reaches a useful construction in fewer generations, but this does not compensate for its slower generations in wall-clock terms. The evidence therefore points to the geometric search landscape, not transformer capacity, as the binding constraint, and the compact transformer is the better use of a fixed compute budget.
