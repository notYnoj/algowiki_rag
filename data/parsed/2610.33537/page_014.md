## 5.1.1. Solution validation and spatial accuracy verification

The profiles of the dimensionless density \(\rho\), temperature \(\theta\), vertical velocity _u_<sub>2</sub> and heat flux _q_<sub>1</sub>, which are computed by the second-order FIM solver on a uniform grid with _N<sub>x</sub>=_128, are plotted in Figure 1. The DUGKS solutions obtained by the dugksFoam solver [38] are provided as the references. In the dugksFoam solver, the spatial domain of the Boltzmann-Shakhov equation is discretized using a second-order finite volume scheme, while the velocity space is represented by dimension-reduced Gauss-Hermite discrete velocity points. For the reference DUGKS computations, 100 physical-space cells are used for all three Knudsen numbers, together with \(81\times 81\) discrete velocity points for _Kn=_0.1, and \(28\times 28\) discrete velocity points for _Kn=_0.01 and 0.001. It is shown that our results agree well with the references at all three Knudsen numbers, confirming the validity of the high-order moment system.

In order to verify the spatial order of accuracy, the FIM solver is run on a sequence of uniform grids with _N<sub>x</sub>_ ranging from 64 to 4096. Since the exact solution cannot be obtained analytically, the numerical solution obtained on the finest grid with _N<sub>x</sub>=_4096 for each spatial discretization is taken as the benchmark solution. On a computational grid consisting of _N<sub>x</sub>_ uniform cells, the _L_<sup>2</sup> error is defined by

\(\text{Error}=\sqrt{\Delta x\sum_{i=0}^{N_x-1}\left(\bar{m}_i-\bar{m}_i^\text{finest}\right)^2},\)

where \(\Delta x=1/N_x\), \(\bar{m}_i\) denotes the cell average of either \(\rho\) or \(\theta\) over the _i_th computational cell, and \(\bar{m}_i^\text{finest}\) denotes the corresponding cell average from the benchmark solution. The errors of the first-order and second-order schemes are displayed in Figure 2, demonstrating that the expected orders of convergence are achieved for all considered Knudsen numbers.

## 5.1.2. Performance of first-order FIM solvers

We begin by examining the first-order FIM solvers, aiming to compare their performance against that reported in [23]. The total number of iterations and the associated wall-clock time spent by FIM-3 and FIM-4, as well as the IMEX-SGS iteration, to reach steady state on the grid with _N<sub>x</sub>=_256 across three Knudsen numbers are listed in  2. It is observed that the convergence of the IMEX-SGS iteration deteriorates significantly as _Kn_ decreases toward the continuum regime, leading to a substantial increase in the total number of iterations. However, the variation in wall-clock time is much less pronounced due to the use of lower-order moment systems for smaller _Kn_.

As expected, both FIM-3 and FIM-4 improve the computational efficiency over the IMEX-SGS iteration, with FIM-4 consistently delivering the best performance. The values of \(\gamma_2\) listed in  2 have been shown in [23] to provide good performance for the corresponding Knudsen numbers. The increase in the preferred value of \(\gamma_2\) as the Knudsen number decreases is consistent with the Fourier analysis in section 3. The convergence histories in Figure 3 further show that increasing \(\gamma_2\) can effectively accelerate the convergence of FIM-3, although the additional improvement becomes limited for larger values. Meanwhile, inappropriate choices of \(\gamma_2\) and the CFL number may introduce minor oscillations in the residual decay, as also reported in [23]. Therefore, selecting \(\gamma_2\) requires a compromise between convergence efficiency and robustness, which relies on empirical tuning. In contrast, FIM-4 replaces repeated hydrodynamic iterations with one Newton-multigrid step in each alternating iteration, thereby avoiding the tuning of \(\gamma_2\). Even when compared with the carefully tuned FIM-3, FIM-4 further reduces the wall-clock time by factors ranging from approximately 1.4 to 4.0. The convergence histories in Figure 3 demonstrate that FIM-4 achieves a faster and more stable residual decay with the lowest computational cost. These results indicate that one Newton-multigrid step is sufficient to provide an effective hydrodynamic correction within the FIM framework.
