## 1 Introduction

Over the past two decades, extensive research in parameterized complexity has focused on subexponential algorithms for planar graphs and, more broadly, on various classes of sparse graphs. While for most parameterized NP-hard problems the best we can hope for under the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH) of Impagliazzo and Paturi \[IP01\] is an exponential dependence on the parameter, there are nevertheless many problems that admit subexponential parameterized algorithms when restricted to planar graphs. This phenomenon was first demonstrated in 2000 \[ABF<sup>+</sup>02\], where the authors gave a subexponential parameterized algorithm for deciding whether a given _n_-vertex planar graph contains a dominating set of size _k_ in \(2^{\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{k})}n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\) time.

In 2005, Demaine, Fomin, Hajiaghayi, and Thilikos \[DFHT05\] introduced the _bidimensionality_ framework, providing elegant tools for designing subexponential parameterized algorithms for problems such as Vertex Cover, Feedback Vertex Set, Dominating Set, or Longest Path. Building on the grid minor theorem of Robertson, Seymour, and Thomas \[RST94\], this framework shows that for a planar graph _G_ and an integer _k_, either _G_ is a trivial yes- or no-instance of the problem at hand, or the treewidth of _G_ is in \(\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{k})\). In the latter case, applying standard dynamic programming techniques on graphs of bounded treewidth yields a running time of \(2^{\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{k})}n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\), where _n_ is the number of vertices. In many situations, the bidimensionality framework can be extended to subexponential parameterized algorithms on _H_-minor-free graphs. Moreover, for a large class of problems, the running time provided by bidimensionality is essentially optimal under the ETH: one cannot expect a \(2^{o(\sqrt{k})}n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\)-time algorithm. For an overview of bidimensionality and its applications, we refer to the standard text book on parameterized complexity \[CFK<sup>+</sup>15\].

However, the bidimensionality framework does not apply to directed graphs or to weighted problems, because the existence of large grid minors alone does not address these settings. A step toward overcoming this limitation is the _treewidth-pattern-covering_ approach \[FLM<sup>+</sup>22\]. Central to this method is a procedure that randomly samples a vertex subset _S_ in polynomial time with the following properties. The induced subgraph _G_\[_S_\] has treewidth \(\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{k}logk)\) and for every _connected_ subgraph _P_ of _G_ on at most _k_ vertices, the probability that _S_ contains all vertices of _P_ is at least \(1/2^{\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{k}log^2k)}n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\). By applying dynamic programming techniques on _G_\[_S_\], this yields a randomized algorithms running in \(2^{\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{k}log^2k)}n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\) time for problems such as Directed _k_-Path, Weighted _k_-Path, or Subgraph Isomorphism (when the sought subgraph is connected) for planar input graphs.

A crucial requirement of the treewidth-pattern-covering approach is that the pattern _P_ must be connected. To handle cases where _P_ is disconnected, Nederlof \[Ned20\] extended the treewidth- pattern-covering approach to find or counts (induced) copies of a _k_-vertex pattern _P_ in a planar graph in \(2^{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})}\text{poly}(\sigma(P)n)\) time, where \(\sigma(P)\) denotes the number of non-isomorphic separations of _P_ of size \(\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})\).<sup>1</sup> In particular, Nederlof’s results yield an algorithm with running time \(2^{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})}n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\) when _P_ is a matching, an independent set, or a connected bounded-degree graph. For any pattern _P_, he obtained algorithms running in 2<sup>O(_k/_log_k_)</sup>_n_<sup>O(1)</sup> time, which are essentially tight under the ETH.

None of the above frameworks provides subexponential parameterized algorithms for problems on _weighted_ graphs when the solution might be disconnected. The bidimensionality framework fails as the existence of a large grid minor does not reveal any information about a weighted solution.
