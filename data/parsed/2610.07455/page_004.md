Over the Boolean domain, these are precisely the two literals _x_ and \(\neg x\)<sup>1</sup>. Literals are more than a notational convenience in these arguments. Indeed, negations or additive shifts allow for much more powerful gadgets which lead to simpler lower bound proofs. Sharma and Velusamy \[SV26a\] showed that this is a fundamental obstruction in applying their techniques to general finite-domain CSPs. Over domains larger than two, the raw non-redundancy of _R_ need not predict the streaming complexity of CSP satisfiability.

The difficulty lies specifically in proving lower bounds. The known upper bounds continue to apply, since ordinary CSP instances are special cases of instances in the literal model. In the lower bound reductions however, literals were critical in gadget constructions that fixed the values of certain variables. Our starting question is therefore the following.

_Can we derive tight characterizations of the streaming complexity of_ CSPs?

As our primary contribution, we give an affirmative answer to the above question, in part making use of a crucial object called the _core_ of the CSP relation _R_, denoted by core(_R_), which intuitively removes domain values that can be collapsed without changing any of these objectives. In the context of CSPs, the notion of a core originated in the 1970s in the context of designing optimal queries to a database \[CM77; KV08\] but the concept was also independently developed in the graph theory literature (see \[HN92; HN08\]). Perhaps the most notable use of CSP cores is their key role in the proof of the CSP dichotomy theorem \[FV98; BKW17; Bul17; Zhu20\]. Despite these many connections, our work appears to be the first to utilize the concept of a CSP core in the context of streaming.

Beyond simply studying SAT(_R_), which asks whether all constraints can be satisfied, we also study Min-CSP(_R_), which approximately minimizes the number of unsatisfied constraints, and Exact-CSP(_R_), which computes the maximum number of satisfied constraints exactly. Thus, Exact-CSP(_R_) is the exact-value version of Max-CSP(_R_), and we use the latter terminology primarily for the approximation literature. As a byproduct of our study, we show that (a) non-redundancy is even more entrenched as a crucial parameter which governs the streaming complexity of CSP problems and (b) the core is a fundamental object which allows for the extension of lower bound techniques to general finite-domain CSPs.

## 1.1 Our Results

We study the one-pass streaming complexity of solving three different CSP objectives defined by a fixed finite relation _R_, namely SAT(_R_), Min-CSP(_R_), and Exact-CSP(_R_). The main parameter that governs the space complexity in our results is non-redundancy. Informally, an instance is non-redundant if every constraint is non-redundant, i.e., removing it would strictly grow the set of satisfying assignments of the instance. This is equivalent to having a witness assignment for each constraint that violates it but satisfies the rest of the constraints. We write NRD_<sub>n</sub>(R_) for the largest number of constraints in such an instance on _n_ variables. Our first theorem takes advantage of both NRD and core(_R_) and characterizes the randomized one-pass streaming complexity of SAT(_R_) up to a log factor.

**Theorem 1.1** (Informal Theorem 5.1 satisfiability characterization)**.** _For every fixed non-trivial relation R, there is a deterministic one-pass algorithm that solves_ SAT(_R_) _using O<sub>R</sub>_(NRD_<sub>n</sub>_(core(_R_))log_n_) _space, and any one-pass algorithm (even randomized) requires at least_ \(\Omega_R(\mathsf{NRD}_n(\mathsf{core}(R)))\) _space._

<sup>1</sup>By the general finite-domain setting, we mean that the relation and its finite domain are arbitrary, and constraints apply the relation directly to variables, without literals or shifts.
