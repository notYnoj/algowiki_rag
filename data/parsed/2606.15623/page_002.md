<tbc>quadratic cost of exhaustive comparison (_O(n_<sup>2</sup>)) makes it impractical at scale: ranking 200 items requires nearly 20,000 comparisons, each demanding seconds of expert time.

Sorting-based approaches reduce the comparison count to _O(n_log_n_) by embedding pairwise queries within a sorting algorithm [14,9]. Recent work further leverages pre-trained vision-language models (VLMs) to provide coarse initial orderings, enabling the sorting procedure to skip trivially resolvable pairs [17,10,16]. Despite this progress, a fundamental tension remains: VLM predictions are noisy--particularly for fine-grained distinctions where items have similar quality scores--yet existing methods either trust VLM outputs as-is or require human judgment for every comparison the sort requests.

We identify three limitations of current approaches. First, VLM-as-annotator methods [20] suffer from position bias and limited accuracy on near-tie pairs, with typical agreement rates of 60-70% against human judgment. Second, purely human sorting methods [9,14] waste budget on easy comparisons where VLM ordering is reliable. Third, ensemble-based approaches [17] introduce substantial computational overhead from neural ranking heads and Gaussian Process inference, limiting scalability to larger item sets.

We propose **Surprise-Guided MergeSort (SGS)**, which reframes the VLM’s role from annotator to _question prioritizer_. Rather than trusting or replacing VLM decisions, SGS measures how _surprised_ the system is by each proposed comparison and allocates the limited human budget to the most ambiguous pairs. The key insight is that a comparison is "surprising" when multiple uncertainty signals--VLM confidence after position-bias cancellation, Elo rating gap, and annotator vote entropy--all indicate genuine ambiguity. Low-surprise comparisons are automatically resolved under a capped inference policy and then propagated through the MergeSort transitive closure.

This design philosophy aligns with recent findings in active ranking for scientific imaging [18], where VLM priors are used only to guide informative sampling rather than as ground-truth annotations. A Round-0 calibration gate keeps SGS robust to _globally_ unreliable priors: when measured VLM-human agreement falls below 70%, SGS disables the sorting backbone and reverts to a warm-started Active Elo, preventing error propagation from a misleading initial ordering (we analyze the gate’s behavior, including its limits, in Section 5).

In summary, our main contributions are threefold:

**1. Agreement-calibrated VLM use.** SGS estimates VLM-human agreement \(\alpha\) from a small calibration set and uses it for both a centeredness-banded Elo warm start and an explicit gate: the MergeSort backbone is used only when the measured prior is sufficiently reliable; otherwise the method falls back to warm- started Active Elo. **2. Surprise-guided comparison routing.** SGS adopts a standard bottom-up MergeSort backbone and adds a budget-allocation layer that combines position-bias-cancelled VLM uncertainty, Elo-gap uncertainty, and vote entropy. High-surprise boundary pairs are sent to humans, while low-surprise pairs are admitted only under a progress-capped automatic-resolution policy.
