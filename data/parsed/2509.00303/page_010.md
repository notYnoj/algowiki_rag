## 6.2 Optimizer Main Results

Figure 3 and  2 present our main evaluation. Figure 3 has monetary cost on the _x_-axis and ranking quality on the _y_-axis (higher is better). Each dot also depicts a 80% confidence interval.  2 shows the top 3 best standalone algorithms and the best score acheived by our proposed optimizers. We sweep a range of user budgets and evaluate both proposed optimizers: the LLM- as-a-judge optimizer and the self-consistency (RRF) optimizer. Our optimizer is consistently competitive with the strongest standalone algorithm on every dataset: it matches or exceeds its ranking quality on 10 of the 15 dataset/model pairs and stays within 0.02 of it on the rest. In particular, the self-consistency optimizer, which aggregates several access paths rather than committing to one, most reliably matches or surpasses the best single algorithm.

**World Population Benchmark.** In the world population benchmark, across all three models, our optimizer using the inquiry prompt (Prompt 4) successfully identified that the world population is factual information. The optimizer automatically routed the query to the web search augmented pointwise method, achieving exceptionally high accuracies of 0.98-0.99 across models. As detailed in  2, while the external pointwise method with web search ranks third and exhibits competitive accuracy, it remains less stable than the standard pointwise approach, which is aligned with our observations in Section 4 and prior literature \[8\].

**Sembench Movie.** The Sembench Movie dataset comprises five distinct queries based on most reviewed movies. During the initial inquiry phase, these queries are classified as subjective, non- factual data. Consequently, the optimizer bypassed the default method of pointwise with web serach. As illustrated in Figure 3, relying solely on the pointwise sorting algorithm, as currently adopted in the Sembench baseline \[29\], results in a significant degradation of ranking accuracy. For instance, on the Claude Haiku-4.5 model, the standard pointwise method scores 0.875 at cost $2.18. In contrast, at a similar price point, the LLM-as- Judge and self-consistency optimizers score 0.885 at $2.60 and 0.882 at $2.49, and their best scores are 0.926 and 0.922. Moreover, across all three models, the optimizers correctly gravitate toward the high-accuracy regions dominated by comparison-based algorithms, matching or outperforming the best single algorithm on all three models as shown in  2.

**DL20.** DL20 comprises 54 passage-ranking queries. These queries cannot be resolved from parametric knowledge or web search, and require the model to directly assess (passage, query) relevance. As the second column of Figure 3 shows, value-based approaches (pointwise and external pointwise) consistently under- perform: across all three models their accuracy plateaus around 0.60-0.66, whereas comparison-based algorithms reach 0.68-0.72. This confirms our earlier observation that complex relevance judgments demand pairwise/listwise comparison and reasoning, rather than independent per-item scoring. Without any ground- truth labels, both optimizers correctly gravitate toward this high- accuracy comparison-based region: on Claude Haiku-4.5, for instance, they attain 0.707-0.720, while the best single algorithm (our proposed external merge sort) scores 0.717, with a similar pattern on the other two models ( 2). Comparing the two optimizers, the self-consistency optimizer exceeds the best standalone algorithm on all three models, while the LLM-as-judge optimizer stays close behind. This quality edge comes at a cost: because the self-consistency optimizer fuses several access paths via RRF whereas the judge executes only the single path it selects,<tbc>

**NFCorpus.** NFCorpus is our most challenging benchmark: each query ranks the entire 3.6k document corpus, of which only a few dozen are relevant, so the ranking signal is sparse and noisy. Absolute accuracy is correspondingly lower than on DL20-- the best single algorithm reaches 0.57-0.65 across the three models. This difficulty manifests as high variance across access paths. On Llama-3.1 70B, standalone accuracy spans a wide range (0.18-0.57): external pointwise scoring collapses, and even the comparison-based sorts disagree widely (quick sort 0.570 vs. external merge sort 0.480). Such variance makes label-free selection especially hard.

In an ideal world, ranking accuracy would increase monotonically with the allocated budget. While standalone algorithms exhibit a broadly positive relationship between computation and accuracy, this trend is not absolute: some higher-cost algorithms are in fact _less_ accurate. As the budget grows, such pricier-but- weaker algorithms become affordable and enter the candidate pool, and an optimizer that commits to a single path may select one of them, as shown in the LLM-as-judge optimizer in Figure 3(k). The self-consistency optimizer is more robust: by aggregating several access paths through RRF rather than committing to one, it averages out any single poor choice and yields a smoother, near-monotone curve. On Claude Haiku-4.5, the judge optimizer peaks early and then degrades as pricier-but- weaker paths become affordable, whereas the self-consistency optimizer rises to 0.64 and stays there. Llama-3.1 70B shows the same pattern: the judge dips to 0.42 at the third budget while self- consistency peaks at 0.63, above the best standalone (0.57). On GPT-5 mini the judge plateaus around 0.57 while self-consistency climbs to 0.673, surpassing the best standalone algorithm (0.651).

**HellaSwag.** HellaSwag is a commonsense sentence completion task: 100 queries ranks a shared pool of 400 candidates. The strongest standalone algorithm is also the most expensive: quick sort with majority voting attains 0.82 on Claude Haiku-4.5 but costs $741. On this dataset the optimizers come close to, but do not exceed, the best single algorithm: the self-consistency optimizer reaches 0.793, 0.811 and 0.813 and the LLM-as-judge 0.796, 0.804 and 0.806 for Llama-3.1 70B, GPT-5 mini and Haiku- 4.5, within 0.003-0.014 of the best standalone (0.799, 0.818 and 0.820). The judge reaches these scores at a fraction of the cost of the best standalone algorithm (e.g., $281 vs. $741 on Haiku-4.5), since it executes a single cheaper path. HellaSwag has exactly one correct completion per query, so per-query _nDCG_@10 is nearly binary; fusing several rankings through RRF can demote the correct item whenever the fused lists disagree, which limits the gain from self-consistency here, and both optimizers show small non-monotonic dips as the budget grows.

**Summary.** Across all datasets and models, both optimizers recover a near-optimal cost--quality Pareto frontier without ground- truth labels, matching or closely approaching the best standalone value-based and comparison-based algorithm at a comparable budget. The two policies occupy complementary regions of this frontier. The LLM-as-judge optimizer relies on internal reflection to sidestep the noise and inconsistent logic of long comparison traces, delivering strong accuracy at low cost; the self-consistency optimizer instead fuses the collective signal of multiple ranking passes to raise the accuracy ceiling, at greater expense.
