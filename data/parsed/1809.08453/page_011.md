<tbc>possibly their negations), it is sufficient to examine the matches of two specific agents among _m<sub>ij</sub>,m<sub>kj</sub>,w<sub>ij</sub>,w<sub>kj</sub>_ to determine if rotations \(\rho_i\) and \(\rho_k\) belong or not to _R_. These two specific agents are called _decisive agents of c<sub>j</sub>_ in the following. We have indeed \(\rho_i\in R\) iff the rank of the match of _m<sub>ij</sub>_ is `rk`<sup>+</sup>(_m<sub>ij</sub>_). Similarly, we have \(\rho_i\unknown\in R\) iff the rank of the match of _w<sub>ij</sub>_ is `rk`<sup>+</sup>(_w<sub>ij</sub>_). Put another way, _m<sub>ij</sub>_ (resp. _w<sub>ij</sub>_) is a decisive agent of _c<sub>j</sub>_ if _v<sub>i</sub>_ (resp. \(\neg v_i\)) belongs to _c<sub>j</sub>_. The clause _c<sub>j</sub>_ is not satisfied iff the two decisive agents are with their match of rank \(\mathtt{rk}^+(\cdot)\). The decisive agents according to the form of clause _c<sub>j</sub>_ are given in the last column of  1.

For illustration, let us return to the 2-SAT instance described by Equations 7 and 8. Given the stable marriage instance generated by the reduction, and a stable marriage `x`, clause \(v_1\wedge v_2\) is _not_ satisfied iff \(\mathtt{rk}(m_{11},\mu_\mathtt{x}(m_{11}))=\mathtt{rk}^+(m_{11})\) and \(\mathtt{rk}(m_{21},\mu_\mathtt{x}(m_{21}))=\mathtt{rk}^+(m_{21})\). More generally, it is possible to count the number of clauses that are _not_ satisfied by examining the ranks of the matches of the decisive agents of each clause.

We will soon explain how to use a GGI operator to count the number of clauses that are not satisfied in the 2-SAT instance. Beforehand, we need to introduce fictitious agents in order to control the positions of the decisive agents in the ordered vector of disutilities for every stable marriage. More precisely, we introduce four fictitious agents _m<sub>j</sub>_, \(m'_j\), _w<sub>j</sub>_, \(w'_j\) per clause _c<sub>j</sub>_ such that _m<sub>j</sub>_ (resp. \(m'_j\)) is the first choice of _w<sub>j</sub>_ (resp. \(w'_j\)) and vice-versa. Thus _m<sub>j</sub>_ (resp. \(m'_j\)) can only be matched to _w<sub>j</sub>_ (resp. \(w'_j\)) in a stable marriage, and therefore the fictitious agents will not interfere with the possible matches of the other agents.

The fictitious agents are placed in the preference lists of the other agents such that \(\mathtt{rk}^+(\cdot)=2j+1\) and \(\mathtt{rk}^-(\cdot)=2j+2\) for the two decisive agents of clause _c<sub>j</sub>_. Furthermore, \(\mathtt{rk}^+(\cdot)=1\) and \(\mathtt{rk}^-(\cdot)=2\) for the remaining (non-decisive) agents. Note that 2_j_+1_\>_2 as \(j\ge 1\) and therefore the two decisive agents of _c<sub>j</sub>_ are at positions 2(_n<sub>c</sub>-j_)+1 and 2(_n<sub>c</sub>-j_)+2 in the permutation that ranks the agents by non-increasing disutilities.

To achieve these properties, we position 2_j_ fictitious agents at the beginning of the preference list of the decisive agents of clause _c<sub>j</sub>_ (e.g., \(m_1m'_1\dots m_jm'_j\) for a decisive agent _w<sub>ij</sub>_). These agents are positioned just before the two possible matches of the agent in a stable marriage. Regarding the non-decisive agents, their two possible matches in a stable marriage are simply placed at the beginning of their preference lists.

For illustration, in the 2-SAT instance described by Equations 7 and 8, the preference list of agent _w_<sub>22</sub> (who is a decisive agent of _c_<sub>2</sub>) is:<tbc>

\(w_{22}:m_1\succ_{22}^wm'_1\succ_{22}^wm_2\succ_{22}^wm'_2\succ_{22}^wm_{21}\succ_{22}^wm_{22}\succ_{22}^w\dots\)

\(m_{22}:w_{22}\succ_{22}^mw_{25}\succ_{22}^m\dots\)
