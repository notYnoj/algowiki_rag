**Graphs & weighted graphs.** Throughout this paper, we consider finite undirected graphs and refer to the textbook of Diestel \[10\] for any undefined graph-theoretic notions. Unless stated otherwise, all graphs are simple.

For a graph _G_, _V(G_) and _E(G_) denote the vertex set and the edge set of _G_, respectively. We denote the number of vertices and edges of _G_ by _n_ and _m_, respectively. A graph together with an edge-weight function \(w:E(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\) is a _weighted graph_. Unless stated otherwise, whenever we write weighted graph, we will mean an edge weighted graph. For \(U\subseteq V(G)\), we write _G_\[_U_\] for the subgraph of _G_ induced by _U_, i.e., _V(G_\[_U_\])=_U_ and \(E(G[U])=\{\{u,v\}\in E(G)|u,v\in U\}\). For two graphs _G_<sub>1</sub> and _G_<sub>2</sub>, their _intersection_ is the graph \(G_1\cap G_2\) with \(V(G_1\cap G_2)=V(G_1)\cap V(G_2)\) and \(E(G_1\cap G_2)=E(G_1)\cap E(G_2)\), while their _union_ \(G_1\cup G_2\) is the graph with \(V(G_1\cup G_2)=V(G_1)\cup V(G_2)\) and \(E(G_1\cup G_2)=E(G_1)\cup E(G_2)\).

Consider a weighted graph _G_ with \(w:E(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\). For \(X\subseteq E(G)\), \(w(X)=\sum_{e\in X}w(e)\). For a subgraph _H_ of _G_, _w(H_)=_w(E(H_)). An ordered sequence of distinct vertices _P_=(_u_<sub>1</sub>=_u,u_<sub>2</sub>_,...,u<sub>\</sub>=v_) is a _path_ in _G_ between vertices _u_ and _v_ if \(\{u_i,u_{i+1}\}\in E(G)\) for all \(1\le i\le\ell-1\). We use the notation _P(u,v_) to denote a path between two vertices _u,v_; alternatively we say that _P_ is a _u-v_ path. The vertices _u_<sub>1</sub> and \(u_\ell\) are the _endpoints_ of _P_, and the remaining vertices are its _internal_ vertices. A _subpath_ of _P_ is any path of the form (_u<sub>i</sub>,u_<sub>_i_+1</sub>_,...,u<sub>j</sub>_) for \(1\le i\le j\le\ell\). The length of a path is defined as the sum of the weights of edges of the path, i.e., \(w(P)=\sum_{i\in[l-1]}w(\{u_i,u_{i+1}\})\). For two vertices _u_ and _v_ of _G_, the distance between them is the length of the shortest path between them. A _cycle_ is a path along with an additional edge between the two endpoints of the path. The _length_ of a cycle is the sum of the weights of its edges. A _shortest cycle_ of a graph is the smallest length cycle. The _girth_, _g(G_), is the minimum-weight of a cycle in _G_, i.e. _g(G_)=min{_w(C_)|_C_ is a cycle in _G_}. We say that the graph _G_ is _clean_ if every edge and vertex belongs to some shortest cycle.

**Planar graphs.** A graph is _planar_ if we can draw it on a plane without any pair of edges crossing (except at the shared endpoint, if any), and such a drawing is called a _planar embedding_ of the graph. We can find a planar embedding of a planar graph in linear time \[19\]. A planar graph _G_ with a fixed embedding on the plane is a _plane_ graph. The _faces_ of a plane graph are the open regions of the plane bounded by edges and containing no vertices or edges in their interior. The outer region is the _external_ face, and the other faces are _internal_. The vertices and edges appearing on the closed walk bounding a face form its _frontier_. A cycle _C_ of _G_ is called a _facial cycle_ if it is the frontier of some face.

For a shortest cycle _C_ of a plane graph _G_, we denote by _G<sub>C</sub>_ the subgraph of _G_ induced by the vertices lying on _C_ or in the interior Int(_C_) of the region bounded by the curve of _C_ in the plane. (In the above by interior region, we mean the region not containing the outer face.) For the two shortest cycles _C_<sub>1</sub> and _C_<sub>2</sub> in the plane graph _G_, we say \(C_1\le C_2\) if every vertex and edge of _C_<sub>1</sub> is either a vertex or edge of _C_<sub>2</sub> or is embedded in the curve of _C_<sub>2</sub>, i.e., Int(_C_<sub>2</sub>).

The _dual_ graph of a plane graph _G_, denoted _G_<sup>∗</sup>=(_F(G),E_<sup>∗</sup>), has one vertex for each face of _G_. For every edge \(e\in E(G)\), there is a corresponding _dual_ edge \(e^*\in E^*\) whose endpoints are the two faces whose frontiers contain _e_; if _e_ lies on the frontier of exactly one face (i.e., _e_ is a bridge), then _e_<sup>∗</sup> is a self-loop at that face. Note that _G_<sup>∗</sup> need not be simple even if _G_ is simple.
