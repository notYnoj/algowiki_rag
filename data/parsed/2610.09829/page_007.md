Let us highlight the aspects of the reduction that we consider the most delicate. Second, the gadgets are glued together by identifying root edges of edge selection gadgets with specific edges of vertex selection gadgets. Each identification creates new cycles passing through several gadgets, and we must certify that none of them has length below _L_. Finally, the equivalence rests on an exact counting argument: the budget \(k=2h+4\genfrac(){pt}{2}{h}{2}\) equals the maximum number of edge-disjoint shortest cycles that the gadgets can jointly supply, so every gadget must be saturated, and saturation is possible only along a selection encoding a multicolored clique.

## 2.2 Proof of Theorem 1.2

We give an intuitive idea behind our algorithm that proves Theorem 1.2. At a high level, our algorithm has two sources of novelty compared to the \(k^{\mathcal{O}(k)}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\)-time algorithm of \[4\]: a finer structural analysis of the laminar shortest cycles tree, which reduces all guessing from quadratically many objects to linearly many, and a new two-layer dynamic programming.

Consider an instance \((G,w,k,d,\ell)\) of Diverse Shortest Cycle Coverage, where _G_ is a planar graph. We assume that every edge of _G_ is in some shortest cycle, and _G_ is embedded on the plane in such a way that the frontier of the external (outer) face is a shortest cycle. Whenever we say that _G_ is a plane graph, we mean that _G_ is embedded this way. The work \[4\] introduces a tree-like structure that captures the shortest cycles in a weighted plane graph, called the _laminar shortest cycles tree (LSCT)_; similar structures have also been previously used (for instance, see \[18, 26, 16, 25\]). Intuitively speaking, a family of shortest cycles C in a given plane graph _G_ is _laminar_ if for distinct \(C,C'\in\mathcal{C}\), one of the following holds: i) _C_ is drawn inside \(C'\), ii) \(C'\) is drawn inside _C_, or iii) the regions (not containing the outer face) bounded by the closed curves of _C_ and \(C'\) are disjoint.

We begin by computing the laminar shortest cycles tree T for the given plane graph _G_ (see Definition 5.4 for the formal definition). The nodes of this tree are shortest cycles in _G_ (but not all of them). Figure 3 illustrates the kind of shortest cycles that are missing from T ; an intuitive explanation follows below. The only shortest cycles in _G_ that are not nodes of T are those consisting of two "non-consecutive" shortest paths, each of length _g(G)/_2, between a pair of vertices \(s,t\in V(G)\), where _g(G_) is the weighted girth of _G_; moreover, such a pair excludes the two "outermost" shortest _s-t_ paths. The shortest cycles that are nodes of T are _tree cycles_ and the ones that are not its nodes are _non-tree cycles_.
