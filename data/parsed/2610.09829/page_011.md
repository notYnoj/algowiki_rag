Let \(\alpha\) be a node of T and let the "closest" tree cycles in an optimal solution that appear "below" \(\alpha\) in T be the unknown cycles \(\beta_1,\beta_2,\cdots,\beta_b\). In the above, by "closest" we mean that there is no tree cycle in the solution that is strictly between \(\alpha\) and \(\beta_i\), \(i\in[b]\). Recall that we cannot precisely guess the \(\beta_i\)s that belong to maximal degree 2 paths. Let us take a closer look at the "unknown" cycle \(\beta_i\), which belongs to a maximal degree 2 path _Q_ in the subtree \(\mathcal{T}_\alpha\) of T rooted at \(\alpha\). Furthermore, let \(\gamma_i\) be the endpoint of _Q_ that is closest to \(\alpha\). Using planarity, we can guarantee that the edges that \(\beta_i\) may share with some \(\beta_j\), where \(j\in[b]\backslash\{i\}\) must also belong to \(\gamma_i\) (see Figure 5) and such intersections are highly structured, as illustrated in the figure.

Also, by the structure of intersections of shortest cycles, we can guarantee that \(P_i=\gamma_i\cap\beta_i\) is a sub-path of \(\gamma_i\). These two properties let us completely identify the "non-trivial" part of the intersection of \(\beta_i\) with other cycles once we know, roughly speaking, the "start" and "end" cycle intersections lying on _P<sub>i</sub>_. Thus, we will use the above information to derive the solution we are looking for. At the above step, we will again exploit planarity to ensure that we can achieve the above in time \(2^{O(k)}\cdot n^{O(1)}\). We remark that only "start" and "end" cycles are relevant for \(\beta_i\). We encode these guessed "end-cycles" as a graph, in which we can guarantee that each connected component is a path or a cycle. These highly structured components then allow us to identify the correct solution descendants by a second, separate dynamic programming.

Let us highlight the aspects of the algorithm that we consider the most novel. First, the marking scheme of \[4\] inherently costs _k_<sup>O(_k_)</sup>: it identifies O(_k_<sup>2</sup>) candidate cycles and guesses the solution among them. To obtain a single-exponential running time, every guess in our algorithm must range over objects whose number is linear in _k_ (the longitudinal paths covering the non-tree cycles, the marked representatives, and the intersection types) and it is planarity that keeps the product of all these choices single-exponential: a sparsity argument in the dual graph shows that the total size of the "relevant sets" of all representatives is linear in their number, so that the intersection types can be enumerated in 2<sup>O(_k_)</sup> time overall. Second, for _d\>_0 the strict laminarity property, on which the approach of \[4\] (as well as our simpler algorithm for the edge-disjoint case) crucially relies, completely breaks down: an optimal solution may be forced to use cycles that pairwise share edges, and the choices of solution descendants on different maximal degree-2 paths of T interact through these shared edges. Our replacement for the greedy step is a second layer of dynamic programming: the guessed "start" and "end" intersections define an auxiliary graph whose connected components are paths and cycles, and the interactions within each component are resolved by a dynamic program along it. Finally, the coverage objective introduces a difficulty absent from pure packing: the algorithm must account exactly for the weight of edges covered by two solution cycles, including cycles it never explicitly identifies. The structured intersections in planar graphs (no edge belongs to three solution cycles, and the pairwise intersections are subpaths of the known representatives) are precisely what makes this bookkeeping possible.
