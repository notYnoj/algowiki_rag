for which log_r/_log_n_ is small; We keep that in mind as our goal.

We may think of \(\le\) and \(\triangleleft\) as notions of reduction between computational problems, in this case using _r_ pairwise scalar multiplications to compute \(n\times n\) matrix multiplication, and we will see many more. Restriction \(S\le T\) implies degeneration \(S\triangleleft T\), but in our analysis (following Strassen’s way of working) we will go even further and use the asymptotic restriction \(S\lesssim T\) defined by \(S^{\otimes n}\le T^{\otimes n}\otimes\langle 2^{o(n)}\rangle\), for which \(S\triangleleft T\) implies \(S\lesssim T\). (Indeed, also \(\langle n,n,n\rangle\lesssim\langle r\rangle\) implies \(\omega_\mathbb{F}\le\mathrm{log}r/\mathrm{log}n\).)

We explain the proof at a high level in terms of degenerations. We first discuss the ingredients. The main objects, besides the matrix multiplication tensors, are the polynomial multiplication tensors P_<sub>a,b</sub>_, which describe the multiplication of a polynomial of degree at most _a_-1 by a polynomial of degree at most _b_-1. In other words, with _e<sub>i</sub>_ standing for the monomial _t_<sup>_i_-1</sup>,

\(\mathsf{P}_{a,b}=\sum_{i=1}^a\sum_{j=1}^be_i\otimes e_j\otimes e_{i+j-1}\in\mathbb{F}^a\otimes\mathbb{F}^b\otimes\mathbb{F}^{a+b-1}.\)

## 1.1.1 Degeneration ingredients

The starting point is that the simplest kind of polynomial multiplication (multiplying a degree 0 polynomial with a degree _b_-1 polynomial, i.e. P<sub>1_,b_</sub>) is the same thing as the simplest kind of rectangular matrix multiplication (multiplying a constant with a length _b_ vector, i.e. \(\langle 1,1,b\rangle\)):

(D0) **Matrix multiplication.** \(\mathsf{P}_{1,b}\cong\langle 1,1,b\rangle\) (Definition 3.1).

By ingredient (D0), and since \(\langle k,\ell,m\rangle\cong\langle k,1,1\rangle\otimes\langle 1,\ell,1\rangle\otimes\langle 1,1,m\rangle\), we see that the semiring, with direct sum \(\oplus\) as addition and tensor product \(\otimes\) as multiplication, generated by the polynomial multiplication tensors P_<sub>a,b</sub>_ and their permutations (where we mean permuting the three tensor legs), contains all matrix multiplication tensors \(\langle n_1,n_2,n_3\rangle\), and their direct sums. It will thus be natural to study the matrix multiplication tensors through (permuted) polynomial multiplication tensors.

Next, what makes polynomial multiplication appealing, is the fundamental fact that polynomial multiplication is computationally very cheap (by interpolation!), which in our language corresponds to a border rank upper bound:

(D1) **Border rank.** \(\mathsf{P}_{a,b}\triangleleft\langle a+b-1\rangle\) (Proposition 3.2).

In the study of matrix multiplication, as we already hinted at above, tensors are commonly combined by the direct sum \(\oplus\) and the tensor product \(\otimes\). Tensor products are natural because \(\langle n_1,n_2,n_3\rangle\otimes\langle m_1,m_2,m_3\rangle\cong\langle n_1m_1,n_2m_2,n_3m_3\rangle\) ( "block matrices are multiplied blockwise"). Direct sums are natural because of Schönhage’s asymptotic sum inequality: if \(\langle n_1,n_1,n_1\rangle\oplus\cdots\oplus\langle n_p,n_p,n_p\rangle\triangleleft\langle r\rangle\) for integers \(p,n_1,\dots,n_p,r\ge 1\), then we can obtain from this degenerations of the form \(\langle m,m,m\rangle\triangleleft\langle s\rangle\) that imply \(n_1^{\omega_\mathbb{F}}+\cdots+n_p^{\omega_\mathbb{F}}\le r\) \[Sch81, Theorem 7.1\]. So a direct sum of matrix multiplication tensors that is a degeneration of a unit tensor also bounds \(\omega_\mathbb{F}\), like the degenerations \(\langle n,n,n\rangle\triangleleft\langle r\rangle\) above.

Now if we look at \(T\otimes\langle 1,1,2\rangle\) we see that this gives a notion of partial direct sum of _T_ with itself, which we denote by \(T\oplus_1T\): two copies of _T_ are placed next to each other with one shared leg and two disjoint legs. More generally \(\oplus_1\) denotes a partial direct sum, whose summands share their first leg (Section 2.1).
