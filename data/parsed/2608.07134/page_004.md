To achieve the result of Theorem 1.2, we build on the approach of Theorem 1.1 and efficiently bundle modifications between consecutive observations. This involves transforming a sequence of arbitrarily interleaved insertions and deletions into an equivalent sequence in which all deletions are performed consecutively. To perform several deletions efficiently, we use the recently introduced _selectable heap_ [SZ22]. Interestingly, this data structure also relies on soft heaps in one of its possible implementations.

Application and future work. We find the separation of modifications from observations and the resulting fine-grained bounds obtained via the sync heap to be of intrinsic interest, as they concern a basic data-structural question and model of computation. We highlight one algorithmic application to a classical scheduling problem called _single-machine maximum-profit unit-time scheduling_.

The standard O(_n_log_n_)-time algorithm for this problem dates back to the 1970s [Law76]. In §2.1 we show an improved linear-time algorithm as a simple application of sync heaps. The problem is covered in many classical algorithms textbooks including [CLRS09, §16.5], [Bru07, §4.4.2], [HSR08, §4.4], but no linear-time algorithm was known for it in the literature, to the best of our knowledge.

A possible future direction is to extend the model to other heap operations, such as `decrease-key` and `meld`, which appear to require new ideas. We believe that our techniques may have applications to other data structures as well.

Further related work. Heaps are among the best-studied data structures in computer science, due to their varied applications, e.g., in graph algorithms. A broad survey of heaps up to 2013 is given by Brodal [Bro13]. Classical binary heaps [Wil64] already support logarithmic-time basic operations, while optimal amortized times for all operations (including `meld` and `decrease-key`) were first attained by Fibonacci heaps [FT87]; these bounds were deamortized in strict Fibonacci heaps [BLT25]. Pairing heaps [FSST86] are a particularly elegant "bookkeeping-free" design that has influenced a line of work in "self-adjusting data structures". More recent designs include quake heaps [Cha13], hollow heaps [HKTZ17], slim and smooth heaps [HKST21, KS20], among others.

The sorting lower bound on the complexity of heap operations can be circumvented in various specialized settings. These include heaps with _integer keys_ [vEBKZZ76, Wil83, FW93, Tho04, Tho07], operation sequences exhibiting _structure_, such as spatial and/or temporal locality [Iac00, IL05, Elm06, DJ09, EFI12, HHR<sup>+</sup>24, vRR25, Rut26, HHRT26], heaps with _predictions_ [BC24], and heaps with relaxed correctness guarantees, such as allowing _approximate answers_ [TZZZ19, Dum19].

Similar in spirit to our work is [KZZ15], where an essentially tight trade-off is shown between the amortized costs of `insert`, `delete`, and `find-min`; see also [BCR96]. In particular, their _lazy binomial heap_ data structure achieves the amortized times O(1) for `insert`, O(_t_) for `delete`, and O(_n/_2<sup>_t_</sup>+log_n_) for `find-min`. Choosing _t_=log_k_ yields a total of O(_n+d_log_k+k_log_n_), where _k_ is the number of `find-min` and _d_ is the number of `delete` operations. Even though the bound resembles the one in Theorem 1.2, the result is not directly comparable to ours, as it concerns a `delete` operation (less common for heaps) that is given a pointer to the element to be deleted, rather than `delete-min`, for which the minimum crucially needs to be identified first. The techniques used in [KZZ15] are also rather different from ours.

Our heap evaluation problem can also be seen as related to the _checking_ problem, where a given offline sequence of _n_ heap operations (together with the outputs of operations) has to be verified for correctness.
