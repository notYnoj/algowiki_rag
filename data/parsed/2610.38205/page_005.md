Finally, we show that the same framework extends to the related problem of constructing sparse \(\tau\)-monotonic graphs; see Section 3.3.

### Hardness of navigability verification.

A key component of our construction, inherited from \[25\], is a black-box procedure that determines which source-target \(\alpha\)-navigability constraints are satisfied by the edges currently in the graph. After distances are discretized, the constraints at each distance scale can be checked using one matrix multiplication. These matrix multiplications dominate the running time of our algorithm, so a substantially faster verification procedure would immediately yield a faster resource-augmented constructions.

This motivates us to study navigability verification as an independent algorithmic problem. We prove two hardness results. First, even when restricted to source-target pairs at a single fixed distance, \(\alpha\)-navigability verification is at least as hard as Boolean matrix multiplication: an \(n\times n\) Boolean matrix product can be computed using one call to the verification procedure and _O(n_<sup>2</sup>) additional time. Thus, bypassing matrix multiplication in our verification routine would require a corresponding breakthrough for Boolean matrix multiplication. Second, we consider exact verification over all undiscretized distances. We show that this problem is equivalent, up to polylogarithmic factors, to Boolean min-plus product. Combining this equivalence with a recent conditional lower bound for Boolean min-plus product \[14\], we show that, under the APSP conjecture, exact \(\alpha\)- navigability verification cannot be solved in \(O(n^{2.5-\delta})\) time for any constant \(\delta>0\). Together, these results suggest that both the matrix multiplication bottleneck and the use of resource augmentation are difficult to avoid within our framework.

## 1.4 Technical Overview and Prior Work

A first general answer for the question of how sparse a navigable graph can be made, and how efficiently such a graph can be constructed was given by \[13\]. They showed every _n_-point dataset admits a nontrivial sparse navigable graph, independent of the ambient dimension and without relying on metric structure. Their construction takes the union of a nearest-neighbor graph and a random graph, and in \(\widetilde{O}(n^2)\) time constructs a navigable graph with \(\widetilde{O}(n^{3/2})\) edges. Equivalently, every point set admits a navigable graph of average degree \(\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{n})\). This bound is essentially tight in the worst-case: for random points in _O_(log_n_)-dimensional Euclidean space, a navigable graph must have average degree \(\Omega(n^{1/2-o(1)})\) (with high probability).

This worst-case picture, however, does not capture the instance-by-instance structure of the problem. Many datasets may admit navigable graphs far sparser than the \(\widetilde{O}(n^{3/2})\)-edge worst-case guarantee. Thus the more refined objective is not merely to construct some sparse navigable graph, but to approximate the sparsest navigable graph for the given instance. This instance-optimal viewpoint was subsequently developed \[12, 25\]. The key observation in both of these works is that the sparsest \(\alpha\)-navigable graph problem decomposes into _n_ coupled set-cover instances, one for each source _s_. For a fixed source _s_, a candidate outgoing edge (_s,u_) covers exactly those targets _t_ for which _u_ gives a valid \(\alpha\)-progress step toward _t_. Thus, choosing the minimum out-neighborhood of _s_ is precisely a minimum set-cover problem over the set of targets _P_\\{_s_}. Solving these _n_ set-cover instances near-optimally yields a near-optimal sparsest \(\alpha\)-navigable graph. In fact, it produces an even stronger approximation property: for every \(s\in P\), the out-neighborhood of _s_ is approximately the size of the minimum neighborhood of _s_ in any navigable graph. This set-cover formulation immediately yields an _O_(log_n_)-approximation to the optimum via the greedy set-cover algorithm, but a direct implementation takes cubic time even only to construct the sets explicitly.
