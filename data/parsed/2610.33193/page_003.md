Then in the 20th century there was a similarly revolutionary invention, of computers. Accordingly, the energy required to accomplish _computational_ objectives has been a subject of academic inquiry for a century. A deeper and more powerful understanding of this thermodynamics of computation would not just be a purely theoretical advance. As a practical matter, we live in a world where the energetic costs of computing are critically important. The International Energy Agency estimates that 485 terawatt-hours was consumed running data centers in the year 2025 and projects that this number will roughly double to 950 terawatt-hours in 2030 [Int26]. Despite this, our theoretical understanding of the fundamental thermodynamic costs of computation is rather limited, partially due to the lack of a strong and flexible model that captures many of the nuances of the thermodynamics of computation beyond logical reversibility. While current computational devices expend energy at rates well above those mandated by the laws of thermodynamics, as technology improves a principled understanding of thermodynamics is key to optimizing the energetic costs of computation.

However, most of the study of the thermodynamics of computation to date has been based on classical thermodynamics principles. While the field of thermodynamics itself has continued to evolve in recent decades, the study of the thermodynamics of computation has not kept pace. In particular, there has been a revolution in non-equilibrium statistical physics in the 21st century, resulting in what is now called _stochastic thermodynamics_. Whereas classical thermodynamics is, in its foundations, concerned only with systems at equilibrium, stochastic thermodynamics focuses on systems arbitrarily far from equilibrium. This makes it the ideal formalism for analyzing the thermodynamics of computation.

This paper serves two purposes: First, we introduce such a new model for investigating the thermodynamics of circuits. Our goal with this model is to strike a balance between broad accessibility to a general computer science audience and correctness with respect to stochastic thermodynamics, which represents our current best understanding of the fundamental energetic cost of machines, including computing machines. Second, we show how this circuit model can be leveraged to prove a wide range of new results ranging from the computational complexity of optimizing Boolean circuits to asymptotically tight lower and upper bounds on the thermodynamic costs of fixed circuits.

We start by giving a detailed overview of the history of the thermodynamics of computation before diving into our model and results.
