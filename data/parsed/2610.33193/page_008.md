Applying equation (1) to the first step, we have that D<sub>0</sub> is the point-mass Pr<sub>D<sub>0</sub></sub>\[_b_=0\]=1, whereas afterward we have Pr<sub>D<sub>1</sub></sub>\[_b_=0\]=Pr<sub>D<sub>1</sub></sub>\[_b_=1\]=1/2. Thus the Landauer cost S(D<sub>0</sub>)-S(D<sub>1</sub>)=-1 is actually _negative_. Physically, this corresponds to the fact that in principle, one removes _k_Tln2 heat from the environment to randomize this bit, releasing the same amount of heat on the subsequent erasure step that takes us from D<sub>1</sub> (_b_ is equally likely to be 0 or 1) back to D<sub>0</sub> (_b_=0). This is essentially "taking a loan and then paying it back".

In the remainder of this paper, we focus on deterministic computation, in which every state has at most one successor. Our model may describe only part of a larger physical device: other parts may perform randomized operations with negative Landauer cost and draw heat from the environment to offset heat released by the deterministic computation. When \(f:D\rightarrow R\) is a (deterministic, single-valued) function being computed, we let L_<sub>f</sub>_(D) (Definition 2.19) denote S(D)-S(_f_(D)), where _f_(D) denotes the distribution on _R_ induced by distribution D on _D_. In this case, the data processing inequality (Definition 2.5) implies that \(\mathcal{L}_f(\mathcal{D})\ge 0\); i.e., a deterministic process cannot increase the entropy of a distribution.

The previous example shows that a logically irreversible operation may have zero entropy production. Next we discuss a "dual" example: an overall operation can be logically reversible, yet require positive entropy production due to "locality", i.e., due to the fact that portions of the computation must be done in physically separate places, so that despite entropy staying constant globally, local operations must produce entropy due to lack of knowledge of rest of the state of the system. Consider three entities Alice, Bob, and Eve. Alice and Bob are at physically separate locations with no ability to communicate. Eve has a uniformly random bit _b_, and sends a copy of _b_ to each of Alice and Bob. Alice must erase her bit, setting it to 0. Globally, this is logically reversible, since the mapping \((b,b)\mapsto(0,b)\) is injective, but since Alice "locally forgets" _b_, she must release a unit of heat into the environment to erase her bit, even though far-away Bob remembers _b_. Though this example may seem contrived and hand-wavey, it is in fact a good example of what we sometimes called _modularity cost_ (also known as _modularity mismatch cost_ in the stochastic thermodynamics literature, e.g., \[Wol19b\]): the fact that a self-contained computational module, such as a gate in a circuit, must release heat to compute an irreversible _local_ operation, despite the fact that elsewhere in the circuit, information about the gate’s input is retained and so globally, the operation is reversible.

To summarize, (1) applied to deterministic computation of a function _f_ obeys EP=Q-L_<sub>f</sub>_(D). L_<sub>f</sub>_(D) is a purely information theoretic term depending only on _f_ and the input distribution D: the loss of entropy S(D)-S(_f_(D)) between the input distribution D to _f_ and the induced output distribution _f_(D). Reversible computing literature is concerned with making L_<sub>f</sub>_(D)=0 for every computational step; in this paper we allow it to be positive, though we freely and regularly use reversible computing techniques as well. Q is the heat released into the environment during this computation; this depends on the physics of the device computing _f_. The Second Law of Thermodynamics states that \(\mathsf{EP}\ge 0\), so since EP=Q-L_<sub>f</sub>_(D), this implies \(\mathsf{Q}\ge\mathcal{L}_f(\mathcal{D})\). From a complexity-theoretic perspective, we wish to minimize EP, since it represents a physically irreversible loss of work (useful energy). However, given a fixed _f_ to compute and distribution D, we have no control over L_<sub>f</sub>_(D), so minimizing Q is equivalent to minimizing EP.
