## 1.3 Prior work on thermodynamics of circuits

One perspective on the thermodynamic costs of circuits is provided by works of Michael Frank [Fra17, Fra18]. In [Fra17], Frank generalizes the conventional computer science notion of logical reversibility to include operations that, while not reversible in general, are so under a restricted subset of their inputs. He denotes such operations as being "conditionally reversible" and shows that they can be implemented to release arbitrarily little heat, so long as the gates never receive an input violating the reversibility precondition. Frank is primarily interested in conditional reversibility since it gives logically reversible gates that may be easier to implement than networks of Toffoli gates. Our work can be seen as a further generalization of this idea; in addition to allowing conditionally reversible gates to release zero expected heat on valid distributions over inputs, we consider gates that are finely tuned for the actual distribution over inputs they expect to receive. In [Fra18], Frank also points out that Q is not quite the correct measure of thermodynamic cost. He points out that the thermodynamic costs associated with Landauer’s principle is not merely the fact that Q must be positive. Rather it is that information which is dissipated as heat will rapidly become unrecoverable to the computational process, resulting in something similar to what we call a modularity cost. Frank justifies the study of logically reversible computing since it is a sufficient condition to avoid Landauer related thermodynamic costs.

Prior work by Wolpert, Kolchinsky, Caravelli, Yadav, and Yousef [WK20, YCW26, YYW26] investigated the thermodynamics of Boolean circuits using the theorems involving _mismatch cost_ -- a metric that quantifies the additional entropy production incurred by running a process on a different input distribution than the "ideal" one for given hardware. In this case, the physics of the device computing the gate may, in a sense, be "summarized information-theoretically" by an _optimal_ input distribution D<sup>∗</sup>, the distribution that minimizes the entropy production according to (1). They show lower bounds on the entropy production induced by running a circuit on a (potentially mismatched) input distribution D, where the lower bound depends only on the logical behavior of the gates with respect to an optimal input distribution D<sup>∗</sup>. In other words, it is possible to prove non-trivial lower bounds on the EP of a circuit without knowing any details of the physical implementation of its gates other than their optimal distributions over its inputs.<sup>11</sup>

The perspective of these works is that we know an optimal input distribution for a gate, and proceeds from there to measure mismatch costs (which are a lower bound on EP). Mismatch costs are a powerful tool for analyzing thermodynamic costs without needing to specify the underlying physics, but they are cumbersome to work with. The thermodynamic accounting they provide requires viewing everything through the lens of distributions over state and costs are neither linear (with respect to the probabilities of inputs) nor local to the gates performing computation. Moreover, the optimal distribution for a gate is a counter-intuitive way to specify its behavior and such a distribution is not unique even when the dynamics of the gate are deterministic.<sup>12</sup>

Nevertheless, these methods are particularly nice as with mild assumptions on the behavior of gates in a circuit (i.e. modularity or time-invariant behavior), they can prove entropy lower bounds that are truly independent of the underlying hardware.

<sup>11</sup>The physical implementation does influence what is the optimal distribution. The significance of mismatch cost is that it can be measured knowing _only_ what is the optimal distribution, i.e., the physical implementation influences the mismatch cost _only_ through how it influences what is the optimal distribution. <sup>12</sup>There is a well defined way to handle this with mismatch costs, but it requires analyzing the pre-image of each possible output independently, but this can be messy to work with.
