## 1.4 Our results

Here we provide a high level overview of our results and the techniques we use to obtain them.

### The thermodynamic circuit model

We introduce a model of circuits where each gate releases heat determined by its input value in order to perform logically irreversible computation. For a circuit to compute a function _f_, we require it to map each input state \((x,\vec{0})\) to output state \((f(x),\vec{0})\); the \(\vec{0}\)’s (which have different lengths if \(|x|\neq|f(x)|\)) denote "ancilla" wires that may be used as extra storage during the computation. In other words, the circuit must erase any "dirty" bits introduced during the computation including the original value of _x_. The amount of heat a gate releases can depend on that gate’s input; imagine that the gate must undergo some physical process to map each of its possible input states to the correct output. Thus, we can assign a "heat function" to each gate \(Q:\{0,1\}^n\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\cup\{\infty\}\) that maps each input to an associated amount of released heat. Technically, the amount of heat released by this process may not be deterministic, so one should think of these values as being expected costs, though sometimes for brevity we may say a gate releases _Q(x_) heat on input _x_, understood to mean _expected_ heat. Thanks to the linearity of expectation, this does not make a difference in the details of our model. We say a heat function for a gate is _valid_ if it obeys the Second Law of thermodynamics (i.e. the EP in (1) is always non-negative) on every marginal input distribution. From this perspective, we connect our model to mismatch costs in Appendix C of the appendix, and show that some of the results in \[WK20, YCW26, YYW26\] have more intuitive proofs using heat functions. We also show how our model can be used to prove new results about the thermodynamic costs of circuits.

Recall from Section 1.2 that our goal is to minimize EP, and that once L_<sub>f</sub>_(D) is fixed, minimizing EP is equivalent to minimizing Q. Despite this equivalence once L_<sub>f</sub>_(D) is fixed, when we prove a one-way bound, we generally prove lower bounds on EP (i.e., negative results stating that some computation _must_ dissipate a certain minimum amount of work that cannot be recovered) and upper bounds on Q (i.e., positive results stating that it is _possible_ to achieve a certain computation while only releasing a small amount of heat). For deterministic computation, \(\mathsf{EP}\le\mathsf{Q}\) so a lower bound on EP is also a lower bound on Q and an upper bound on Q is also an upper bound on EP. As is typical in theoretical computer science, we will use _O_-notation to suppress constant factors in our bounds. Note that constant factors are important to track in the case where \(\mathsf{Q}=\Omega(h(n))\) and L_<sub>f</sub>_(D)=_O(h(n_)). Depending on the hidden constants, the EP for such a computation could be anywhere between 0 and \(\Omega(h(n))\).

There are two important caveats with our approach in this paper. First, when discussing EP, we are implicitly assuming that the input distribution to our circuit is sampled independently from any larger context. If a copy of the input to a circuit is retained somewhere outside of the circuit, then the drop in entropy in (1) should actually be zero and we get that EP=Q. When proving lower bounds on EP this is a non-issue, as the additional context can only increase the EP. When proving upper bounds on EP, we specify that we are interested in the _circuit entropy production_, which we define as EP under this assumption on the input distribution. Second, when giving upper bounds on either the Q or the EP of a circuit, we are operating under the "axiom" that every gate could in principle be built with any valid heat function. While the validity constraint protects our gates from violating the Second Law of thermodynamics, other physical or engineering constraints may theoretically prevent such a gate from existing.
