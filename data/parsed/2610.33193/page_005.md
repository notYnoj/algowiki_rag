Second, over the last few decades the field of non-equilibrium statistical physics has undergone major developments, culminating in a new subfield called _stochastic thermodynamics_ [VdBE15, Sei25]. These developments have upended our understanding of the thermodynamics of physical systems that implement computation. In particular, they reveal that the traditional understanding of logical reversibility and Landauer’s principle is at best partially correct: while the Second Law does indeed imply that decreasing entropy of a system locally requires increasing entropy outside the system, nothing in the Second Law says that this must be a physically irreversible loss of work.

To give a simple example of a logically irreversible process that can be done in a physically/thermodynamically reversible way (i.e., can be done with arbitrarily little dissipated work), suppose we have a particle in a cylinder, and identify the particle being in the top half of the cylinder as a bit 1, while the particle being in the bottom half is identified as bit 0, with equal probability. So if we compress the cylinder to its bottom half using a piston, we perform a bit erasure, since afterwards the particle is guaranteed to be in the bottom half. Moreover, in first-year physics we learn that if this compression is done sufficiently slowly, then all the work expended in that compression can be recovered afterward simply by _uncompressing_ the cylinder sufficiently slowly, returning it to its original volume, and returning the particle’s distribution to equally likely to be in either half (see Szilard engines for more details, [Szi29, KMPA14]).

This is precisely the property that defines a process as being thermodynamically reversible. In contrast, if one compresses the cylinder too quickly, no matter how one subsequently uncompresses the cylinder, one won’t recover all the work that was used in the compression. This is what is meant by thermodynamic irreversibility. This example illustrates that logical and thermodynamic reversibility are independent properties of a process; it is possible to have one without the other. (See [Sag14] for a pedagogical elaboration of this point.)

Third, if the function _f_ being computed is not injective, it is not possible to compute the mapping \(x\mapsto f(x)\) in a logically reversibly way; instead reversible computing models typically compute \(x\mapsto(x,f(x))\). But the mappings \(x\rightarrow f(x)\) and \(x\rightarrow(x,f(x))\) are fundamentally different. If _f_ is non-injective or if _f_<sup>-1</sup> is prohibitively expensive to compute (i.e., if _f_ is a one-way function), then it is impossible to efficiently and reversibly obtain just _f(x_) from the pair (_x,f(x_)).<sup>6</sup> One might think it is okay to just leave an extra copy of _x_ in your computer’s memory, but if you compute _f_ repeatedly and old values of _x_ are overwritten, the cost of erasing these old values must be paid (see [Wol19a, Sec.11]). One might hope to store values of _x_ generated by intermediate steps of a larger logically reversible computation and reversibly uncompute them during the uncomputation step, however, temporarily storing _x_ increases the space complexity of the overall computation (see [Ben89]).
