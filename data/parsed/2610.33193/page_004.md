Phrased in terms of _work_ (i.e., accessible energy), _Landauer’s principle_ states that _logically irreversible operations must expend work in order to release heat into their environments_.<sup>2</sup> Given a transition from _n_ equally likely predecessor states to _m_ equally likely successors, _k_Tln(_n/m_) heat must be released, regardless of the physical implementation of the operation. Here T denotes the system’s temperature<sup>3</sup> and _k_ is Boltzmann’s constant.<sup>4</sup>

Landauer argued \[Lan61\] that most real computation would necessarily execute many irreversible operations, implying an unavoidable cost of \(\Omega(tk\mathbb{T}ln2)\) to execute _t_ steps of a computation regardless of the physical implementation of the computing device. Bennett \[Ben73\] challenged this assumption, showing how to convert an arbitrary Turing machine _M_ into a logically reversible Turing machine _R_ computing the same function. The configuration graph of _R_ is then a 1D line \(c_0\rightarrow c_1\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow c_t\). A completely unbiased random walk on this graph, requiring no work to bias it forwards, would then visit final configuration _c<sub>t</sub>_ in _O(t_<sup>2</sup>) time on average.<sup>5</sup>

Bennett’s original construction \[Ben73\] simulates a _t_-step Turing machine in _O(t_) steps but pays with an increase in memory complexity up to \(\Theta(t)\), essentially needing to write down the entire history of the computation on a separate worktape to maintain logical reversibility, bringing the memory use up to asymptotically match the time. (Thus a polynomial-time, log-space computation, for example, pays exponentially extra memory to make the computation logically reversible.) A series of papers over the next few decades \[Ben89, LS90, LV96, LTV98, LMT00, BTV01\] showed various improved simulation time/space tradeoffs in reversible Turing machines; see \[Ben82, Vit05, Mor08\] for useful surveys. Toffoli and Fredkin \[Tof77, Tof80, FT82\] pioneered the study of reversible computation in other computational models such as cellular automata and circuits. Meanwhile in the physics community, there has been work on understanding the thermodynamics of logically reversible Turing machines \[SCSB15b, BJO21\].

So what is there to say about the fundamental thermodynamic costs of computation that has not already been said in the reversible computing literature? First, we may consider a situation in which we do not have access to a logically reversible gate set, in which we wish to understand the fundamental energetic costs associated with whatever computational devices we do have on hand. While there has been progress towards actually building logically reversible computational devices (e.g. \[AFK<sup>+</sup>98, Fra02, FBT<sup>+</sup>20\]), these systems are not yet ready for widespread use and most computation today is performed in a highly irreversible manner.
