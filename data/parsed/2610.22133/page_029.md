## A Motivation for the Kratsch-Spinrad problems

Almost all the structures considered by Spinrad and Kratsch are fundamental to structural graph theory. They are closely intertwined and can be understood through four central graph classes:

The first class consists of _perfect graphs_, defined as graphs where every induced subgraph has a chromatic number equal to the size of its maximum clique. Perfect graphs are best known for the Strong Perfect Graph Theorem, conjectured by Berge in the 1960s [Ber60, Ber61] and proven by Chudnovsky, Robertson, Seymour, and Thomas [CRST06]. In this context, _star-cutsets_ were first introduced by Chvátal [Chv85] to characterize minimal imperfect graphs, establishing star- cutsets as a powerful structural obstruction used to decompose graph classes and prove perfection [Chv85, Ree87].

_Triangulated graphs_ (also called _chordal, rigid-circuit, monotone transitive_, or _perfect elimination_ graphs) are graphs in which every cycle of length four or more has a chord. Dirac [Dir61] gave two equivalent characterizations: a graph is chordal if and only if every induced subgraph is either a clique or contains two non-adjacent _simplicial vertices_ or, equivalently, if and only if every minimal cutset is a _clique-cutset_. The latter formulation highlights why many problems can be solved faster on chordal graphs [Gav72, RTL76]: a clique cutset decomposes a graph into independently solvable pieces, since the separating clique interacts uniformly with every part of the split. This property of clique cutsets can be exploited for other graph classes as well.

A superclass of triangulated graphs is the class of _weakly triangulated graphs_, in which _two-pairs_ play a fundamental role. Weakly triangulated graphs were first defined by Hayward [Hay85] as graphs containing no induced _holes_ (that is, induced cycles of length at least five) or _antiholes_ (holes in the complement). Note that this is a generalization of triangulated graphs, since forbidding induced cycles of length four and five already forbids all antiholes. Sritharan conjectured that weakly triangulated graphs are exactly the graphs in which every induced subgraph is either a complete graph or contains a two-pair (so two-pairs play a role analogous to that of simplicial vertices). This was later proven by Chvátal and Rusu [CR93] (see also [ES95, CRS02]). Two-pairs are also central in the design of efficient algorithms for weakly triangulated graphs [HHM90, HSS07].

Last but not least, _AT-free_ graphs were first introduced by Lekkerkerker and Boland [LB62] as a way to relate interval graphs and triangulated graphs: they showed that a graph is an interval graph if and only if it is both chordal and AT-free. AT-free graphs were subsequently studied in relation to other graph classes, such as cocomparability graphs and perfect graphs [Gal67, LB79, GMT84], before being investigated in their own right in the seminal work of Corneil, Olariu, and Stewart [COS97]. One property of interval graphs that [COS97] extended to AT-free graphs is that every such graph contains a _dominating pair_ realizing the diameter. These and other properties of AT-free graphs allow several NP-hard problems to be solved in polynomial time on AT-free graphs (e.g., [Sta12, KM12, BKKM99, Kra00, KMT08]); moreover, recent algorithmic results on AT-free graphs have even achieved running times that bypass the fine-grained lower bounds [Duc22, DDHV25].

## B Dominated vertex is equivalent to OV

Recall that a vertex _u_ in a graph **G** is dominated by a node _v_ if every neighbor of _u_ is also a neighbor of _v_ or is _v_. We will present two reductions. The first will reduce the problem of finding a dominated vertex to OV in _n_ dimensions. The second will reduce the OV problem to finding a dominated vertex in a given graph. We will then conclude with a short extra proof, that OV in _n_ dimensions is equivalent to Dominated vertex which is also equivalent to solving \(\exists x\in V,\exists y\in N(v),\forall z\in N(v),(y,z)\in E\).

**Lemma B.1.** _Given an n-node graph_ **G**=(_V,E_) _one can create in_ O(_n_<sup>2</sup>) _time an instance of OV on two sets of n vectors on_ 2_n dimensions so that it has a pair of orthogonal vectors if and only if G has a dominated vertex._
