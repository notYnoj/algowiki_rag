## Chapter 2

### Matrix sequences: spectral distributions and asymptotic structures

Matrix sequences constitute a fundamental tool in the analysis of numerical methods for differential equations. A matrix sequence is defined as a family of square matrices {_A<sub>n</sub>}<sub>n</sub>_, where each matrix _A<sub>n</sub>_ has size \(d_n\times d_n\) and the dimension _d<sub>n</sub>_ increases with _n_, satisfying _d_<sub>_n_<sub>1</sub></sub>_\<d_<sub>_n_<sub>2</sub></sub> whenever _n_<sub>1</sub>_\<n_<sub>2</sub>. This increase reflects the refinement of a discretization process and naturally arises in the numerical approximation of continuous problems. Matrix sequences typically originate from the discretization of ordinary differential equation (ODEs), partial differential equations (PDEs), and FDEs. Discretizing a differential equation on a computational grid leads to a linear system whose size depends on the number of degrees of freedom. As the mesh is refined to improve accuracy, the number of unknowns increases and the associated coefficient matrices grow in size, producing a sequence indexed by the discretization parameter. These matrices are not arbitrary, but often inherit structural properties from the underlying differential operator, the equation coefficients, and the geometry of the domain.<br>
The structure of a matrix sequence may be explicit, as in the case of Toeplitz, circulant, \(\tau\), or Hankel matrices, or asymptotic and hidden, as described by the theory of GLT matrix sequences. The study of matrix sequences is primarily motivated by their asymptotic spectral behavior. The limiting distribution of eigenvalues and singular values provides essential insight into stability, conditioning, and convergence of numerical schemes, and plays a key role in the design of efficient algorithms, including preconditioned iterative solvers and multi-level methods.<br>
This chapter introduces the core concepts underlying the study of matrix sequences, which provide the spectral and algebraic foundation for the numerical methods developed in this thesis. We first review eigenvalue and singular value distributions as the main tools for describing the asymptotic spectral behavior of large matrices arising from discretized PDEs and FDEs. We then present the notion of convergence for matrix sequences through approximating classes of sequences, which allows one to capture both explicit and hidden asymptotic structures. Finally, we discuss multi- level matrix structures resulting from multi-dimensional discretizations. These topics form the basis of the GLT framework used throughout the thesis; see [42, Chapter 2] for a comprehensive treatment.

### 2.1 Spectral distributions of matrix sequences

In many relevant situations, important asymptotic information about a matrix sequence is captured by its eigenvalue or singular value distribution.
