## Introduction

Fractional calculus (FC) provides a natural and powerful extension of classical differential calculus by allowing derivatives and integrals of non-integer order [100, 105]. Although fractional derivatives already appeared in the early development of calculus, for a long time they remained mainly of theoretical interest. In recent decades, however, fractional differential equations (FDEs) have attracted growing attention from both pure and applied mathematics due to their remarkable ability to model complex phenomena that cannot be adequately described by classical integer-order models [74]. The key feature underlying their success is the intrinsic nonlocality of fractional operators, which introduces memory effects in time and long-range interactions in space, allowing a more faithful representation of many real-world processes. FDEs play a central role in the modeling of anomalous diffusion, a class of transport phenomena characterized by deviations from standard Brownian motion [93]. Such deviations arise naturally in heterogeneous and porous media, plasma physics, viscoelastic materials, image reconstruction, finance, and wave propagation in biological tissues [49, 83]. Depending on the physical mechanism involved, fractional derivatives may act in time, space, or both variables. Time-fractional models typically describe non-Markovian dynamics with memory effects, while space- fractional models capture heavy-tailed jump distributions and long-range spatial interactions [75, 84]. Widely used operators include the Riemann–Liouville, Caputo, and Grünwald–Letnikov derivatives, as well as Riesz operators and the fractional Laplacian, together with their tempered variants, which introduce exponential damping to account for finite-range effects [92, 110].<br>
Despite their modeling flexibility and expressive power, FDEs pose significant analytical and computational challenges. Closed-form solutions are rarely available and, when they exist, they are often unsuitable for practical computations. As a consequence, numerical methods play a fundamental role in the study and simulation of fractional models. Many numerical methods have been developed to efficiently solve FDEs, including finite difference methods [132], finite element methods [30], finite volume methods [36], and spectral methods [47]. Although these approaches are capable of producing accurate approximations, achieving high accuracy typically requires very fine spatial and temporal discretizations. As a consequence, the resulting algebraic systems become large and computationally demanding, especially in multi-dimensional settings. A major source of difficulty lies in the intrinsic nonlocal nature of (fractional) differential operators. Unlike classical local operators, their discretization leads to dense coefficient matrices, resulting in high memory requirements and increased computational cost.<br>
In many applications, fractional models arise not only in direct problems, where the governing equations and parameters are known, but also in inverse problems (IPs). Typical IPs include the identification of unknown sources, coefficients, initial conditions, boundary data, or even the fractional order itself from indirect and often noisy measurements [70]. IPs are particularly challenging because they combine the intrinsic ill-posedness of IPs with the nonlocal behavior of fractional operators.
