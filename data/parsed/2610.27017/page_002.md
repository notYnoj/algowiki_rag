Owing to the nonlocality of fractional differential operators, it is difficult to obtain the analytical solutions of fractional differential equations, and thus developing efficient numerical methods to acquire their numerical solutions has become a research focus in this field.

Discretizing equation (1.1) on a uniform grid using the shifted Grünwald difference scheme in space and the backward Euler scheme in time yields a sequence of Toeplitz-like linear systems. Due to the nonlocality of fractional differential operators, the coefficient matrices are generally dense, which renders the discrete linear systems computationally expensive for general purpose solvers. For solving such linear systems, [5] proposed a class of diagonal-Toeplitz splitting (DTS) iteration methods, implemented by alternately solving shifted diagonal and shifted Toeplitz subsystems. Building upon the DTS framework, [16] used a natural \(\tau\) approximation of the symmetric Toeplitz component and proposed a sine-transform-based splitting preconditioner. More recently, [26] decomposed the coefficient matrix by extracting a scalar matrix, a diagonal compensation matrix, and a block \(\tau\) matrix. The sum of these matrices was then employed to construct a class of \(\tau\)-splitting iteration methods.

For certain structured matrices, the multigrid method exhibits excellent solution efficiency[24, 15, 6]. Under suitable conditions, this method enjoys mesh-independent convergence. This advantageous property has drawn the attention of many researchers to investigate the application of multigrid methods to fractional partial differential equations. For instance, [21] employed the two-grid method to solve the dense Toeplitz linear system obtained after discretizing the one-dimensional constant-coefficient fractional diffusion equation, and utilized fast Toeplitz matrix-vector multiplication to reduce the computational overhead . [9] proved the convergence of the multigrid method for two-dimensional space-fractional diffusion equations with constant diffusion coefficients. Based on the generalized locally Toeplitz (GLT) sequences theory, they further extended this result to the case of uniformly bounded and positive variable diffusion coefficients. Furthermore, numerous studies adopt the GLT theory to analyze the spectral properties of coefficient matrices and thereby design multigrid methods[2, 25, 3]. In addition, [7, 8] extended the symbol-based multigrid analysis for scalar Toeplitz matrices to block-circulant and block-Toeplitz systems generated by matrix-valued symbols, and derived sufficient conditions for the two- grid and V-cycle methods .

The multigrid method consists of a smoother and a coarse-grid correction that complement each other. The coarse-grid correction is responsible for eliminating the low-frequency components of the error. For Toeplitz-like matrices, coarse-grid correction using bilinear interpolation as the transfer operator is effective and general [20]. Correspondingly, the smoother should rapidly attenuate the high-frequency components of the error that are difficult for the coarse grid to represent, and it is usually implemented by classical iterative methods. The weighted Jacobi method is widely adopted due to its simplicity and low computational cost per iteration. With an appropriate relaxation parameter, this method exhibits favorable smoothing ability for high-frequency components of the error[30, 32]. Therefore, the proper choice of the relaxation parameter is the key to constructing an efficient smoother.

Previously, research on the selection of relaxation parameters for the weighted Jacobi smoother has mainly focused on classical elliptic partial differential equations[12, 13]. [3]extended this study to dense Toeplitz- type linear systems arising from the discretization of tempered fractional diffusion equations. They used the spectral information provided by the generating function of the coefficient matrix to expand the range of parameters that guarantee the smoothing property of the Jacobi smoother, and gave estimation formulas for suitable relaxation parameters. However, while their estimation formulas yield feasible relaxation parameters, the resulting parameter may not always match the numerically optimal choice for finite-dimensional linear systems.

In recent years, the integration of machine learning with traditional numerical methods has demonstrated great potential. Machine learning techniques can identify nonlinear relationships between problem characteristics and numerical algorithms from data that are difficult to characterize explicitly, thereby providing new approaches to the design of complex numerical algorithms and the automatic selection of their parameters. Moreover, once trained, machine learning models can typically make rapid predictions for new problems at a low computational cost.
