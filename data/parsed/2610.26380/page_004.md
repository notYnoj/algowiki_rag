<tbc>A second family of algorithms considers the dynamic formulation restricted to one-step look-ahead policies, see Powell and Ryzhov (2012) for an overview. At each round, the decision-maker chooses the evaluation that (approximately) maximizes the expected value of information (EVI), the improvement in the quality of the terminal selection that results from one additional evaluation. For top-1 identification, Chick and Inoue (2001), Chick et al. (2010) derive policies that approximately maximize EVI for both the PCS and EOC objectives. Recently, Zhang et al. (2023) developed a top-_k_ identification policy that maximizes an approximation of the EVI for PCS. Assuming evaluations follow Gaussian distributions with known variances, Frazier et al. (2008) derive the exact one-step look-ahead policy that maximizes the EVI for EOC, known as the knowledge gradient (KG) policy. In our setting, however, KG’s myopia can be particularly detrimental with binary evaluations (Groves and Branke 2019, Li et al. 2024). We return to this point in §2.3.

A third related family of algorithms is based on the expected improvement (EI) approach (Jones et al. 1998), which originated in the Bayesian Optimization literature. Various works have studied its application to top-1 identification (Ryzhov 2016, Qin et al. 2017, Chen and Ryzhov 2019). In its simplest form, EI scores each item by how much its utility, in expectation, exceeds that of the current best candidate and evaluates the item with the highest score. EI’s simplicity is appealing, and although it does not explicitly look ahead, it still reflects future dynamics: evaluating items with high potential to improve on the current best can help inform which evaluations to conduct in future rounds. However, EI does not naturally extend to reflect alternative evaluation forms with different information contents and costs. Furthermore, it chooses which _single_ item to evaluate next, whereas our evaluation alternatives include choosing a _pair_ to compare.

Finally, the third modeling decision concerns the form of the evaluations themselves. In psychometrics, the field of measurement of psychological data, the two most common forms of input for human feedback are ratings, where an evaluation assigns a categorical score to a particular item, and rankings, where a set of items is ordered relative to one another (Thurstone 1927, Likert 1932, Torgerson 1958). We consider each in its parsimonious form--binary thumbs-up/thumbs-down ratings and pairwise comparisons--both among the most common input choices. Restricting to these simple versions of ordinal feedback keeps evaluators’ cognitive load low (Revilla et al. 2014, Kutscher and Eid 2024). The two input forms have distinct pros and cons in our setting. Ratings can be cheaper and easier to provide than pairwise comparisons (White et al. 2024), and a few of them can be an efficient way to establish the ballpark utility of an item. However, ratings are not efficient at distinguishing the relative order of close items, which is essential for differentiating between top-_k_ candidates. Additionally, a rating (say, a thumbs-up) can carry a different meaning to different evaluators, resulting in potential ambiguity when aggregating and interpreting them. Pairwise comparisons, in contrast, only contain _relative_ information about item utilities, and while their interpretation is unambiguous, their usefulness depends heavily on which items are paired. The number of possible pairings is quadratic in the number of items, so starting from an uninformed prior would result in many unhelpful pairings early on.
