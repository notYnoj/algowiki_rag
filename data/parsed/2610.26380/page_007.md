It seeks to reduce uncertainty about the items currently near the top-_k_ cutoff without requiring any direct change to the current top-_k_ set. Doing so pays off in future rounds, since the more effective we are at singling out items near the top-_k_ cutoff, the more effectively we can direct the remaining evaluations.

The difficulty is that mismatch loss does not admit an analysis-friendly expression. The magnitude of an item’s error depends on where it stands relative to the _k_th or (_k_+1)th best of the remaining items, so computing an item’s mismatch loss requires an expectation with respect to the joint utility distribution and an order statistic of the other _I_-1 items, where even the latter itself is intractable. Our resolution is to replace this random top-_k_ threshold with a simpler threshold that is recalibrated each round, such that, in expectation, _k_ items’ utilities will exceed it. With this threshold, an item’s mismatch loss depends only on the marginal distribution of its utility relative to it. This separability gives a clean and interpretable way to account for an evaluation’s effect on _all_ items, those being directly evaluated and, via covariance, those that are not. The sum of these mismatch losses is our surrogate objective, and in each round, the policy chooses the evaluation whose reduction of this loss per evaluation cost is largest.

We also point out a potential trap that arises when combining the two evaluation types, in part because we initially fell into it. Ratings contain information about the average utility of the items, while comparisons do not. Learning about this average reduces the variance of each item’s utility but does not help distinguish the top-_k_, since shifting all utilities together leaves their ordering unchanged. A surrogate that rewards this reduction in variance would therefore unfairly favor ratings over comparisons. We discuss this issue in §3 and show that our surrogate does not reward learning about the average utility, so ratings and comparisons are on equal footing.

As the horizon approaches its end, being forward-looking becomes less valuable, and this strength of the surrogate can become a weakness. In this regime, the myopia of the KG policy would be desirable. We therefore develop the KG policy for our problem, with a switching criterion for the _endgame_, the final rounds where its myopia outperforms the surrogate.

We support our method through both analysis and simulation. In the analysis, although the surrogate objective was constructed from analytical intuition rather than derived, we show that it can be obtained from an upper bound on the EOC, which rigorously justifies and refines our choice of the top-_k_ threshold. We also prove that the resulting policy that allocates by minimizing the surrogate in each round is asymptotically consistent. In simulation, we show that the allocations this policy produces are intuitive and insightful, and that it substantially outperforms existing benchmarks.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: §2 presents the model and defines the objective. §3 develops the surrogate objective, which §4 uses to construct the sequential evaluation procedure and establish its asymptotic consistency. §5 develops the knowledge gradient policy for the endgame and the switching rule.
