The other difficulty is the _modeling_, which connects the application to the analysis and sets the stage for how useful that analysis will be. First, we position the problem in the literature and walk through the modeling decisions involved. We then discuss how we arrived at our solution approach.

The problem of identifying the best few items by allocating evaluation resources is central to the _ranking and selection_ and _best-arm identification_ literatures, where what we refer to as evaluations are typically called samples. Unlike the classical work on multi-armed bandits (Robbins 1952), where rewards are collected while learning, here the only concern is with the quality of the terminal selection, the setting known as _pure exploration_ (Bubeck et al. 2009). Much of this literature focuses on identifying the best (top-1) item (Chen et al. 2000, Frazier et al. 2008, Chen and Ryzhov 2023). We consider the more general problem of top-_k_ identification (Chen et al. 2008, Zhang et al. 2023, Liu et al. 2024).

In this literature, evaluating (sampling) an item typically means directly observing a noisy realization of its utility (quality), which we will refer to as untransformed evaluations. Such untransformed input is natural in applications such as simulation optimization and hyperparameter tuning, where the observed output is itself the object being optimized. In our setting, the utility of each item is latent, since the value of including each item in the chosen set is never evaluated directly. Instead, each item’s quality is observed indirectly through evaluations that take the form of ratings or pairwise comparisons, both of which transform the latent utilities into coarse ordinal outcomes.

To highlight why we do not rely on adapting an established algorithm, we highlight three common modeling and analysis decisions in the ranking and selection literature. We discuss how each decision relates to our problem and, when relevant, how we overcome the additional challenges posed by differences in our setting.

The first modeling decision concerns the terminal objective: how we grade an evaluation allocation policy based on the _k_ items it ultimately selects. The two most common choices are the probability of correct selection (PCS) (Chen et al. 2000, Glynn and Juneja 2004, Li et al. 2025) and the expected opportunity cost (EOC) (Chick and Inoue 2001, Chick et al. 2010, Gao and Chen 2015a). PCS is the probability that the selected set is exactly the true top-_k_, so it treats every incorrect selection equally. EOC is the expected shortfall in utility between the selected set and the true top-_k_ set, making bigger selection mistakes more costly. PCS suits highly specialized or high-stakes decisions where any deviation from the best possible selection is considered a failure. For more common tasks, where a serious mistake can be far costlier than a marginal one, EOC can be the more natural fit. Since EOC is more aligned with the applications we consider, we adopt it in this work.

A second decision concerns how the allocation problem is formulated, with two common formulations at opposite ends of the spectrum. One is static allocation, where the decision variable is the total number of evaluations assigned to each item.
