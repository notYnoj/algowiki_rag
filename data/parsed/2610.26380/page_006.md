In the crowdsourcing literature, these problems are more commonly studied under latent-utility models. Chen et al. (2016) develop an approximate KG algorithm for learning the full ranking of items (as opposed to the top-_k_) from pairwise comparisons. Li et al. (2018), Pérez-Ortiz et al. (2020), Fageot et al. (2026) develop a unified statistical model for combining pairwise comparisons and ratings. Fageot et al. (2026) show empirically that combining ratings and pairwise comparisons can improve estimation of the latent utilities. More directly related to our problem, Li et al. (2018) also give a heuristic approach for choosing evaluations for top-_k_ selection, a slightly modified version of which we use as a benchmark.

Finally, top-_k_ identification from pairwise comparisons is well studied in the dueling-bandit and active- ranking literature. Usually set in the fixed-confidence regime where sampling continues until a target confidence is reached (as opposed to a fixed budget _R_), these algorithms tend to require large numbers of evaluations--far beyond the budgets we consider. See, e.g., Mohajer et al. (2017), Ren et al. (2018), Chen et al. (2018), Heckel et al. (2019), Saha and Gopalan (2019), Ren et al. (2020), Saha and Gopalan (2020), Goldberger and Rudi (2026a) for recent developments and Bengs et al. (2021) for a review.

Instead of explaining our evaluation allocation approach directly, we find it easier to lay out where it came from. Our starting point was the KG policy primarily because, among the existing methods, it is well suited for small-budget problems and can naturally accommodate the combination of evaluation types--our key initial motivation for undertaking this research. However, the KG policy allocates evaluations in each round as if the final selection would immediately follow. Except for the last evaluation, that is not the actual task. The task is to prepare for the terminal selection, and it may be that the best evaluation to do next is decided by how it may inform later evaluation choices. To illustrate how much KG misses out on future dynamics, consider that in its eyes any evaluation whose outcome cannot change the current top-_k_ set has no value at all.

Ideally, we would incorporate the full dynamics into the evaluation choices, but based on the existing literature, anything beyond solving a sequence of state-dependent, static problems seems out of reach. Our key insight leading to our approach came from flipping the perspective from the positive focus on what the decision-maker wants to achieve--choosing the best _k_ items--to a negative perspective--how to reduce errors. There are two ways to err ex-post: including an item that does not belong (_inclusion error_) and excluding one that does (_exclusion error_). Ex-ante, items have an expected _mismatch loss_: the expected magnitude of an inclusion error for items in the current top-_k_ and an exclusion error for those not in it. We then construct a per-round _surrogate_ objective that values uncertainty reduction by how much it reduces the expected mismatch loss. We contrast this with the KG policy that adopts the terminal top-_k_ EOC as each round’s objective. It uses the current uncertainty to assess possible changes in the membership of the top-_k posterior means_. Our method instead holds the current means fixed and values an evaluation by how much it is expected to reduce _uncertainty_. The terminal top-_k_ decision then drives the evaluation choices<tbc>
