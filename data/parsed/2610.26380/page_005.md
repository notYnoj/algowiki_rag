However, once we do have a clear sense of which items are top-_k_ contenders, comparisons can be far more effective than ratings at pinning down their relative ordering (Li et al. 2018). It follows that the two evaluation forms can be especially useful when combined (Fageot et al. 2026). Intuitively, ratings can be used more in early rounds to get a coarse picture of which items are "good" and which are not, and then as that picture forms, comparisons can gradually take over, refining the relative ordering of the good items and narrowing in on the ultimate top-_k_ selection. Our model allows the two evaluation types to differ in accuracy and cost (effort), following the empirical findings of Pérez-Ortiz et al. (2020) and White et al. (2024).

The two evaluation forms can also interact in non-intuitive ways. Consider a simple example with four items, _A_, _B_, _C_, and _D_, whose utilities are independent ex-ante. With ratings alone, a rating of _B_ informs its utility relative to the other items but leaves our beliefs about their utilities unchanged. Now suppose we observe the comparisons \(A\succ B\) and \(C\succ D\), followed by \(B\succ C\). Intuitively, the third comparison suggests that _A_ is preferred to _D_ even though neither was involved in the comparison. The mechanism by which our model reflects this is that comparisons create dependency in the joint posterior over the utilities: the third comparison raises _B_’s estimate and thereby also lifts _A_, while lowering _C_’s estimate and thereby also lowering _D_. This dependency also changes what we learn from a rating. A subsequent rating of _B_ can now influence our posterior beliefs about _A_, _C_, and _D_. In fact, with a more complex comparison history, a rating of _B_ can even reverse our estimated relative ranking of two non-evaluated items, such as _A_ and _D_. Thus, the value of an evaluation for informing the top-_k_ set may not be apparent from what it tells us about the evaluated items alone. Including such dependency in the joint posterior is an opportunity, as it leads to better discrimination between items and hence a more accurate top-_k_ choice. But this added accuracy does not come for free: reflecting the dependency in the dynamic choice of evaluations adds complexity.

Work related to top-_k_ selection tasks in which evaluations are, like ours, binary pairwise comparisons (and possibly also ratings) is scarce in the literature, especially with small budgets. In this setting, one common approach to model evaluations is to assign each possible _evaluation_ its own parameter. For pairwise comparisons, this means a separate parameter specification for each pair of items, representing the probability that one item is preferred to the other. Although the outcomes are binary, this model is still in the untransformed evaluation setting: the outcomes remain a direct noisy realization of the underlying parameter. An alternative approach is the latent-utility model of our setting, where each _item_ has a single value, its utility, that generates the comparisons (and ratings).
