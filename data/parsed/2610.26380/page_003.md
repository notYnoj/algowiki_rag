<tbc>**_N_**=(_N_<sub>1</sub>_,...,N<sub>I</sub>_) for the vector of evaluations assigned to items _i_=1_,...,I_ and _R_ for the total evaluation budget, the allocation problem is

\(min_N\text{EOC}(N)\text{s.t.}\sum_{i=1}^IN_i\le R.\)

This allocation problem faces two main difficulties. The objective EOC(**_N_**), and likewise PCS(**_N_**), generally does not have a closed form that is amenable to analysis. Additionally, the problem is an integer program, which typically makes it intractable to consider the full solution space at scale. In the fully dynamic formulation, one evaluation is chosen at a time, avoiding the combinatorial complexity of the static problem. At each decision point, the state consists of the current posterior distribution over the items’ utilities and the remaining budget, and the action is the next evaluation. The state transition updates the posterior based on the outcome and deducts the evaluation’s cost from the remaining budget. This problem is formulated as a dynamic program which is intractable because the combination of possible evaluations, outcomes, transitions, and future states grows rapidly with the horizon (Frazier et al. 2008, Hong et al. 2021, Zhang et al. 2023).

Our base formulation is fully dynamic with evaluations chosen sequentially. However, allocating one evaluation at a time can have logistical obstacles. There may be considerable overhead in assigning and conducting evaluations, which can favor giving an evaluator a _batch_ of tasks at once. We therefore also consider a hybrid approach in §6, where a batch of evaluations is chosen in each round. This sits between the two formulations and inherits both difficulties. Within a round, the allocation is an integer program, each linked through the between-round dynamics.

The complexity of the problem under both formulations has given rise to a variety of solution approaches. Before turning to our own, we discuss the three most relevant well-studied families, all developed in the untransformed evaluation setting. The first is a family of algorithms known as Optimal Computing Budget Allocation (OCBA), originating with Chen et al. (1997, 2000), Glynn and Juneja (2004). At a high level, the algorithm loops through three steps: (i) take the items’ current mean estimates as if they were the ground truth utilities, (ii) choose the next evaluation(s) by determining the static allocation that would maximize PCS (or minimize EOC), and (iii) update the estimates of the means based on the outcome. However, step (ii) is intractable due to the complexities of the static allocation problem discussed above. To overcome this, it is common to consider the asymptotic regime where \(R\rightarrow\infty\) and find the allocation proportions that (approximately) optimize the resulting problem. Eventually, the estimated means will converge to the true means, and thus the evaluation proportions will converge to the (approximately) optimal proportions. The majority of the OCBA literature studies the top-1 setting with PCS, with extensions to top-1 EOC (He et al. 2007, Gao et al. 2017), as well as to top-_k_ PCS (Chen et al. 2008, Gao and Chen 2015b, Zhang et al. 2016) and top-_k_ EOC (Gao and Chen 2015a). Such approaches tend to perform better in large-budget settings. In fact, for small budgets, it has been demonstrated that such allocation procedures may even perform worse than equal allocation (Peng et al. 2018, Liu et al. 2024).
