To illustrate how to apply the theorem, consider again the example from before. We apply Theorem 1.2 with _c_=9, and for some arbitrarily small constant \(\epsilon\). The theorem then chooses some constant \(k=k(c,\epsilon)\). There is indeed some _k_-sum in _A_ that is \(\Omega(|A|^{k-1-c})\)-popular (as there are |_A|<sup>k</sup>k_-sums taking at most _k|A_|<sup>10</sup> distinct values), satisfying the premise of the theorem. Theorem 1.2 then implies that a large part of _A_ is contained in some set _H_ of size at most _K|A_|<sup>10</sup> and doubling \(|H+H|\le K|H|\). Here, the doubling \(K=O_{c,\epsilon}(|A|^\epsilon)\) is controlled by the initial choice of \(\epsilon\). I.e., we can force _H_ to be arbitrarily well-structured (as long as _K_ is polynomially large), at the only cost of increasing the constant _k_. Theorem 1.2 turns out to be a surprisingly powerful tool that is likely to find more applications beyond our specific interest related to the Min-Plus Convolution problem.

The proof of Theorem 1.2 builds on the _asymmetric_ BSG Theorem of Tao and Vu \[TV06\], a significantly more intricate extension of BSG to pairs of sets of different sizes. In fact, our result is the first in fine-grained complexity, and even theoretical computer science as a whole, that exploits the asymmetric BSG Theorem, to our knowledge.

At a very high level, the proof of Theorem 1.2 is by increasing _k_ gradually and tracking the sets of popular \(\ell\)-sums for \(\ell=4,5,\dots,k\). By a pigeonhole argument, at some point, the popular \(\ell\)-sums and \((\ell+1)\)-sums correlate (both in size and structurally), at which point the asymmetric BSG theorem can be applied to extract the desired low-doubling set. We provide a detailed proof in Section 3. We also face the extra challenge that for our algorithmic applications, it does not suffice to know that _H_ exists, but we must be able to _compute H_. Therefore, along the way we also devise an efficient randomized algorithm to find _H_ as in Theorem 1.2 (see Theorem 3.5). Notably, to this end we also had to design an efficient algorithm for the asymmetric BSG Theorem (see Section 7). This black box will certainly be handy for other algorithms in the future.

Finally, let us remark that there are some other higher-order variants of BSG that have been studied in additive combinatorics \[SSV05, BC11, Shk14, Lee25\]. However, unfortunately none of these existing results applies in the weakly structured regime required for our applications (though some of the same ideas, e.g. as in \[BK11, Shk14\], turn out useful for the proof of Theorem 1.2 as well). This concludes our additive combinatorics excursus.

## 1.3 Low-Rank 3SUM

Equipped with Theorem 1.2 we are now in a position to start implementing the structure-versus- randomness approach following Fischer \[Fis26\]. Recall from before that the foundation of Fischer’s proof is to identify a certain _rank_ parameter, and to design a truly subcubic-time algorithm for computing the min-plus product of _low-rank_ matrices. An immediate challenge of extending Fischer’s proof to Min-Plus Convolution is the lack of such a rank notion. It is unclear what should be the rank of a sequence or a set with respect to normal convolutions, let alone Min-Plus Convolution.

A main contribution of our paper is to identify a suitable notion of rank for sequences, which is strong enough (so that unstructured instances are sufficiently pseudo-random to complete our reductions) but not overpowering (so we can still design a fast algorithm for Min-Plus Convolution on low-rank sequences).

In fact, we will directly work with _3SUM_, a harder problem than Min-Plus Convolution.
