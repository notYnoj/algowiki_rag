Fix parameters \(1\le B,P\le N\) whose values we will determine later. We may assume \(U\ge 2\) and that _B_ is a power of two. We begin by splitting the inner dimension \[_N_\] into _O(N/B_) contiguous blocks of size at most _B_. We call these blocks level-0 blocks, and denote one such block by _K_<sup>(0)</sup>. At level \(0\le\ell\le logB\), each level-0 block is split into at most \(2^\ell\) contiguous blocks of size at most \(B^{(\ell)}:=B/2^\ell\). Note that _B=B_<sup>(0)</sup>. We let \(K^{(\ell)}\) denote a level-\(\ell\) block and let \(h\le logB\) denote the last level that we search. As before, we say that \(K^{(\ell)}\) is a block witness for (_i,j_) if there exists \(k\in K^{(\ell)}\) such that _A_\[_i,k_\]+_B_\[_k,j_\]=_C_\[_i,j_\]. For each triple (_i,k,j_), define \(\Delta_{ikj}=A[i,k]+B[k,j]-C[i,j]\), so that we are looking, for each (_i,j_), for some _k_ such that \(\Delta_{ikj}=0\).

Let us begin by recalling the properties required by the randomized algorithm. Let P denote all primes between \[_P,_2_P_\]. Consider some prime \(p\in\mathcal{P}\). We say a block \(K^{(\ell)}\) is a candidate block witness for (_i,j_) with respect to _p_ if there exist \(k\in K^{(\ell)}\) such that \(A[i,k]+B[k,j]\equiv C[i,j]modp\), and \(K^{(\ell)}\) is a false candidate if it is a candidate block witness but not a block witness. Following the proof of Claim 3.3, we can show that for a randomly chosen prime \(p\in\mathcal{P}\), any block \(K^{(\ell)}\) is a false candidate with probability at most \(O\left(\frac{B^{(\ell)}logU}{P}\right)\). Thus, we may choose \(P=\Theta(BlogU)\) to bound this probability by a constant, say 1/2. In fact, by independently selecting _s_ random primes \(p\in\mathcal{P}\), one can reduce this probability to 2<sup>-_s_</sup>. To derandomize the above approach, we will instead show how to deterministically find primes \(p\in\mathcal{P}\) that reduce the number of false candidates.

At level \(\ell\), we will ensure that the number of false candidates is upper bounded by a function \(\Phi^{(\ell)}:=\Theta\left(\frac{N^3}{B2^{7\ell}}\right)\), a bound which is trivially satisfied at \(\ell=0\). We will deterministically choose a constant number of primes such that the number of false level \(\ell\) candidates decreases by a factor of 2<sup>7</sup>. We know that a random prime will eliminate all but a \(O\left(\frac{B^{(\ell)}logU}{P}\right)=O(2^{-\ell})\) fraction of false candidates. Thus, a natural attempt is to deterministically choose a prime _p_ that eliminates the largest fraction of false candidates (or minimize \(\Phi^{(\ell)}\)). Unfortunately, both the quantity of false candidates eliminated by a given prime _p_ (and \(\Phi^{(\ell)}\)) may be hard to compute, as it may involve checking whether a candidate is genuine or not (see \[JPSX26\] for further discussion). Instead, we find a function _Z(p_) that is a constant-factor approximation of \(\Phi^{(\ell)}\) (Lemma 3.8) and we can efficiently compute (Lemma 3.9). This indirect approach of finding an estimate of the desired bound that is easier to compute is similar to \[JPSX26\], although our estimator is distinct and tailored to our specific application. Thus, choosing a prime _p_ that minimizes _Z(p_) suffices to ensure that the number of false candidates decreases by a factor of \(O(2^{-\ell})\). Iteratively choosing a constant number of such primes at level \(\ell\) (each time ensuring we minimize _Z(p_)) decreases \(\Phi^{(\ell)}\) by the desired \(2^{7\ell}\) factor.

Given the above hierarchy, we proceed to search blocks in a depth-first order. For any given pair (_i,j_), iterate over all level 0 blocks. At each block, we will check whether it is a candidate block (as before with \[AV26\]), and if so process its children in depth-first order. At the final level of the hierarchy, we enumerate elements in all candidate blocks, and terminate the search for (_i,j_) whenever a true witness is found. The depth-first order of the traversal ensures that at level \(\ell\), at most 1 true block witness is examined. Thus, the total number of level \(\ell\) blocks examined at level \(\ell\) is at most \(O(N^2+\Phi^{(\ell-1)})\), since we must also examine the children of false level \(\ell-1\) candidates. Since \(\Phi^{(\ell)}\) is decreasing in \(\ell\), our algorithm makes progressively fewer queries to the matrix product computed by \[AV26\] at higher levels \(\ell\), but incurs higher preprocessing cost as the inner dimension \(D^{(\ell)}:=B^{(\ell)}P\) is decreasing in \(\ell\). Thus, instead of using Theorem 1.2, we use the data structure version of Theorem 25 (see Corollary 31 of \[AV26\]) which allows us to obtain faster preprocessing at the cost of slower queries.

We elaborate on the high-level approach described above below.

## Step 1: Finding a Good Modulus.
