Quantum minimum finding shows that coherent access can reduce the number of queries needed for selection [8]. A coherent comparison acts on a superposition of design pairs, and the resulting amplitudes can interfere before measurement. An algorithm can use this interference to favor better ranks in its output distribution. For OO, the question is whether this capability improves alignment probability at a fixed comparison budget and how any improvement depends on the size of the good-enough set. However, accepting more designs as good enough also makes classical selection easier. Assessing a quantum benefit therefore requires comparison with the classical optimum for the same target and comparison budget.

Asymptotic query bounds for quantum selection [8, 9] do not determine the exact alignment probability at a small, fixed comparison budget. Fixed-budget quantum simulation optimization has also been studied through convergence rates for the expected optimality gap under quantum simulation access [10]. That criterion measures loss in performance value, whereas alignment measures whether the selected rank meets the target. To the best of our knowledge, prior work has not determined the exact alignment optimum over all admissible classical or quantum strategies in the comparison model. This optimum is needed to distinguish a benefit of coherent access from a weakness of a particular classical selection rule.

To address this gap, we formulate quantum ordinal optimization (QOO) as a class of problems that maximize the worst-case alignment probability under a specified oracle model and query budget. The oracle, selected-set size and alignment level are part of the problem specification. As a first instance, we study exact coherent pairwise comparisons with one selected design and alignment level one. Exact comparisons separate selection from estimation error, while a single output reduces alignment to meeting the rank target. For each budget and good-enough set, the objective is the largest alignment probability that can be guaranteed for every strict order of the designs. The budget counts only comparison queries. Estimation error and implementation cost require further models, as discussed in Section 8.

In this comparison problem, we establish the classical optimum for every budget smaller than the number of designs and determine the one-comparison quantum optimum. One comparison is the smallest budget at which selection can depend on the unknown order, yet it already permits a quantum gain. We report each value as an equivalent blind-pick size, the number of designs that a blind pick would need to reach the same alignment probability, which need not be an integer. Our main results are as follows.

1. For every good-enough set and every budget smaller than the number of designs, we prove that the optimal alignment probability with exact classical comparisons equals that of a blind pick whose size exceeds the budget by one. A knockout among the blindly picked designs attains this probability. Although the comparisons can involve twice as many designs as their number, no adaptive procedure that returns one design does better than this blind pick when comparisons are exact.<tbc>

2. Averaged over a random order, the law of the rank of the design selected by a quantum algorithm is a polynomial in the rank. Its degree is at most twice the comparison budget or one less than the number of designs, whichever is smaller, and this bound is attained. By contrast, the degree for classical algorithms is at most the comparison budget. At one comparison the larger quantum degree is necessary for any gain.<tbc>
