_Upper bound._ By Proposition 1 it suffices to bound the success averaged over a uniform order. Fixing the private randomness turns an algorithm into a deterministic comparison tree with at most _q_ comparisons on every branch, and the average success of the algorithm is the average over seeds of the average success of these trees. Fix a tree and a leaf \(\ell\) of positive probability. Its comparison graph has at most _q_ distinct edges, and a connected graph on _k_ vertices has at least _k_-1 edges, so the component of the output has \(k\le q+1\) vertices. Let _V_<sub>1</sub>_,...,V_<sub>_q_+1</sub> be a uniformly random sequence of distinct ranks. The set {_V_<sub>1</sub>_,...,V<sub>k</sub>_} is a uniform _k_-subset, and for every \(j\le k\) its _j_-th smallest element is at least min(_V_<sub>1</sub>_,...,V<sub>k</sub>_), which is at least min(_V_<sub>1</sub>_,...,V_<sub>_q_+1</sub>). Each term of (6) is the law of the _j_-th smallest element of a uniform _k_-subset, so

\(Pr[x_a\le r|E_\ell]\le Pr[min(V_1,\dots,V_{q+1})\le r]=1-\frac{\genfrac(){pt}{2}{n-r}{q+1}}{\genfrac(){pt}{2}{n}{q+1}}.\)

Averaging over leaves and seeds gives the bound. Repeated or reversed comparisons add no distinct edge, and a self-comparison adds no edge between distinct labels. Leaves with contradictory answers have probability zero. An output that was never compared has _k_=1 and rank law 1_/n_.

**Corollary 1.** _(i) For_ \(n\ge q+1\) _and_ \(1\le r\le n-q\)_, the equivalent blind-pick size of q exact classical comparisons is exactly_ \(s_\text{eq}^C(n,r)=q+1\)_. (ii) The rank law of every symmetrized classical algorithm with budget q is, on the rank grid, a polynomial in the rank of degree at most_ min(_q,n_-1).

_Proof._ Part (i) is (4), because \(q+1\le n-r+1\) lies in the range where \(B(n,r,\cdot)\) is strictly increasing. For (ii), the symmetrized rank law is the average of (6) over leaves weighted by their probabilities, and each term of (6) has degree \(k-1\le q\) in _t_. Every function on _n_ points is a polynomial of degree at most _n_-1.

Theorem 1 determines the best comparison-based procedure when comparisons are exact and one design is selected. No adaptive choice of pairs improves on a blind pick of _q_+1 designs followed by a knockout, although _q_ comparisons can involve up to 2_q_ designs. Any procedure that returns the best of the same _q_+1 designs with _q_ comparisons attains the same value, whether it compares the current winner with the next design, as in the proof, or plays a bracket. The blind-pick formula itself is classical \[2, 3\], and the winner-rank law of tournament selection without replacement gives the same expression \[35\].

For the upper bound, conditioning on a leaf of a comparison tree with shuffled inputs appears in work on approximate selection with unreliable comparisons \[36\], and lower bounds there and in \[37\] fix the asymptotic order of the number of comparisons. The proof of Theorem 1 is elementary. It uses leaf conditioning, a count of linear extensions and a stochastic comparison of order statistics. The theorem gives the optimum over all adaptive procedures at every budget \(q\le n-1\) and every good-enough set. For \(q\ge n-1\) a knockout of all _n_ designs returns the best one, so _C<sub>q</sub>(n,r_)=1 for every \(r\ge 1\). In the scenario-by-scenario comparison of selection rules proposed in \[2\], Theorem 1 gives the best rule for the scenario of exact comparisons and one selected design.
