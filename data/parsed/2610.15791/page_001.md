# Quantum ordinal optimization under a fixed comparison budget

Hongyu Wu

Mike Wiegers Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering, Kansas State University, Manhattan, KS 66506, USA

Ordinal optimization selects designs that are good enough rather than optimal when evaluation is expensive and the budget is small. The alignment probability measures the chance that the selected set contains at least a prescribed number of truly good designs. We formulate quantum ordinal optimization as a class of problems that maximize this probability under a fixed oracle budget. We study exact, coherent pairwise comparisons with one selected design as a first instance, with the budget counted in comparison queries. For every classical comparison budget smaller than the number of designs, we prove that a knockout among uniformly sampled designs attains the optimum. The sample is drawn without replacement and contains one more design than the number of comparisons. For a uniformly random order, the selected design’s rank has a polynomial probability law. At the same comparison budget, the maximum quantum degree is at most twice the classical maximum, a factor attained whenever there are more than twice as many designs as comparisons, so a quantum algorithm can realize rank laws that no classical algorithm with the same budget can. We determine the quantum optimum with one comparison for every number of designs and every good-enough set. For selecting the best of _n_ designs, the optimal success probability with one quantum comparison is approximately 3.30_/n_ for large _n_, compared with 2_/n_ for one classical comparison. When the best design is sought, one quantum comparison gives a higher alignment probability than two classical comparisons whenever there are more than nine designs.

### 1 Introduction

Many design and operational decisions require a choice from a large finite set of designs whose performance is estimated through expensive simulation. Ordinal optimization (OO) addresses such problems by accepting good-enough designs and comparing their order rather than their values [1, 2]. OO measures selection success by the alignment probability, the probability that the selected set contains at least a prescribed number of truly good-enough designs. This required number is called the alignment level [3]. In constrained OO, a selected design needs to be feasible.
