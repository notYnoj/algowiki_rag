<tbc>work [11], we introduced an algorithm based on Houdayer cluster updates [12] to improve sampling from biased optimizers. The approach operates through rejection-free cluster exchanges at zero temperature: given two independent sets {_s<sub>i</sub>_} and {_s<sub>j</sub>_}, the algorithm computes their symmetric difference \(\Delta_{ij}=s_i\oplus s_j\) (vertices present in exactly one solution), identifies the _c_ connected components (clusters) in the induced subgraph \(G[\Delta_{ij}]\), which we refer to as the disagreement graph, and generates 2_<sup>c</sup>_ candidate configurations by independently choosing which clusters to exchange. This mechanism preserves solution quality, and can occasionally improve it (Section IIID), while enabling large-scale structural rearrangements that can reach configurations unavailable to local moves.

While fundamentally non-ergodic, i.e., offering no guarantee of complete manifold coverage, the method proved effective at reducing sampling bias in quantum annealing data from D-Wave systems and improving diversity for classical spin-glass solvers [11], whose sampling biases are documented in Refs. [8, 9, 13, 14].

The present work applies this approach to MIS measurement records from Rydberg atom arrays. Applied to two independent sets, the cluster exchange keeps every candidate independent by construction (Section IIC), so none is rejected as infeasible. We use the method to ask how much ground-state information a neutral atom measurement record contains beyond the solutions the device reports. Because the instances studied here come with exact reference optima and exact ground-state degeneracies, we can measure both how many additional optimal configurations the record yields and what fraction of the true manifold that represents. We source initial solution pools from quantum hardware runs on QuEra’s Aquila quantum device, using quantum measurements as high-quality seeds for classical cluster-based exploration. Raw hardware measurements may select adjacent atoms, and these are repaired through a two-stage greedy procedure [7]. The first stage iteratively removes the vertices with most conflicts until independence is restored, and the second greedily adds vertices that maintain independence to recover maximality. This repair process runs in O(|_E_|) time per candidate and typically preserves optimal or near-optimal set sizes.

We evaluate our approach on 43 problem instances across three categories whose hardness parameter spans several orders of magnitude: Benchmark instances (137 atoms each), hard instances [7] that classical kernelization alone cannot reduce to an empty kernel (115 - 231 atoms), and standard instances (7 - 205 atoms). The quantum seeds come from measurements on QuEra’s Rydberg quantum device, with solution counts ranging from 2 to 493 distinct configurations per instance at the size the hardware reached. Our classical post-processing systematically processes quantum solution pairs through Houdayer cluster updates, applying adaptive stopping criteria to balance manifold exploration against computational cost.

The results demonstrate amplification across the full<tbc>

<tbc>dataset: solution diversity increases by factors ranging from \(1\times\) to \(11923\times\), with the quantum seed pool expanded to contain hundreds or thousands of distinct configurations at the seed size in seconds to minutes on commodity hardware [15]. Discovery dynamics reveal that manifold exploration proceeds efficiently until saturation, with most new solutions discovered within the first few hundred to few thousand pair proceedings. We develop stopping criteria based on discovery rate saturation (halt when the number of newly discovered solutions over a sliding window of 500 consecutive pairs falls below 0.1% of the current pool size) and computational budgets (10<sup>5</sup> pair limit), enabling practical termination without exhaustive enumeration.

For three instances (`L12\_seed829`, `L17\_seed424`, and `L17\_seed46`), the classical algorithm discovered independent sets _larger_ than those found by quantum hardware yielding solution quality improvements, not merely diversity amplification within a known manifold. These cases involve size increases from 35 to 36 atoms (`L12\_seed829`) and from 68 and 67 to 70 atoms (`L17` instances), demonstrating that classical post-processing can improve upon quantum optimization results for analog quantum optimizers that are inherently noisy. As we detail below, these improvements reach the exact optimum in one case and come within one atom of it in the other two.

This work makes the following contributions:

1. **How much a measurement record contains:** quantification, across 43 instances spanning three hardness families, of the additional distinct configurations recoverable from a neutral atom measurement record beyond those the device reports, a median of \(24.3\times\) overall and \(8.9\times\) over the 35 instances whose seeds were already optimal, reaching \(11923\times\) for `HP1478`, for 18 minutes of laptop time across the entire dataset, measured end to end.<tbc>

2. **Coverage measured against exact degeneracies:** because exact ground-state degeneracies are available for these instances, the recovered _fraction_ of the manifold is measured rather than bounded, a median of 21% and provably complete on six instances. This converts the non-ergodicity of the method from a disclaimer into a measured quantity, and shows that completeness is attainable precisely when the manifold is small enough for a fixed seed budget to enumerate.<tbc>

3. **An audit of what the hardware determined:** comparison against exact reference optima shows the quantum seed pool sat strictly below the true optimum on 8 of the 43 instances, including six of the nine hard instances. On three of these, classical recombination discovered strictly larger independent sets than the hardware found, reaching the exact optimum on one. Distinguishing an amplified true manifold from an amplified suboptimal one requires such a reference.
