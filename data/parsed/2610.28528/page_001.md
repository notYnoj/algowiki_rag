# Efficient Ground-State Manifold Amplification for Rydberg Atom Arrays via Houdayer Cluster Updates

We demonstrate a hybrid quantum-classical approach for exploring ground-state manifolds of maximum independent set problems, in which quantum hardware supplies the initial solutions and an entirely classical post-processing algorithm expands them, increasing the solution diversity of quantum hardware measurements by several orders of magnitude. Quantum optimization devices, while capable of generating high-quality solutions, can suffer from fundamental sampling limitations. This is particularly problematic for applications that strongly depend on a large variety of uncorrelated optimal solutions. We apply the Houdayer cluster update algorithm to systematically expand quantum-generated solution sets through classical post-processing. We validate our approach on a test bed of 43 problem instances from QuEra’s Aquila analog annealing device, achieving diversity amplification factors, defined as the increase in the number of distinct solutions at a given set size, of up to \(11923\times\) with a median of \(24.3\times\) across different problem scales and hardness parameters. Because exact ground-state degeneracies are known for these instances, the recovered fraction of the manifold can be measured rather than bounded: post-processing recovers a median of 21% of the ground-state manifold, and provably all of it for six instances. We also demonstrate that, in some cases, the classical post-processing can discover independent sets larger than those found by the quantum hardware, thus improving on solution quality and not just diversity. While the method is non-ergodic and cannot guarantee complete manifold coverage, it consistently discovers a larger number of high-quality, and in some cases strictly better, configurations than quantum sampling alone. This work demonstrates how simple classical post-processing can enhance the utility of quantum optimization results.

### I. INTRODUCTION

The maximum independent set (MIS) problem is a paradigmatic NP-hard combinatorial optimization problem [1], which remains NP-hard on unit disk graphs [2], with applications spanning network design, computational biology, and portfolio optimization. Given an undirected graph _G_=(_V,E_), the goal is to find the largest subset \(S\subseteq V\) such that no two vertices in _S_ are adjacent. Neutral atom arrays with programmable geometries have emerged as a leading platform for quantum optimization of MIS problems [3-5]. In these systems, atoms are positioned with sub-micrometer precision and coupled via Rydberg blockade interactions that naturally encode the MIS problem on unit disk graphs, as originally proposed in Ref. [3]. The blockade radius _r<sub>b</sub>_ defines the graph connectivity: edges connect atoms within distance _r<sub>b</sub>_, while independence constraints arise from the strong energetic suppression, rather than strict impossibility, of simultaneous Rydberg excitation within the blockade sphere. Recent experiments on Rydberg atom arrays [4], including QuEra’s Aquila quantum device [6, 7], have demonstrated quantum annealing algorithms (QAA) capable of generating high-quality solutions of MIS problems with hundreds of atoms.

However, quantum optimization devices face a fundamental sampling limitation. While capable of accessing

optimal or near-optimal configurations, quantum measurements from a typical hardware run produce only tens to hundreds of distinct (degenerate) solutions (for example, ranging from 2 to 493 in the data set of Ref. [7] studied here). Moreover, quantum annealers have been shown to be biased samplers, both on superconducting hardware [8, 9] and on Rydberg atom arrays [10]: they do not find all ground states with equal probability. This biased sampling becomes problematic when the ground- state manifold contains exponentially many degenerate optimal configurations as is often the case for the MIS. However, the classical post-processing presented in this work reveals that the aforementioned unit disk graph instances can have as many as 10<sup>3</sup> to 10<sup>6</sup> distinct maximum independent sets, with the largest manifold explored containing nearly 2 million optimal configurations. These counts are specific to instances of a few hundred atoms, and the degeneracy grows rapidly with size, so larger devices should make the sampling problem worse rather than better. The limited sampling diversity of quantum hardware leaves vast regions of these degenerate manifolds unexplored, restricting practical utility for applications requiring multiple diverse solutions such as ensemble methods and entropy calculations, design space exploration, post-optimization inclusion of constraints, or robustness analysis, to name a few.
