This has been established both on superconducting annealing hardware \[8, 9\] and, for Rydberg atom arrays of the kind used here, in Ref. \[10\]. That Houdayer cluster updates improve precisely this class of sampling bias was established extensively for spin-glass optimizers in earlier work \[11\]. Here we apply the same principle to independent sets returned by quantum hardware, with the added requirement that the hard independence constraint of the MIS be respected throughout. It is this limited and non-uniform coverage that motivates classical post-processing.

## C. Houdayer Cluster Updates

The Houdayer cluster update algorithm \[12\] was originally developed for two-dimensional spin-glass systems and has since been extended to higher-dimensional binary systems in regimes (e.g., temperature or dimensionality) where clusters do not percolate \[18\] and thus are effective in resampling configurations of the system and improving thermalization when simulated via Monte Carlo methods. More recently, it has been applied to improve sampling from biased quantum and classical optimizers \[11\]. The core idea is to recombine pairs of solutions through non-local cluster exchanges, generating many new candidate configurations from a single pair in a rejection-free manner (i.e., not affecting the combined energy of the system).

Given two solution configurations _s<sub>i</sub>_ and _s<sub>j</sub>_, the algorithm first computes their symmetric difference \(\Delta_{ij}=s_i\oplus s_j\), i.e., the set of vertices on which the two solutions disagree. The agreement backbone \(s_i\cap s_j\) remains fixed throughout. The induced subgraph \(G[\Delta_{ij}]\) is then decomposed into _c_ connected components, each representing an independent region of disagreement. Because these components are disconnected in \(G[\Delta_{ij}]\), the vertex assignments within each cluster can be swapped independently without affecting any other cluster. Enumerating every binary exchange pattern, each specifying for each cluster whether to take vertices from _s<sub>i</sub>_ or _s<sub>j</sub>_, yields 2_<sup>c</sup>_ candidate solutions from a single pair. Every such candidate is independent by construction, because each cluster contributes vertices from one parent only, no edge of _G_ joins distinct components of \(G[\Delta_{ij}]\), and a backbone vertex cannot be adjacent to a selected vertex of either parent. When _s<sub>i</sub>_ and _s<sub>j</sub>_ have equal size, a further consequence follows. Complementary candidates, obtained by exchanging every cluster, have total size |_s<sub>i</sub>_|+|_s<sub>j</sub>_|, so if that common size is the maximum independent set size neither candidate can exceed it and both must equal it. Every cluster of two maximum independent sets is therefore balanced between the two parents, and the exchange is size-preserving without any rejection step. If instead the parents share a suboptimal size, individual clusters may be imbalanced, and taking the larger side of such a<tbc>

<tbc>cluster yields a strictly larger independent set. This is the mechanism behind the quality improvements of Section IIID: for `L12\_seed829`, 17 of the 2016 seed pairs contain at least one imbalanced cluster, and for `L17\_seed46` all 6 do, and each of those yields a valid solution at least one vertex larger than the seeds. For suboptimal parents a candidate can also be non-maximal, and the repair of Section IID then adds vertices. All 2_<sup>c</sup>_ patterns are enumerated without a cap on _c_, whose largest observed value was 14.

Starting from the initial quantum seed pool S<sub>0</sub> at the best-found size _k_, the algorithm iterates over the \(\genfrac(){pt}{2}{|\mathcal{S}_0|}{2}\) pairs drawn from the seed pool, collecting newly discovered solutions of size _k_ into the output pool as they are found. Discovered solutions are not themselves re-paired: the pair list is fixed by S<sub>0</sub>, so the number of exchange operations is bounded a priori rather than growing with the pool, and within a pass every recovered configuration is a single recombination of two of its seeds. Solutions are stored as frozen sets of vertex indices for O(1) deduplication via hash lookup, so each configuration is counted only once regardless of how many pairs produce it. Candidates larger than _k_ are collected separately and, once the pass ends, the largest of them seed a new pass at that size, repeated until a pass finds nothing larger. These further passes are what enable classical post-processing to surpass the size of the seed pool on hard instances. The algorithm terminates with an expanded pool S<sub>final</sub> of optimum size \(k_\mathrm{final}\ge k\), and the amplification factor _A_=|S<sub>final</sub>|/|S<sub>0</sub>| is recorded.

## D. MIS Repair

Two operations in the pipeline can yield a set that is not a maximal independent set: raw hardware measurements, which may select adjacent atoms when the blockade is imperfect, and cluster exchange, which preserves independence (Section IIC) but can leave a vertex addable. We handle both with a two-stage greedy procedure adapted from Ref. \[7\]. Only its first stage is ever required for raw measurements, and only its second for exchange candidates. In the first stage, vertices are removed iteratively in order of decreasing conflict degree, the number of neighbors also in the current set, until no violations remain. This greedy ordering minimizes the number of removals needed to reach feasibility. In the second stage, vertices outside the current set are added greedily in order of increasing degree in the remaining graph, so that each addition leaves as many future candidates unblocked as possible. This extension continues until no further vertex can be added without creating a conflict, restoring maximality. The full procedure runs in O(|_E_|) time, with _E_ the number of edges of the graph, per candidate and in practice preserves the optimal set size for the large majority of repaired solutions. A small fraction of candidates repair to a smaller-than-optimal set and are discarded.
