• **Algorithmic improvement:** The most obvious achievement of our main result is a quasi-polynomial improvement in running time. While the quantitative improvement is modest, in the field of fine- grained complexity quasi-polynomial [Wil18, LW17, AFK<sup>+</sup>24] and even smaller improvements (e.g., [GMW19, JX24, DMM<sup>+</sup>25]) are often celebrated as significant steps towards pinpointing the complexity of fundamental problems (and approximate ED and LCS are certainly fundamental!). More importantly, perhaps, this improvement shows the algorithmic potential of our new structural insights (see discussion below on "tree total deviation").<tbc>

• **Separation between** \((1+\varepsilon)\)**-Approximate and Exact Computation (both ED and LCS):** For exact ED and LCS, prior work [AHWW16, AB18] shows that even shaving off a few logarithmic factors is highly unlikely without refuting established fine-grained complexity assumptions. Although a sequence of approximation algorithms faster than this lower bound have recently been known [CDG<sup>+</sup>20, BEG<sup>+</sup>21, And20, BR20, GRS20, KS20b, AN20], none of them achieve an approximation factor better than 3. Therefore, subject to certain circuit complexity hardness assumptions (see [AHWW16, AB18] for details), our result is the first to separate the complexity of \((1+\varepsilon)\)-approximation from exact computation of ED and LCS.<tbc>

• **Separation Between Randomized and Deterministic Algorithms (LCS):** For LCS, obtaining similar approximation-time tradeoffs using _deterministic_ algorithms would imply breakthrough circuit lower bounds [CGL<sup>+</sup>19] (see also [AB17, AR18]). Indeed, many randomized approximation algorithms for both LCS and ED have been proposed, but it was not clear whether this randomization was necessary. Our algorithm is the first to break the frontier of deterministic lower bounds by using randomization, thereby demonstrating a fundamental separation: subject to certain circuit complexity hardness assumptions (see [CGL<sup>+</sup>19]), randomized algorithms for approximate LCS are strictly faster than their deterministic counterparts.

## Additional related Work

In addition to aforementioned works on approximate ED and LCS on worst case inputs, many works also give improved guarantees under assumptions on the inputs such as small edit distance<sup>4</sup> [CGK16, GKS19, KS20a, BCR20, BCFN22a, BCFN22c, GKKS22, KS23, RSSS25], semi-random strings [AK12, Kus19, BSS20], or edit distance to error correcting codewords [HRS19]. See also e.g., [KR09, CGKK18, HSS19, BGS21, KPS21, CFH<sup>+</sup>21, JNW21, BCFN22b, KS24, BCFK24] for additional related works in other models.

Ideas related to the breakthrough constant factor approximation algorithm for edit distance have also been applied to related problems, such as tree edit distance [SS22] and, more recently, Frechet distance [CHZ25]. For tree edit distance, we leave it for future work whether our techniques can also apply to improve [BGHS19]’s state of the art (nearly) quadratic running time for \((1+\varepsilon)\)-approximation. For the Frechet distance, existing hardness of approximation results rule out polynomial savings assuming SETH [Bri14], and it remains open whether quasi-polynomial saving is possible.

Our work gives a quasi-polynomial improvement in running times for central problems of interest in fine-grained complexity. Other notable examples of fine-grained complexity with quasi-polynomial improvements include All-Pairs Shortest Paths [Wil18] (equivalent to the Min-plus matrix multiplication), online matrix-vector multiplication [LW17], and combinatorial boolean matrix multiplication [AFK<sup>+</sup>24].

<sup>4</sup>The careful reader may notice that some of these citations do not explicitly mention "small edit distance" in the title, but they give fast algorithms for solving a "gap edit distance" problem of distinguishing e.g. edit distance _k_ vs _k_<sup>1.5</sup>; this kind of gap problem is only non-trivial when \(k\checkmark n\).
