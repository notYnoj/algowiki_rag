# From Hamilton-Jacobi–Bellman to Riccati via Carleman: The Scalar Case

Philip J. Elias<sup>1</sup>, Qiyu Sun<sup>2</sup>, and Nader Motee<sup>3</sup>

**_Abstract_--The Hamilton-Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation of a nonlinear system is a nonlinear partial differential equation, and in that form it gives little away. This paper shows that it hides a Riccati equation. Carleman linearization reveals this structure. For scalar analytic input-affine systems, the lifted HJB equation is a single infinite-dimensional Riccati equation in a lower-triangular Toeplitz matrix. Its first entry is the Riccati equation of the standard linearization at equilibrium. Its remaining entries hold the nonlinear terms. Nothing about this is an approximation. Under controllability of the standard linearization and detectability of the cost, the equation has exactly one solution with positive diagonal entries, and it can be computed one entry at a time. This solution is the derivative of the value function. We prove that finite-sections of the solution are exact and converge exponentially to the derivative of the value function near the origin. The scalar case has a closed-form solution. This closed form is our benchmark: every result on the lifted equation is checked against it. Nothing in this structure depends on the state dimension. Our methodology points to a general theory of optimal control in which the HJB equation of analytic nonlinear systems becomes an infinite-dimensional Riccati equation.**

### I. INTRODUCTION

We revisit a 1961 work by Al’brekht [1] on the power series approach to optimal control. He considered an analytic control system with an analytic cost and showed how to compute the optimal feedback near an equilibrium one term at a time. The first step is the linear-quadratic problem, as the quadratic part of the value function solves a Riccati equation. Each further term is then obtained from a linear equation whose right-hand side depends only on the terms already known. In 1969, Lukes [2] proved that the series converges and that the feedback it defines is optimal and stabilizing near the equilibrium, as long as the linearized system is stabilizable and detectable. Over the years, this recursion has become the standard tool to compute optimal feedback for smooth nonlinear systems. It has also been turned into algorithms and software [3]. In this paper, we turn the question around. Rather than computing the terms of the series individually, we ask what equation they satisfy together. Carleman linearization gives the answer. The series comes from the Hamilton-Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation. Optimal control seeks a control law that minimizes a given cost functional [4]. Dynamic programming [5] reduces this problem to the HJB equation, a nonlinear partial differential equation whose solution is the value function [6]. For nonlinear systems, the equation rarely has a closed-form solution, and a smooth solution may not exist globally [7]. One therefore turns to approximations, such as Galerkin projection [8] and polynomial approximation [9]. The power series method of Al’brekht and Lukes is one of them. It computes the Maclaurin coefficients of the value function one degree at a time. It has been applied to bilinear systems [10] and to systems with additive control [11]. The method is local by nature. Adding terms improves the accuracy near the equilibrium, but it does not enlarge the region where the series is valid [3].

Carleman linearization lifts a nonlinear finite-dimensional system to an infinite-dimensional bilinear one [12]-[14]. It is the dynamical systems analogue of the Maclaurin expansion, as the state is replaced by the sequence of its monomials, and the nonlinear vector field by an infinite state matrix. The idea extends to Fourier bases [15], [16]. It also underlies quantum algorithms for nonlinear differential equations [17], [18]. Related lifts to higher-dimensional linear models have been used to compute invariant sets [19]. Carleman linearization has also been used to extend the Lyapunov equation to nonlinear systems [20] and to estimate domains of attraction [21]. In both cases, the lifted equation is linear in the unknown. The price of the lift is that the resulting operators are unbounded. In practice, they must be truncated [14], [22].

In earlier work, we lifted the HJB equation itself [23], [24]. This produced an infinite-dimensional equation, quadratic and symmetric in the unknown, that resembles an algebraic Riccati equation. We also gave an iterative algorithm to solve it. However, one term of that equation contained an operator that depends on the unknown only implicitly. This operator concealed the structure of the equation. Since then, truncations of the lifted equation have been learned from data [25], [26]. A related Riccati-like operator equation has also been derived in a Koopman framework [27]. None of these works gives the solution of the lifted equation in explicit form. Without it, one cannot check what the equation actually computes. This is where the scalar case helps.
