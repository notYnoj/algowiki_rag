## 2.1 Randomized Sorting

The main issue with sorting in the adversarial comparison setting is balancing worst-case approximation factor with worst-case number of comparisons. Standard sorting algorithms either always guarantee a good approximation, but can be forced to do many comparisons (i.e. quicksort), or always guarantee few comparisons, but can be forced to be give a bad approximation (i.e. mergesort). With deterministic algorithms, it was shown in \[AFHN15\] that it is not possible to get the best of both worlds, where they gave a tradeoff between approximation factor and comparisons. Our algorithm shows that in the randomized case, however, it is possible to be near-optimal in both aspects.

In the style of quicksort, our algorithm aims to partition the input set _X_ into two sets _Y_ and <o>_Y_</o> such that \(\overline{Y}\ge_4Y\) . If we can guarantee this in every recursive call, we must return a 4-approximate sorting, as no pair differing by more than 4 can ever be put in the wrong order \[AFJ<sup>+</sup>18\]. To ensure a good bound on the number of comparisons, we also require \(|Y|,|\overline{Y}|\ge n/8\).

The first phase of our algorithm attempts to partition using random pivots _O_(log_n_) times. Let _x<sub>L</sub>_ and _x<sub>R</sub>_ be the _n/_8-th smallest and _n/_8-th largest elements of _X_ respectively. For a fixed pivot _x<sub>p</sub>_, if _x<sub>p</sub>\>x<sub>L</sub>_+1, at least _n/_8 items must compare less than _x<sub>p</sub>_. Thus, if \(|X\cap[x_L,x_L+1]|\le n/4\), by a Chernoff bound, less than half of the pivots we try will have less than _n/_8 items compare less with high probability. Similarly, if \(|X\cap[x_R-1,x_R]|\le n/4\), less than half of the pivots we try will have less than _n/_8 items compare greater with high probability. If both of these inequalities are satisfied, it follows that we will find a "good" partition from one of our pivots with high probability. Otherwise, without loss of generality we assume more than half of the pivots had less than _n/_8 compare less. In this case, we have \(|X\cap[x_L,x_L+1]|>n/4\) with high probability. We will exploit this property in the other phases of the algorithm.

The second phase of our algorithm estimates the order of each element _x<sub>i</sub>_ by comparing it to a small subset of _X_. Then, we create a partition by taking _Y_ to be the elements with estimated order less than _n/_4, and <o>_Y_</o> the elements with estimated order at least _n/_4. For some fixed item _x<sub>i</sub>\<x<sub>L</sub>_-1, there must be 7_n/_8 items that compare greater than it, so by a Chernoff bound, _x<sub>i</sub>_ ends up in _Y_ with high probability. Similarly, if _x<sub>i</sub>\>x<sub>L</sub>_+2, there must be 3_n/_8 items that compare less than it (since \(|X\cap[x_L,x_L+1]|>n/4\)), so by a Chernoff bound it ends up in <o>_Y_</o> with high probability. Thus, by a union bound, \(\overline{Y}\ge_3Y\) with high probability. We note that if we did not have such high density in \[_x<sub>L</sub>,x<sub>L</sub>_+1\], the elements of order around _n/_4 (which could be arbitrarily far apart value-wise) would be impossible to differentiate with a small sample.

The final phase of our algorithm ensures \(|Y|,|\overline{Y}|\ge n/8\). Without loss of generality we assume \(|Y|\le|\overline{Y}|\). If \(|Y|\ge n/8\), nothing needs to be done as both sets are sufficiently large. Otherwise, we repeatedly sample _m=O_(log_n_) elements of <o>_Y_</o>, sort them, and move the _m/_8 smallest elements to _Y_ until \(|Y|\ge n/8\). Since |_Y|\<n/_8 and \(|X\cap[x_L,x_L+1]|>n/4\) before any iteration, there must be at least _n/_4 items \(\le x_L\) that are not in _Y_ . Thus, by a Chernoff bound, the _m/_8 smallest elements are all \(\le x_L\) with high probability. Since `Tournament` incurs error at most 2, it follows that \(|Y|\ge_4Y\) at the end with high probability. Note that by choosing a random permutation to guarantee disjoint subsets, we can do this sampling in parallel.
