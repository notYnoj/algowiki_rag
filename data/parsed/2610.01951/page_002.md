Top-two algorithms provide a simpler alternative. They maintain a current leader and compare it with one challenger at a time \[16, 17, 9\]. This structure is easy to implement and has strong asymptotic guarantees. In particular, Jourdan et al. \[9\] proved asymptotic \(\beta\)-optimality--optimal leading-order complexity when the best arm receives sampling fraction \(\beta\)--for several top-two rules on bounded rewards, including the Bernoulli \(\beta\)-EB-TCI rule studied here. Non-asymptotic results with the exact leading coefficient are more limited. The closest existing analysis is the Gaussian Top-Two UCB (TTUCB) result of Jourdan and Degenne \[8\], which treats a different top-two rule and a different reward model.

We analyze the exact Bernoulli \(\beta\)-EB-TCI rule, where EB-TCI stands for Empirical-Best Transportation-Cost Index. Under the unguarded rule, the empirical-best arm is sampled with probability \(\beta\) at each round. With probability \(1-\beta\), the algorithm selects the challenger minimizing \(j\mapsto W_t(B_{t+1},j)+logN_{t,j}\). Here _W<sub>t</sub>_ is a one-sided Bernoulli generalized likelihood-ratio (GLR) statistic: when _B_<sub>_t_+1</sub> is the empirical leader, _W<sub>t</sub>(B_<sub>_t_+1</sub>_,j_) measures the evidence that separates the leader from arm _j_. The additional term log_N<sub>t,j</sub>_ favors less-sampled challengers when the evidence values are comparable. It is part of the algorithm, not an auxiliary proof term.

A finite-confidence analysis of this rule must address three coupled questions. First, how closely do the empirical GLR indices follow their counterparts computed at the true means under adaptive sampling? Second, once the true best arm becomes the persistent leader, does the penalized challenger rule continue to sample every suboptimal arm often enough? Third, how long does it take until the true best arm remains the leader and the best-arm sampling fraction is close to \(\beta\)? The stopping-rule result of Jourdan et al. \[9\] controls the probability of a wrong recommendation on runs that stop, but does not itself prove that stopping occurs. Termination and sample complexity therefore require separate control of entry into this regime, challenger sampling, and the stopping threshold.

Our first result resolves challenger sampling and stopping once the algorithm has entered the stabilized regime. By this we mean that, from some time onward, the true best arm is always the empirical leader and its sampling fraction remains in a fixed neighborhood of \(\beta\). We show that every suboptimal-arm pull count then grows linearly and that the stopping time has leading term \(T_\beta^\star(\mu)log(1/\delta)\). The proof combines a deterministic population allocation benchmark for the penalized challenger with a time-uniform comparison between empirical and population pairwise Bernoulli GLR statistics.

Our second result removes the assumption that this regime has already been reached. For the unguarded algorithm, we obtain a non-asymptotic high-probability stopping bound for every Bernoulli instance with a unique best arm. To turn this bound into an expected stopping-time guarantee, one needs _sufficient exploration_: after an almost-surely finite random time with finite expectation, every arm must have at least order \(\sqrt{t/K}\) pulls. For pairwise-distinct means, Jourdan et al. \[9\] establish this property for the unguarded tie-breaking variant covered by their analysis. This result does not automatically apply to our fixed deterministic tie-breaking convention. We therefore also study a simple exploration guard that pulls a least-sampled arm whenever some count is below \(\sqrt{t/K}\). The guard enforces sufficient exploration directly while using only \(O(\sqrt{Kt})\) pulls by time _t_.

Our main contributions are:

1. **Analysis of the exact penalized challenger.** We analyze the Bernoulli GLR challenger with its logarithmic count penalty.
