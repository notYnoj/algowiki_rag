<tbc>in Direct Preference Optimization \[Rafailov et al., 2023, DPO\], the input is a pair of model outputs in which human users have indicated their preference over another and the model is fine-tuned to follow that preference. For a prompt _x_ and an answer pair _y_<sub>1</sub> and _y_<sub>2</sub>, denote by \(y_1\succ y_2|x\) that _y_<sub>1</sub> is preferred over _y_<sub>2</sub>. DPO learns a Bradley-Terry reward model \[Bradley and Terry, 1952, BT-model\] for this preference, i.e., \(p(y_1\succ y_2|x)\). The Plackett-Luce reward model \[Plackett, 1975, Luce, 1959, PL-model\] extends BT to a multinomial case that represents a ranking over choices. Our insight in VAs is that general BT (and PL) reward models baked into the LLM are sufficient to perform simple tasks. Assuming those capabilities and thus relying entirely on the accuracy of the reward models:

**Definition 1** ((_n,_2)-Verbalized Algorithms)**.** _Let_ \(T[\tau]\) _be a computational task (e.g., sorting) over objects of type_ \(\tau\) _and let_ \(A[\tau]\) _be an algorithm that solves_ \(T[\tau]\) _with an n-arg boolean oracle_ \(f[\tau]:\tau,\cdots,\tau\rightarrow\{\top,\perp\}\)_. A_ Verbalized Algorithm _instantiates_ \(A[\tau]\) _with_ \(\tau=\mathtt{str}\) _by implementing f_\[str\] _as a query to an LLM whose output is constrained to_ {_"yes","no"_} _which maps to_ \(\{\top,\perp\}\).

VAs keep the high-level control flow of \(A[\tau]\) intact, while only replacing the oracle \(f[\tau]\) with an LLM-based _f_\[str\]. This preserves the theoretical properties of the classical algorithm \(A[\tau]\) if the model’s reward model matches the ground truth. VAs can also leverage other existing algorithmic paradigms, such as concurrent algorithms. While the definition above focuses on the BT reward model, VAs naturally extend to PL reward models as (_n,k_)-VAs (defined in the appendix, used in submodular maximization section). If the reward model is inaccurate, a non-classical robust algorithm that assumes noisy oracles could bound the error rate of the output using known theoretical results. A deeper investigation into the theoretical error rates is left for future work.

**Symmetrization:** In certain tasks, we further improve the oracles in VAs via _symmetrization_, which mitigates two biases in the model: (1) Positional bias \[Liu et al., 2024\], which is the bias derived from the ordering in the input context (e.g., always assigning a higher logit to the first choice in MCQ tasks), and (2) sycophancy \[Casper et al.\], which is the bias toward certain answers, such as "yes".

For asymmetric oracles _f(x_<sub>1</sub>_,x_<sub>2</sub>) in (_n,_2)-VAs, such as (2,2)-comparison oracles for sorting, let _p_<sub>12</sub> and _p_<sub>21</sub> be next-token probabilities for answering "yes" with the original prompt and a prompt whose _x_<sub>1</sub> and _x_<sub>2</sub> are swapped. Due to asymmetricity, ideally \(f(x_1,x_2)=\neg f(x_2,x_1)\) holds, but in fact \(p_{12}\neq 1-p_{21}\) due to the bias. Symmetrized (_n,_2)-VAs use \((\frac{p_{12}+(1-p_{21})}{2},\frac{(1-p_{12})+p_{21}}{2})\) as the final probability for "yes", "no". Symmetrization for (_n,k_)-VA is discussed in the appendix.

## 3 Verbalized Maximum

We first establish our claim that _even the recent sophisticated models often fail at simple algorithmic tasks_. As an example task, we first investigate their ability to find the maximum in a list _X_ of _n_ randomly selected integers between 1 and 10000. Due to the lack of principled algorithm for the LLM to follow, a naive LLM often fails even at this simple task despite with the help of constrained decoding, especially when the list gets longer.

We compare two approaches: **Constraint decoding baseline** queries LLMs with a string "What is the largest number in \[comma-separated numbers in _X_\]?". We use constraint decoding \[Willard and Louf, 2023\] to limit the space of possible outputs to one of the elements in _X_. **Verbalized "Native" maximum** overrides Python’s built-in `-gt`-- operator with an LLM call and applies the built-in `max` function, which sequentially reduces the list with _O(n_) comparisons. Each binary comparison for \(x_1,x_2\in X\) is replaced by a yes-no style LLM query "Is X larger than Y? X: _x_<sub>1</sub> Y: _x_<sub>2</sub>", where the next output token is restricted to "yes" and "no" with constrained decoding. The model output is mapped to \(\top/\perp\) if the sampled output is "yes" / "no", respectively. Finally, **Verbalized parallel elimination tournament** performs \(\lceil log_2n\rceil=O(logn)\) steps of parallel pairwise comparisons, where each step eliminates half of the elements \[Karp, 1988\]. As each match in the tournament eliminates 1 element, it performs _n_-1 comparisons in total.

We evaluated the algorithms above across 20 randomly sampled lists for each of \(n\in\{100,1000\}\) with various-sized Qwen3 models (1.7B, 4B, 32B) with and without thinking \[Team, 2025\].
